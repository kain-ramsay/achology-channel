Can you learn psychology on your own, at home?

Can you learn psychology on your own, at home? Yes, you can, and you don't need to enrol anywhere to make a start. Studying at home on its own doesn't make you a registered psychologist.

Where can you learn psychology on your own for free?

You're welcome to begin with a free course, and two well-known ones are easy to find. OpenLearn is the Open University's free learning site, and its course called Starting with psychology is listed as introductory, at five hours of study. It offers a free statement of participation.

OpenLearn describes that course as a look at the different explanations psychologists have put forward for why people feel, think and behave as they do. You might like to read that description first. It will show you whether those are the questions you'd like answered.

Yale University's Introduction to Psychology is taught by Paul Bloom on Coursera, an online learning platform. Its page says no prior experience is required, and that it's designed for two weeks at 10 hours a week. It adds that learners can go at their own pace.

The page also says you can enrol for free and that the Certificate has to be purchased. You might like to read the enrolment page first to see what the free option includes. It's a short read.

How might you start learning psychology on your own?

You might begin with an introduction, to meet the ideas first-hand and see whether psychology holds your interest. A notebook beside you can catch the moment an idea reminds you of someone you know. You're welcome to start there.

When you study alone, you might want someone to talk things over with. Think of learning a language from an app: you'd collect plenty of words by yourself, but you'd probably want to say a few aloud. Explaining a new idea to a friend is one way you might try it.

Will learning psychology at home make you a registered psychologist?

Not on its own, and it's handy to know that before you begin. In the United Kingdom, the Health and Care Professions Council (HCPC) says there are nine protected titles for practitioner psychologists, and that anyone using one must be on its Register. They include registered psychologist and clinical psychologist.

The same HCPC page says the job title psychologist, by itself, isn't protected by law. The British Psychological Society (BPS) says the title psychologist isn't a designated title in the legislation, although registered psychologist is. Both pages are about the United Kingdom.

Achology's own answer on replacing a university degree describes a degree as the main door into regulated jobs such as clinical psychology, and into research. If you live somewhere other than the United Kingdom, your own country's regulator or professional body is the place to ask what its titles need.

Is it still worth learning psychology if you don't want the title?

You're welcome to think so, and a job doesn't have to be the reason. Perhaps you'd like to understand why a parent bristles when you offer advice, or why you go silent in a tense meeting. You might like to start by wondering about moments like those.

Achology holds that a practitioner goes first: they try what they learn on their own life before sharing it with anyone else. That's why you might see a free course, or a first Achology course, as a fair test of whether a longer road, such as a degree, is one you'd like to take.

Where could you go next with Achology?

Achology teaches applied psychology through self-paced online courses, and none has prerequisites. The Diploma Course in Modern Applied Psychology (DiMAP) has more than 51 hours across 175 lessons. It includes a lesson called The 7 Primary Schools of Psychological Thought, then a section on each of six schools.

The diploma is education, and it isn't a licence. Its certificates are never presented as a degree or a statutory qualification, and what an Achology certificate proves sets out what each one shows. You're welcome to read around the subject first in Applied Psychology Explained.

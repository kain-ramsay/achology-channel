Psychoanalysis is the study of what happens in your mind below the level you can see into directly. Sigmund Freud gave the idea its most famous shape with the iceberg: the small part of the mind you are consciously aware of sits above the waterline, and a much larger part, holding repressed desire, unresolved conflict and instinct you never examine directly, sits beneath it, quietly shaping what you think, feel and do. Freud split that hidden territory further into the id, the ego and the superego. The id is the part that wants what it wants, immediately, with no interest in consequence.

The superego is the part that holds your inherited sense of right and wrong, absorbed from parents and culture long before you could question it. The ego sits between the two, the part of you that actually deals with the real world, weighing what the id wants against what the superego permits and what reality will allow.

That single model explains more of your own behaviour than it might seem to at first. It explains why a reaction can arrive before you have consciously decided to have it, since the id and the superego are both already active long before the ego gets a chance to weigh in on the decision. It explains why a belief formed in childhood can still be running your adult decisions, unexamined, decades later, because nothing in the model requires a belief to be re-tested before it goes on shaping behaviour.

And it explains why the version of yourself you show other people is so often not quite the same as the version underneath, since the part of you managing that gap, the ego, is working constantly and mostly out of sight, mediating between an instinct that wants what it wants and a conscience that has its own, separately inherited, view of what is allowed.

This school of Achology's teaching draws on one course section, twenty lessons, and works through four connected ideas: the structure of the mind itself, where your beliefs and your sense of self first took shape, the gap between the face you show and the self underneath it, and how the past keeps reaching into your present relationships without your permission. What follows works through all four in order.

It is worth being plain about what psychoanalysis is not claiming, before working through what it is. It does not claim that every decision is secretly irrational, or that a person is powerless against their own unconscious. What it claims is narrower and more useful: that a meaningful share of what shapes your reaction, your relationship and your sense of who you are was decided somewhere you were not consciously paying attention, usually a long time ago, and that the decision still holds unless something brings it back into view. Bringing it back into view, repeatedly and without flinching from what is found there, is what every lesson in this section is doing, in one form or another.

## The Psychoanalytic School of Psychological Thought
*(Diploma Course in Modern Applied Psychology, section 3)*

### The mind beneath the surface

Perception is where this section starts, and for good reason: before anything else, it is perception, not the world itself, that decides how you react. What you take in is filtered by past experience, culture and personal belief before it ever becomes a reaction, which is why perception can distort reality and produce a defensive or avoidant response to something that never warranted one at all. Two people standing in the identical room, hearing the identical remark, can walk away having had two different experiences of it, and the difference between them is not the remark, it is the filter each of them ran it through on the way in.

Reflecting on your own perception, and questioning the assumption behind a reaction before you act on it, is the starting point this section sets for understanding anyone's inner world more accurately, your own included, and it is the reason this whole school of thought opens on perception rather than on any of the specific mechanisms that follow it.

Freud's iceberg model gives that inner world its structure, and the image is worth sitting with rather than skimming past, because the proportions are the whole point of it. The conscious mind holds what you are aware of right now, the visible tip above the water, small compared to everything beneath it. The preconscious holds what is not currently in focus but is easy enough to recall when you reach for it, the part of the iceberg sitting just under the surface.

The unconscious, the largest part of the iceberg by far, holds repressed desire, instinct and unresolved conflict that shapes behaviour and emotion without your awareness ever being consulted, and it is the sheer size of this part relative to the visible tip that the model is built to communicate: most of what is shaping you was never going to be found by looking only at what you can already see. Freud then named the three parts operating inside that structure. The id operates on the pleasure principle, seeking immediate gratification with no regard for consequence.

The ego operates on the reality principle, mediating between what the id wants and what the real world will genuinely allow. The superego holds the moral compass, built from parental guidance and social norm, and it is often the superego that produces guilt or pride depending on how closely your behaviour matches the standard it has internalised. Mapped onto the iceberg, the id sits deep in the unconscious, the superego spans both conscious and unconscious, and the ego does its mediating work in the conscious mind, in full view, even while the forces it is mediating between mostly are not.

This section draws one further connection worth holding onto: the same three-way tension Freud described maps onto the Wise Mind model taught elsewhere in the curriculum, where a reasonable, logical mind and an emotional, impulsive mind are balanced by a third, wiser state sitting between them. Two different vocabularies, describing a similar internal negotiation.

### Where your beliefs first took shape

If the id, ego and superego explain the structure doing the negotiating, this section's next movement explains where the material it is negotiating over first took shape. Beliefs are built from a blend of experience, deduction, inference and the acceptance of other people's opinions, arriving from at least four different directions rather than one, which is part of why a single belief can feel so solid: it was rarely built from only one kind of evidence.

And because a person is born with no preconceived notions at all, arriving with nothing already decided, childhood is when the deepest and least examined of those beliefs get laid down, at exactly the age when there is the least capacity yet to question what is being absorbed. The section illustrates the point with a story rather than a definition, the story of a young elephant tethered to a small post by a rope it could not break as a calf. Fully grown, strong enough to snap the rope without effort, the elephant stays tethered anyway, because the belief that it cannot break free was formed once, early, and never re-tested.

Human limiting beliefs work the same way: a childhood event gets misread, the misreading hardens into a belief that was never true to begin with, and the belief goes on shaping adult decisions long after the child who formed it has grown well past the situation that created it, and long after the rope, if it were ever tested again, would break.

This section traces that formation directly with a timeline exercise, following how a handful of pivotal early moments can leave a lasting imprint on self-image, and it names something worth taking seriously about the process: this kind of self-examination can be genuinely difficult, since it asks you to reconsider beliefs you have held, unquestioned, for a long time, and it is worth approaching with patience rather than force rather than treated as a problem to solve quickly.

A belief traced back to a single afternoon in a classroom, or a single offhand remark from a parent, can feel too small to have caused something as large as a lifelong pattern, and the section's own answer to that doubt is that size was never the point. A belief does not need a large cause to become permanent. It only needs to go unquestioned.

Alongside belief sits a closely related pair the section draws out on purpose: self-esteem, the value you place on yourself right now, and self-concept, the more forward-looking vision of who you might still become. The two are related but distinct, and many people get stuck in the gap between them, a kind of limbo where present self-esteem feels low while the self-concept still ahead feels doubtful, producing a cycle of self-doubt that recognising the gap is the first real step out of. The limbo is specific: it is not simply feeling bad about yourself, and it is not simply doubting your future.

It is the particular discomfort of standing between the two, unable to fully accept where you are and unable to fully believe in where you are trying to get to, and the section treats naming that specific discomfort, rather than trying to talk yourself out of it, as the more useful starting point.

### The face you show and the self underneath

Introspection, looking inward on purpose to observe your own thought, gets its own treatment here, paired with a specific exercise the section names directly, "How Present Is Your Past?", built to prompt reflection on how past experience is still shaping present thought, feeling and decision, whether or not you have noticed it doing so. The exercise is deliberately visual and reflective rather than a checklist, on the reasoning that a question this personal rarely answers itself in list form, and it treats the noticing itself, not any particular answer arrived at, as the useful outcome.

But introspection has a limit, which this section confronts directly through the work of Pierre Janet: "The unconscious sees the men behind the façade." Whatever face you present outward, the unconscious mind perceives past it, to what you genuinely think and feel and intend, and the section argues that aligning with that underlying truth is not optional if self-awareness and growth are going to be more than surface-deep. People build façades, often without meaning to, as a form of self-protection or as a way of keeping social harmony, and the cost of the façade is a disconnection from the self it is covering, producing exactly the kind of inner conflict introspection alone struggles to resolve.

The Johari Window, built by Joseph Luft and Harrington Ingham, gives that problem a structure, and it does so by treating self-knowledge as four separate areas rather than one single blur. The Open Area holds what you know about yourself and what others see too, the territory that open communication and trust can expand, and it is the only one of the four areas both parties can already see into. The Blind Spot holds what others see in you that you do not see in yourself, by definition invisible from where you are standing, and it can only be uncovered through direct feedback from people you trust enough to say it.

The Hidden Area holds what you know but choose not to share, kept there by choice rather than by blindness, reducible only by choosing to open up. The Unknown Area holds what neither you nor anyone else has yet seen, the part of the window with nothing yet written in it, shrinkable only through genuine self-exploration and new experience that has not happened yet. Expanding the Open Area, deliberately, at the expense of the other three, is what this section holds up as the practical route out of the façade Janet described, since a larger Open Area leaves correspondingly less room for a gap between the face you show and the self underneath it.

Growing it takes two different kinds of effort, not one: asking for honest feedback to shrink the Blind Spot, and choosing to disclose more to shrink the Hidden Area, and a person who only does the first of those two things is still leaving half of the available ground uncovered, however much progress the first half genuinely represents on its own.

Defence mechanisms are where that façade gets its machinery. Anna Freud catalogued them as unconscious strategies for coping with uncomfortable feeling, internal conflict or outside pressure, and this section names denial, refusing to acknowledge a painful truth; projection, shifting an uncomfortable feeling onto someone else by attributing it to them instead; repression, pushing a difficult memory or impulse out of conscious reach entirely rather than facing it; and rationalisation, building a seemingly logical excuse that lets you avoid the emotional accountability a situation genuinely calls for.

Each one works the same way underneath its own particular disguise: it lets you avoid a piece of reality that would otherwise cost something to face, at the price of the reality not going anywhere while you avoid it. A second lesson takes the same territory into a real relationship, describing how one partner's habit of avoiding conflict, and the other's habit of intellectualising rather than feeling a problem through, built emotional distance between them over years, until naming the pattern directly, out loud, to each other, became the route to something more constructive replacing it.

The lesson is candid that this cost real time to recognise, and that recognising a defence mechanism in your own behaviour is harder than spotting one in somebody else's.

### How the past reaches into your present relationships

Two lessons work through a live psychoanalytic conversation and then step back to examine it, and the point of showing both is deliberate: a psychoanalytic conversation can surface the roots of a present-day issue by tracing it back to past experience and behavioural pattern, but the act of reviewing that conversation afterwards, openly and without defensiveness, is presented as carrying almost as much insight as the original conversation did. The review names what happened in the exchange, where a pattern surfaced, where a question landed and where it did not, in a way the original conversation, being lived rather than watched, could not fully see about itself while it was still taking place.

Sharing that review, rather than keeping the observations private, is treated as part of its value too, since a pattern named only to yourself is easier to quietly set aside later, once the discomfort of noticing it has faded, than one already said out loud to another person who can hold you to having noticed it. Self-awareness, this section argues twice over, is built as much in the reflecting as in the happening, and a conversation that is never looked back on afterwards keeps most of what it could have taught.

Transference and countertransference name the specific mechanism by which the past keeps showing up uninvited in present relationships, and the two lessons on it are worth reading as a pair rather than separately, since neither one is complete without the other. Transference is what happens when feelings, expectations or attitudes formed in an earlier relationship, often with a parent or a sibling, get projected unconsciously onto someone in the present who did nothing to earn them, a manager, a partner, a friend, standing in a role their predecessor once occupied without ever agreeing to it.

Countertransference is the mirror of that: the emotional reaction the other person has in response, often rooted in their own unresolved experience rather than in anything genuinely happening between the two of them, which means a single misplaced reaction can trigger a second misplaced reaction in return, each one answering the other's past rather than the present moment either of them is genuinely standing in.

Both are common well outside a therapy room, in any relationship carrying enough emotional weight, a family bond, a friendship or a working relationship alike, and the section's point is a practical one: understanding both patterns is what makes a more conscious, more accurately targeted response possible, in place of a reaction aimed, without your knowledge, at somebody who is not really there.

### Weighing it all up

The section closes by widening the lens rather than narrowing it further. Ulric Neisser's five levels of self-awareness are introduced here as a layered account of what knowing yourself genuinely involves, and the layering matters: no single level is the real you sitting underneath the others, each one is simply a different angle on the same person. The Ecological Self is rooted in your direct physical presence in the world, the immediate sense of your own body in space. The Interpersonal Self is built through social exchange, the self that exists only in relation to other people.

The Extended Self links your past, present and future into one continuous story, the self that can say "I" about a memory from decades ago and a plan for next year in the same breath. The Private Self holds what stays internal and unobserved by anyone else, whatever you are thinking right now that nobody else has access to. The Conceptual Self, the most complex layer, is built from identity, role and the cultural narrative you have absorbed about who you are, often without ever choosing it directly.

Alongside this sits a practical framework for wise, ethically sound decision-making, built on weighing emotion, values and logic together rather than letting any one of the three decide alone, grounded in the idea that a virtuous life gives a person the basis for making difficult choices well even when a fast, purely emotional or purely logical answer would have been easier to reach for.

The section ends by weighing psychoanalysis itself, strengths and limits both, which is itself consistent with everything that came before it: a school of thought built around examining what usually goes unexamined does not exempt itself from the same treatment. Its strength is real: a genuine focus on the unconscious mind, and on how early experience shapes present thought and behaviour, gives it real purchase on deep-seated pattern and unresolved conflict that a purely surface-level approach would miss entirely.

Its limits are named just as plainly: the process can be time-intensive, and some of its concepts are abstract enough that they are hard to apply without guidance or without a second person to help surface what the Blind Spot in the Johari Window, by definition, cannot show you on your own. The section's own closing exercise asks the plainest version of its question directly: do the recurring themes in your relationships or your emotions truly trace back to something in your past, and what might follow from seeing that connection clearly, kept alongside rather than resolved instantly.

## What this adds up to

Twenty lessons, one connected argument: a great deal of what drives your behaviour is not decided at the level you can see into directly. Freud's iceberg names the structure, limiting beliefs and the timeline of self-image explain where the hidden material came from, defence mechanisms explain how that material gets kept out of view once it is uncomfortable, the façade and the Johari Window explain the gap between what you show and what is really there, and transference explains how the past keeps reasserting itself in relationships that have nothing to do with where the feeling originally came from. None of it argues that the unconscious controls you completely.

It argues something more workable: that the parts of your own mind you have never examined are still operating, whether you look at them or not, and that looking is the only thing this section's own material has shown to change how they operate. The value of a model like this one is not that it explains everything about you. It is that it gives you a specific place to look next time a reaction feels bigger than the moment that triggered it.

The plainest question worth carrying out of this section, once all twenty lessons have been read rather than skimmed, is not why you reacted the way you just did. It is the one the section itself keeps returning to, in a different costume each time it appears, whether the lesson in front of you is Freud's iceberg, the elephant's rope, or the gap between the face you show and the self underneath it: what belief, formed a long time ago and never re-tested, might still be tethering you to a post you could walk away from today. The section does not promise that finding the answer is comfortable.

Pierre Janet's own line stands as its fairest warning: the unconscious sees past the façade regardless of whether you go looking for what it sees, so the only real choice on offer is whether you look on your own terms or wait to be shown.

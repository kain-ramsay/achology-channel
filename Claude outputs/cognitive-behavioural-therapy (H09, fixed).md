## Body

## What Is Cognitive Behavioural Therapy?

Replay the last conversation that kept you up. You turned it over a dozen times, decided what it meant, decided what it said about you, decided how the next one would go. By the time you fell asleep, you'd concluded the other person was angry with you and that things would be awkward from now on, even though none of it had happened yet. Cognitive Behavioural Therapy has a name for exactly what your head just did to you.

That's not a malfunction. It's your brain doing exactly what Cognitive Behavioural Therapy, or CBT, says it does: turning one thought into a feeling, the feeling into a behaviour, the behaviour into proof you were right to have the thought in the first place. The system works. It's just working against you in that moment, and Cognitive Behavioural Therapy is what interrupts it.

Cognitive Behavioural Therapy rests on one idea small enough to fit in a sentence: your thoughts, feelings and actions are connected, so changing one changes the others. The [NHS describes CBT](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/talking-therapies-medicine-treatments/talking-therapies-and-counselling/cognitive-behavioural-therapy-cbt/overview/) as a type of talking therapy that helps you change how you think and act, and calls it a recommended treatment for many mental health problems, including anxiety, depression and sleep difficulties.

That's the clinical use of it. But you don't need a diagnosis for the idea to work on you: how you think about something shapes how you feel, and how you feel shapes what you do next, whether you're in a therapist's chair or lying awake at 2am. That's the version taught here, not a course of treatment but a way of understanding your own mind, and Achology teaches it across three full courses and more than sixty hours of training. It rests on ideas that go back further than any therapist's office, built by thinkers who were mapping the mind long before CBT had a name.

## How Your Thinking Shapes Your Life

Here's the part most explanations skip. It's not one bad thought that gets you. It's the loop.

Achology's own course material calls it the vicious cycle: a negative thought fuels a feeling, the feeling fuels a behaviour, and the behaviour confirms the very thought that started it, on repeat, until something interrupts it. A moment of self-doubt becomes a feeling of inadequacy. That feeling leads you to avoid a challenge, and avoiding it seems to prove you were right to doubt yourself in the first place. You can't argue your way out of a loop like that; you can only see it clearly enough to step out of it.

The clearest place to watch it turn is in how someone responds to a setback, and the psychologist Carol Dweck named two attitudes people fall into after years of studying exactly that. A fixed mindset treats your abilities as set in stone, so a setback confirms your limits. A growth mindset treats them as buildable through effort instead, so the same setback becomes information rather than a verdict, and as Dweck put it, challenges become exciting rather than threatening. Same event, two completely different Tuesdays, depending on which one you're running.

That single shift, from threat to curiosity, shows up again in where you choose to spend your attention. Stephen Covey's Circle of Control separates what you can change, your own actions and habits, from his Circle of Concern, the things you can only worry about, an idea the psychologist Julian Rotter had already named locus of control decades earlier. Ask yourself honestly: how much of today's worry lived in the circle you can't touch? A growth mindset and a well-placed Circle of Control tend to arrive together, because both are, in practice, a decision to spend your energy where it can do something.

None of this happens in isolation from how you see yourself more broadly. The psychologist Ulric Neisser mapped five deeper layers of self-knowledge, from your basic sense of your own body through to the story you've built about who you are over time. Working through all five gives a fuller picture of who you are than any single label can, and that fuller picture is what Cognitive Behavioural Therapy works with, not just the parts of you that show on the surface.

Two lessons ahead take the fixed and growth mindsets in full, alongside a practical exercise for sorting your own concerns into the circle you can control. Between the two, a shift in attention turns into something you can use before the week's out, whichever Tuesday you happen to be having when you get there.

## Where Your Beliefs Actually Come From

Quick question: how many of your beliefs did you choose, and how many did you just absorb without ever deciding to? Most people assume the answer is nearly all of them. It rarely is.

The philosopher John Locke would say fewer than you think. He argued the mind starts as a blank slate, and every idea arrives through one of two routes: sensation, what your senses take in, and reflection, what your mind does with that afterwards. Nothing arrives pre-loaded. It's all built, piece by piece, from experience, and that building process isn't always accurate.

One of the clearest ways to see the inaccuracy is a phrase from the philosopher Alfred Korzybski: the map is not the territory. Your mental map is shaped by your own history and assumptions, and it's always a simplification of what's there. Two people can live through the same event and walk away with two different accounts of what happened, and neither one is lying. They're just reading from a different map.

Expectation redraws that map quietly, without asking permission. The Pygmalion effect describes how believing more of someone, or of yourself, tends to produce more, through a feedback loop of tone, attention and effort that almost nobody notices while it's happening. Here's the part worth sitting with: the label you carry about yourself isn't a fixed description, it's closer to a prediction. And predictions can be revised.

Revising them starts with catching the unhelpful thinking patterns that run in the background: expecting the worst, or judging a whole day by its worst five minutes. Naming a pattern is most of the work of loosening its grip, which is why Albert Ellis, the founder of Rational Emotive Behaviour Therapy, argued for meeting these patterns with compassion rather than a clinical label. He believed treating a struggling thought as pathology does people more harm than good, and treating it as a habit anyone can question does far better. Notice the difference: one framing makes you a patient, the other makes you someone with a habit you can change.

A full look at the five categories of belief, and a set of honest questions for testing your own relationship with the truth, sit in the lessons ahead. Working through them slowly is what turns an assumed belief into a chosen one, rather than one you inherited and never questioned.

## The Model at the Heart of CBT

Everything above builds to one model, and it's small enough to say in three letters. Almost everyone who's ever sat with a CBT practitioner meets it in the first session. It's the one idea worth remembering if you remember nothing else here.

A stands for the Activating Event, the thing that happens. B stands for the Belief you form about it, usually instantly and without noticing. C stands for the Consequence, the feeling and behaviour that follow from the belief. Here's what makes the ABC model worth learning: two people can face the same activating event and land in entirely different places, and the event isn't what explains the gap, the belief in between is.

This is also where CBT quietly parts ways with an older assumption in psychology. Rather than treating a diagnosis as a life sentence, CBT rests on the assumption that people can learn, adapt and grow. A CBT practitioner works from the belief that no one needs to lean on a professional forever; the aim is to leave a person with tools they keep using long after sessions end.

Ellis, whose compassionate stance shaped the last section, also catalogued a list of what he called self-defeating beliefs. Among them: the idea that you must be approved of by everyone, that a setback proves you're incompetent, or that people who've hurt you deserve punishment. Read that list and you'll probably recognise one, and left unchecked, beliefs like these quietly steer a life. Once you can name one, you can ask whether it's true, or just familiar, which are not the same thing.

None of this works, though, if perception and reality get treated as one thing. What you perceive is filtered through your own experience and mood, rarely a perfect match for what's there: the same map-and-territory idea from the last section, showing up again at the level of a single moment instead of a whole belief.

The ABC model gets a full, worked walkthrough ahead, alongside Ellis's complete list of self-defeating beliefs. Together they turn the model from something you recognise into something you can apply. Next time it matters, rather than after, when the thought has already had its way with you and the moment has passed.

## The Psychology Behind the Method

Cognitive Behavioural Therapy didn't appear from nowhere, and two thinkers explain why it treats a thinking pattern as learned rather than fixed, which is the whole point. Both were writing about something else entirely, long before CBT existed as a named approach. Their ideas turned out to explain it anyway.

Jean Piaget mapped how children's thinking develops in stages, from the purely sensory reasoning of infancy through to the abstract thinking most adults use. Erik Erikson mapped a parallel set of stages across an entire life, each one built around a conflict, such as trust against mistrust, that a person works through before moving on. Put them side by side and they say the same thing twice: thinking matures rather than arriving finished. Which is exactly why a pattern that formed once can be unformed.

Much of what CBT tries to correct was named precisely by [Aaron Beck](/learn/psychology/articles/aaron-beck-the-pioneer-who-revolutionized-cognitive-psychology/). His work on cognitive distortions described the habitual thinking errors, assuming the worst, seeing everything in black and white, that quietly skew how a person reads their own life. David Burns later popularised Beck's list in his book Feeling Good, which is why you'll often see it described that way. The psychologist Daniel Kahneman added [a wider catalogue of cognitive biases](/learn/psychology/articles/20-common-cognitive-biases-that-influence-your-decisions/): anchoring, where the first piece of information you hear quietly shapes every judgement after it, and confirmation bias, where you notice the evidence that agrees with you and somehow miss the rest.

Not every unhelpful pattern lives inside one head. Some play out between two people, in real time. The Drama Triangle, named by the psychiatrist Stephen Karpman, describes three roles people fall into during conflict: the Victim, who waits to be rescued, the Persecutor, who blames, and the Rescuer, who fixes problems nobody asked them to fix. All three keep a conflict circling without ever resolving it, and if you're honest, you've played all three at some point, probably in the same argument.

Catching a pattern like that while it's happening, rather than an hour later in the shower, is really a question of emotional intelligence, which breaks into four working parts. Self-awareness means noticing your own emotions as they arise, and self-management means keeping them from running the show under pressure. Social awareness means reading what someone else is feeling, and relationship management means using all three to resolve something with another person. Each part builds on the one before it, which is why self-awareness sits at the base of the whole model and nothing above it works without it.

Kahneman's System 1 and System 2 thinking get a full walkthrough ahead, alongside all four domains of emotional intelligence. Seeing the two side by side makes it easier to spot which one is driving a decision while you're still inside it, rather than working it out afterwards when it's too late to matter.

## Putting CBT Into Practice

Understanding a model is one thing. Using it mid-argument, mid-spiral, mid-2am, is another thing entirely, and it's the thing most people want most. The tools below are what make that difference real.

Socratic Questioning is the main tool: Cognitive Behavioural Therapy's way of testing a thought instead of just reacting to it. Is there real evidence for this, is there evidence against it, and what would a trusted friend say if you told them? These aren't rhetorical: they're a genuine check on whether a thought deserves the weight you've just handed it. Ellis extended the ABC model into a full five-step version, ABCDE, adding D for Disputing the belief and E for the Effect that follows, which is Socratic Questioning built directly into the model rather than bolted on afterwards.

When a feeling arrives too fast for any of that, the STOPP Card offers a different kind of tool: a way of buying yourself a few seconds before you react. Stop, take a breath, observe what's happening, pull back to see the bigger picture, then practise what works. Arnold Lazarus offered a simpler version still, for the moments a person can't even name what they want: describe it in one word. One honest word can point toward a clearer goal than the distress ever managed on its own.

One more tool runs in a different direction entirely, sorting what's known rather than testing a thought. The Johari Window, developed by Joseph Luft and Harrington Ingham, splits what you know and don't know about yourself into four areas: open to both you and others, hidden, visible to others but not to you, and unknown to everyone. Feedback from someone you trust is the only thing that moves something from the blind area into the open one. You cannot do that part alone, and there's no getting around that.

None of these tools work well in someone else's hands unless the person using them has built a few things first. Self-awareness keeps a practitioner grounded rather than projecting their own biases onto a client's problem. Authenticity keeps a session honest rather than performed. A solution-focused mindset, one that expects a person can change, tends to help clients believe the same thing about themselves, whether the person being helped is a client or simply you on a hard week.

Socratic Questioning and the full ABCDE framework each get a proper walkthrough ahead, including live demonstrations of both on a real thought. Watching the method run once, start to finish, makes it far easier to run it yourself later, when it matters and there's no time to think it through from scratch.

## Building Self-Awareness From the Ground Up

Here's an uncomfortable one to end on: the more certain you are that you already understand yourself, the harder it becomes to notice what you've missed. It's the one blind spot confidence can't fix on its own. Only a wider view of yourself can do that.

Sigmund Freud didn't invent the idea of an unconscious mind, but he's the reason most people have heard of it. He compared conscious awareness to the visible tip of an iceberg, with a much larger unseen mass doing its work beneath the surface, which is exactly why introspection alone can't hand you self-awareness. This is why this part of the course leans on the Beginner's Mind: an open and curious stance toward your own thinking rather than a fixed and certain one. Approaching your own mind the way a beginner would, willing to be surprised, tends to reveal more than confidence ever does.

Fixed and growth mindsets return here too, underneath a plainer question than either of them: what's the actual gap between where you are now and where you'd like to be? Not the vague feeling of dissatisfaction. The actual gap, named honestly, in health, finances or relationships. That's what turns a bad mood into something you can work on.

A guided look at the levels of self-awareness, and a practical exercise for closing the gap between your current life and the one you're aiming for, wait in the lessons below. Both are designed to be worked through slowly, over more than one sitting, not skimmed once and filed away.

None of this is meant to land all at once. CBT is really a small number of moving parts: thought, feeling, behaviour, and the belief sitting quietly in between. Start with the one part that felt most familiar as you read this, whether that was a mindset you recognised or a pattern you've caught yourself in more than once. That's the thread worth pulling first.

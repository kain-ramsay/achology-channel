Cognitive Behavioural Therapy, CBT, rests on one idea small enough to hold in a sentence: your thoughts, your feelings and your actions are connected, so a change to any one of the three changes the other two. A thought produces a feeling. The feeling produces a behaviour. The behaviour produces evidence that seems to confirm the original thought was right, and Achology's own CBT teaching has a name for that exact pattern: the vicious cycle, a single moment of self-doubt spiralling into feelings of inadequacy, into avoidance or withdrawal, into fresh proof for the belief that started it.

CBT interrupts that cycle by naming its parts. The cognitive model traces the path from an activating event, something that happens to you, through the belief you form about it, to the feeling and behaviour that follow from that belief. Mapping your own experience onto that path is called formulation, and it sits at the practical centre of the whole approach: not a diagnosis, but a structured way of seeing how a pattern of thinking or acting came about, and where it can be changed.

CBT is structured, collaborative and goal-oriented, usually run over a defined period rather than indefinitely, and built to leave you with tools you keep using long after any sessions end. Its original purpose was broader than treating a named condition. It was designed to give people frameworks for understanding their own thought patterns and their own capacity to grow, which is why Achology teaches it as a life skill open to anyone, not only as a course of clinical treatment.

Achology teaches CBT across three full courses. The CBT Toolkit: Core Principles and Real-World Applications, the Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT) Practitioner Course, and Cognitive Behavioural Therapy for Mental Health and Wellness cover 111 lessons between them, organised here into the six sections the three courses teach CBT in. What follows works through all six in turn: how a single thought becomes a belief, why two people can read the same event two different ways, and the specific tools CBT gives you for catching a distorted thought before it runs your evening.

## The Foundational Ideas of Cognitive Behavioural Therapy
*(The CBT Toolkit: Core Principles and Real-World Applications, section 1)*

This is where Achology's CBT teaching opens, and it opens with the mechanism itself: the vicious cycle, where a negative thought fuels a destructive emotion or behaviour, and that behaviour then reinforces the original thought. Sitting alongside it is the Wise Mind model, which separates the Reasonable Mind, driven by logic and fact, from the Emotion Mind, driven by feeling and impulse, and argues that the calmest, clearest decisions come from a blend of the two rather than either alone.

The cognitive psychologist Ulric Neisser mapped self-knowledge itself into five levels: the Ecological Self, formed through direct sensation and physical presence in the world; the Interpersonal Self, formed through social contact and reading other people's cues; the Extended Self, which links your past and your future into one continuous personal story; the Private Self, holding thoughts and feelings nobody else sees; and the Conceptual Self, built from the roles, beliefs and values you hold. Reflecting on all five together builds a more balanced picture of who you are than any single one can on its own.

This section also introduces fixed and growth mindsets, the belief that ability is either a fixed trait or something built through effort, and sets out what keeps a person stuck in the fixed version and what a deliberate shift toward the growth version takes.

It closes on three practical tools: the CBT communication framework, which links an outside event to the thoughts, feelings and behaviours it triggers inside you; the progress principle, which treats the gap between your current state and your ideal one as something to close through small, sustainable steps rather than one large leap; and the distinction between a problem, which has a solution, and a tension, which has to be managed rather than solved.

The section ends by separating your Circle of Control, what you can act on directly, from your Circle of Concern, what you can only worry about, and argues that most unnecessary stress comes from spending time inside the second circle instead of the first.

## Cognitive Behaviour Therapy for Personal Effectiveness
*(The CBT Toolkit: Core Principles and Real-World Applications, section 2)*

The second section opens on the foundational assumptions CBT is built on: that thoughts, emotions and actions are interconnected, and that people are capable of learning and adapting rather than being fixed by circumstance. From there it turns to something sharper, the labels a person applies to themselves. "Labels are for tin cans, not for human beings" is the line this section builds around: a label such as "reserved" can describe a trait, but it can just as easily close off the opportunities a person never tries because the label told them not to.

Sigmund Freud's account of the mind appears here in full: the conscious, holding what you are aware of right now; the preconscious, holding what is easily recalled but not currently in focus; and the unconscious, holding repressed memory, instinct and desire, which Freud linked to the Id, the Ego and the Superego. Alongside it sits a practical exercise for exploring what the unconscious is holding, built on the idea that suppressing the past rarely works as well as learning from it.

The philosopher John Locke's argument that all knowledge starts in experience, the mind as a blank slate written on by sensation and reflection, sets up a five-part model of belief: existence beliefs, association beliefs, cause and effect beliefs, prediction beliefs, and meta beliefs, which are beliefs about your own beliefs. The Pygmalion effect follows, the finding that high expectation, held either by another person or by yourself, tends to produce improved performance through a quiet feedback loop of tone, attention and self-perception.

Alfred Korzybski's line, the map is not the territory, does similar work from a different angle: your understanding of a situation is a simplified, personal construction, not the situation itself, and two people can build two different maps from one identical event.

This section is also the one that names the traps: the mind as a battlefield when cognition and feeling pull against each other; emotional time-travel, where dwelling on the past or rehearsing the future distorts how the present feels; and two full lessons on the unhelpful thinking patterns that run automatically and go unnoticed until they are named. It closes on three tests worth applying to yourself: a Socratic test of whether a given thought is accurate, three paths toward emotional stability built on feelings, faith and facts, and a series of truth statements built to test how willing a person is to face their own life without flinching.

Albert Ellis's own objection to the standard diagnostic model closes the section: that categorising a struggle by its symptoms, rather than understanding the person having it, tends to leave people feeling labelled rather than helped.

## The Central Ideas of Cognitive Behaviour Therapy
*(Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT) Practitioner Course, section 1)*

The Practitioner Course opens by defining the role directly: a CBT practitioner guides someone through identifying and altering the thought patterns behind their stress, anxiety or low mood, working collaboratively rather than prescriptively, and using tools such as thought diaries and behavioural experiments to keep the process grounded in real life rather than theory. The Wise Mind model returns here with its full lineage attached: it comes from Dialectical Behaviour Therapy, and blends the Rational Mind's logic with the Emotional Mind's feeling into something more balanced than either alone.

Albert Ellis, the founder of Rational Emotive Behaviour Therapy, contributes two of this section's sharpest lists. The first names the habits that block real listening: self-preoccupation, jumping to assumptions, judgement, defensiveness, distraction, and rehearsing your own response instead of hearing the other person's. The second, split across two lessons, sets out fourteen self-sabotaging beliefs, among them the belief that you must be loved and approved of by everyone, the belief that a setback proves you are incompetent, and the belief that a perfect solution exists for every problem. Left unchallenged, each one quietly shapes behaviour without ever being examined.

This section also carries CBT's original purpose forward into a clear statement: the discipline was built to equip people with frameworks for growth, not only to relieve symptoms, resting on the assumption that every person carries an innate capacity to learn and adapt. Seven principles follow, naming CBT as interconnected, goal-oriented, collaborative, time-limited, evidence-based, skill-building and present-focused, alongside a glossary of the terms the rest of the curriculum leans on: cognitive, behaviour, belief, consequence, distress, exposure.

Its central tool is the ABC model. A stands for the Activating Event, the situation or circumstance that triggers a reaction. B stands for the Belief formed about it, often automatically and without noticing. C stands for the Consequence, the emotional and behavioural outcome that follows from that belief.

The model matters because two people can meet an identical activating event and land in two different places by evening, and the event itself never explains the gap. The belief sitting quietly in the middle does. The section closes on perception and reality, working through why two witnesses to one event can walk away with two different, sincerely held accounts, and returns briefly to Korzybski's map and territory to make the same point from another direction.

## The Psychology Behind Cognitive Behaviour Therapy
*(Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT) Practitioner Course, section 2)*

This is the hub's longest section, and it is where CBT's own thinking meets the wider field of developmental and cognitive psychology. Jean Piaget's four stages of cognitive development, sensorimotor, preoperational, concrete operational and formal operational, trace how a child's capacity for abstract thought builds across childhood. Erik Erikson's eight stages carry the same idea across a full lifetime, from trust versus mistrust in infancy through to integrity versus despair in old age, each stage centred on a specific conflict that has to be resolved before the next one can be met well.

Noam Chomsky's theory of universal grammar and the language acquisition device sits alongside them, the argument that the human brain arrives with a built-in structure for language rather than learning it purely from external example.

Daniel Kahneman's distinction between System 1, fast and automatic, and System 2, slow and deliberate, explains why so many cognitive distortions take root in the first place: most run on System 1. Anchoring bias is the tendency to rely too heavily on the first piece of information you receive when making a decision, whatever it happens to be. Confirmation bias is the tendency to favour information that already matches what you believe, and to overlook what does not.

Loss aversion is the tendency to fear a loss more than you value an equivalent gain, which is why the same amount of money lost feels heavier than the same amount gained feels good, and why it quietly shapes decisions well beyond finance. Aaron Beck's cognitive distortions follow directly from this, catastrophising, assuming the worst possible outcome, and black-and-white thinking, judging a whole situation as entirely good or entirely bad, both habitual filters that skew how events get read.

The Superman Complex, overestimating your own capacity to handle everything, and the Inferiority Effect, a persistent sense of falling short, are treated as a matched pair here, two extremes either of which can quietly damage a close relationship if it goes unnamed.

Emotional Intelligence gets its own full model: self-awareness, self-management, social awareness and relationship management, each one building on the one before it. Stephen Karpman's Drama Triangle names the roles a conflict tends to fall into, the Victim who waits to be rescued, the Persecutor who assigns blame, and the Rescuer who steps in uninvited and, in doing so, keeps the Victim from ever solving the problem themselves.

The section closes on a run of practical, less-theoretical material: a five-dimension test for whether a personal goal aligns with who you are (physical, emotional, rational, volitional and spiritual), the inside-out principle, which argues your inner state drives your experience of an event more than the event itself does, cognitive flexibility as a trainable skill rather than a fixed trait, the four recognised learning styles, visual, auditory, kinaesthetic and reading and writing, and a firm point about the standards worth setting before investing time in helping someone else who is not genuinely open to that help.

## Cognitive Behavioural Models for Understanding People
*(Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT) Practitioner Course, section 3)*

The Johari Window, built by Joseph Luft and Harrington Ingham in 1955, opens this section and gives CBT one of its most durable tools for self-awareness. It splits what is known and unknown about a person into four areas: the Open Area, known to both the person and others; the Hidden Area, known to the person alone; the Blind Area, visible to others but not to the person themselves; and the Unknown Area, untapped potential neither side has yet seen. The Blind Area matters most here, because only someone else's honest feedback can move anything out of it. Introspection alone cannot.

This section builds several of CBT's practical models around that same insight. The Current State versus Ideal State model turns a vague ambition into an actionable plan by naming exactly where you stand now against exactly where you want to be, then building a roadmap between the two. Facts, Feelings and Faith names three foundations, objective truth, emotional depth and a sense of purpose that looks beyond the immediate difficulty, that together build a more balanced approach to life's changes than any one of the three alone. The Circle of Control and Circle of Concern distinction returns here too, applied specifically to where a person's energy should go when a challenge feels bigger than they are.

Its real centrepiece is Albert Ellis's ABCDE framework, which extends the ABC model with two further steps. D stands for Disputing, actively challenging an irrational or unhelpful belief by questioning the evidence behind it, closer to cross-examining a witness than to positive thinking. E stands for Effect, the improved emotional and behavioural outcome that follows from disputing a belief well. Socratic Questioning sits beside it as CBT's main tool for doing that disputing in practice: is there real evidence for this thought, is there evidence against it, and what would someone who knows you well actually say if you said this thought out loud to them.

Cognitive restructuring, described here as putting a destructive thought on trial, applies the same standard of evidence to a belief a person has been carrying unexamined.

Two further tools round the section out for moments when a full Socratic process is not practical in the moment. The STOPP Card strategy is introduced as a technique for strengthening emotional self-regulation when feelings are running high. Arnold Lazarus, a pioneer of multimodal therapy, offered something smaller still: asking a client in a heightened state to name what they want in a single word, a shift that moves focus away from the distress and toward a clear, positive goal.

The section closes on the natural progression emotion tends to follow, from frustration and anger through fear and angst to guilt and shame, distinguishing states that arise naturally from life's challenges from those that tip into something more overwhelming, before naming the six attributes of an effective CBT practitioner and the seven kinds of decision that build maturity and character over time.

## The Foundational Ideas of Cognitive Behavioural Therapy
*(Cognitive Behavioural Therapy for Mental Health and Wellness, section 1)*

The third course opens on the same territory as the first, foundational ideas, but reaches most of its ground from a different angle. It opens on a claim about the brain itself: a single shift in perspective can retrain how the brain works, and building greater self-awareness of conscious thought is what disrupts a negative thinking pattern once it has taken hold. That claim is followed immediately by a caution against relying on introspection alone.

Feeling persistently anxious, easily angered or out of sync with yourself can signal that greater self-awareness is needed, but looking inward on its own is not a reliable route to finding it, since a person's own blind spots are, by definition, invisible to that same person. Aaron Beck is quoted directly here: "The most significant event in the life of a patient is the patient's change in perspective," a line this section treats as close to CBT's actual definition.

The Wise Mind model reappears too, this time again attributed explicitly to Dialectical Behaviour Therapy, alongside the Cognitive and Communications Framework, formulation once more, mapping an external event to the internal thought, feeling and behaviour it produces.

This section gives the Johari Window a second, deeper pass across two lessons, adding a concept the earlier appearance did not: the Beginner's Mind, an open, curious stance toward your own thinking that is offered here as the direct alternative to the false certainty that keeps a person stuck in old patterns. Freud's account of the unconscious returns too, this time with its own historical framing: writing in 1915, Freud did not originate the idea of a conscious and unconscious mind, but did more than anyone to bring it into wide recognition, comparing conscious awareness to the visible tip of an iceberg sitting above a much larger unseen mass.

Fixed and growth mindsets get their fullest treatment of the whole hub here, across four lessons, and this is the one place in the curriculum where Carol Dweck is named directly as the researcher behind the model, quoted in her own words: "In a growth mindset, challenges are exciting rather than threatening. So rather than thinking, 'Oh, I'm going to reveal my weaknesses,' they think, 'Wow, I'm going to grow.'" The truth statements from earlier in the hub reappear too, now aimed specifically at the discomfort of facing financial instability, strained relationships or health difficulties head-on rather than deflecting them.

The section closes with a full breakdown of the cognitive thinking process itself, perception, attention, memory, reasoning and decision, tracing exactly how the mind moves from noticing something to acting on it, before returning one last time to the gap between current and ideal life conditions and to the Circle of Concern and Circle of Control, this time credited explicitly to Stephen Covey's The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People.

## What this adds up to

Six sections, three courses, one underlying claim running through all of them: your thoughts are not simply a private commentary on your life, they are the mechanism that builds your feelings and your behaviour, moment by moment, whether you notice it happening or not. The ABC model, the Johari Window, the Circle of Control, and Aaron Beck's cognitive distortions each describe one part of that same mechanism from a different angle, which is why they keep reappearing across three different courses rather than sitting in only one. Achology teaches CBT this many times, this many ways, because the mechanism it describes runs constantly, and recognising it once rarely makes it stop running on its own.

The next time a single moment tips you into a spiral, the useful question is not why this keeps happening. It is smaller and easier to answer directly: what is the actual evidence for the specific thought you just had, and would that thought survive being said out loud to someone who knows you well. Most versions of the spiral do not.

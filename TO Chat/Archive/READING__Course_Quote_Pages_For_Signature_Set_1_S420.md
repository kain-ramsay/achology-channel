## Page 1. Why Security Comes at the Cost of Freedom

Some part of you has been choosing the safe option for a long while, and lately it feels less like caution and more like being stuck. Other days you ask why a safe choice should leave you feeling smaller. The reply from Kain Ramsay is short: "If we choose security, it's going to come at the cost of freedom."

### An Idea That’s Worthy of Your Consideration

This quote by Kain Ramsay was taken from his course, the Diploma Course in Modern Applied Psychology (DiMAP), and its first part helps clarify the second. Just before it, the lecture returns to what it calls the opportunity cost idea, which means that choosing one thing gives up another. It says that if we choose red, it's going to come at the cost of green, and that if we choose green, it's going to come at the cost of red. The line you've just read applies the same pattern to security and freedom.

Listening to every recording in [the DiMAP course](/academy/neuro-linguistic-programming/diploma-modern-applied-psychology/) takes 51 hours or more, and this lecture is one of them. Straight after the line, the lecture turns it round: if we choose freedom, it's got to come at the cost of security. The line and its mirror image describe what a choice gives up, and neither says which one is right for you.

### What the Quote Might Be Suggesting to Us

Taken on its own, the line is about cost, not about which choice is better. It says that choosing security comes with giving up some freedom, and the word "choose" puts the decision with you. The lecture goes on to ask whether certainty really gives us security, and what in life we can be certain of. The line itself makes no claim about that, only about the price.

Amara has been offered a place on a new project with no promise it will last, and she's staying in her current role because the pay lands on the same date every month. One possibility is that she's choosing security and giving up the chance to find out what the new project would ask of her. Another is that she's weighed the price and decided it's worth paying, which the line doesn't rule out. The lecture itself asks whether you're staying in a job or role just because it gives you a little bit of security.

In 2012, [Schwartz and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0029393) proposed a refined theory in which values form a circular motivational continuum, a ring on which 19 values are ordered by compatible and conflicting motivations, including self-protection versus growth. In 15 samples from 10 countries (N = 6,059), their analyses largely supported the predicted order. The lecture refers to "Shalom's interpretation" of core values just before the line, which this page reads as Shalom Schwartz's. The abstract doesn't name security or freedom, so the paper doesn't test this line.

### What Can We Learn From the Idea That ‘Choosing Security Costs Freedom’?

In Achology's view, nobody is standing still: everyone is heading somewhere, and the things you agree to and the things you postpone are taking you there, whether you chose it or not. Achology also teaches that it's easier to follow the loudest voice in the room than to work out for yourself where your life should go. Read beside Kain's line, choosing security is one way of heading somewhere, and the line points to the price that comes with it.

Elsewhere in the lecture, Kain leans towards freedom, saying that if we want the best, we've got to pursue freedom instead of security, and that we can be free recklessly or free wisely, with intentionality and purpose. Achology's teaching on direction adds that a direction can be changed today. This page doesn't tell you which to choose; it points to seeing the price before you pay it.

### A Question That Deserves an Honest Answer

A safe choice can leave its cost unsaid, and a free one can leave unsaid what it protects. Either can sit in a life for years without being looked at, because nothing forces the question. Seeing the price doesn't mean changing the choice; it means knowing which choice you're making. Where in your life have you chosen safety without asking what it cost you in freedom?

## Page 2. Why Connection Determines Your Peace

Plenty of conversations can fill a week and still leave you feeling oddly alone, as though nobody has met the real you. You may be asking whether the fault lies in your friendships or in how much of yourself you bring to them. Here is why connection determines your peace, in the words of Kain Ramsay: "The depth of connection that we experience in our relationships is ultimately going to determine the amount of peace that we experience in our lives."

### An Idea That’s Worthy of Your Consideration

This quote by Kain Ramsay was taken from his course, the Diploma Course in Modern Applied Psychology (DiMAP), and its first part helps clarify the second. In the lines just before it, the lecture says the quality of our relationships depends on how authentic and congruent we're willing to be, and that our willingness to be congruent sets the level of connection we experience. Congruence, in everyday terms, means that what you show matches what you feel inside, which is this page's gloss of the lecture's word. The line you've just read is the last link in that chain.

Among the recordings in [the DiMAP course](/academy/neuro-linguistic-programming/diploma-modern-applied-psychology/), a set running to 51 hours or more, this one also says you know someone loves you when they're confident enough to be themselves around you. It calls just being yourself the greatest risk we can take in life. On this page's reading, that risk is the ground the line stands on: depth needs someone willing to be real.

### What the Quote Might Be Suggesting to Us

Read by itself, the line is about depth, not numbers, and it says nothing about how many relationships you have. Its wording is strong, since "ultimately going to determine" is firmer than "can help", so this page treats it as Kain's teaching rather than a measured result. The lecture's route to depth is giving other people the opportunity to know the real, unabridged version of us. It puts the first move with us: we must first be willing to take the risk of showing who we are.

Ines has a full calendar and still ends most weeks feeling that nobody around her knows how she's really doing. One possibility is that her conversations stay on the surface, and the lecture's chain would point to how much of herself she lets into them. Another is that something other than depth is behind the feeling, which the line doesn't address. The lecture says that if we don't give people the chance to know the real us, the person they stay connected with is someone else.

In a series of experiments, [Kardas, Kumar and Epley](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspa0000281) found that conversations between strangers felt less awkward, and created more connectedness and happiness, than participants expected. Participants also felt more connected to deep conversation partners than to shallow ones after having both kinds. The studies are about conversations, mostly between strangers, and the abstract doesn't measure peace. They support depth building connection, which is one step short of connection determining peace.

### What Can We Learn From the Idea That ‘Connection Determines Our Peace’?

In Achology's view, your closest relationships steer much of the rest of your life, and trust grows out of truth and nowhere else. Achology teaches that if two people only ever say what the other wants to hear, neither of them knows where they stand. On this page's reading, depth is something two people build by telling each other the truth, which is slower than keeping things smooth.

Achology also teaches that the wider the gap between your real self and the self you pretend to be, the more tired, anxious and lonely life feels. That is a neighbour of Kain's line rather than the same idea, since it's about pretending and the line is about depth. Achology's teaching on relationships adds that truth has to be earned, so on this page's reading being real with someone is about showing them yourself, not about telling them what is wrong with their life.

### A Question That Deserves an Honest Answer

Keeping things smooth is easy to mistake for keeping the peace. It can work for a long time, and it can also leave you among people who know only a tidier version of you. Depth is built one conversation at a time, and each one starts with a small choice about how much of yourself to bring. Which of your relationships would feel different if the real you were in it more often?

## Page 3. Why Clear Requests Protect Boundaries

You've been going along with things you'd rather have refused, and the resentment is beginning to colour how you feel about the people asking. Part of you fears that setting a limit would make you rude, or at least hard to like. Kain Ramsay's account of why clear requests protect boundaries is this: "We can make honest and humble requests, and this allows other people to at least get clear where they stand with us."

### An Idea That’s Worthy of Your Consideration

This quote by Kain Ramsay was taken from his course, the Diploma Course in Modern Applied Psychology (DiMAP), and its first part helps clarify the second. It comes from a set of self-reflection questions, one of which asks in which areas of your life you haven't yet taken responsibility for being honest about what you want. Kain likens this to a boundaries issue, saying that when people walk over us, it isn't that they're stronger than we are but that we lack personal boundaries. The line is his next step: when we make our wants known, other people can at least see where they stand.

This lecture sits among the recordings of [the DiMAP course](/academy/neuro-linguistic-programming/diploma-modern-applied-psychology/), which take 51 hours or more to hear in full. Right after the line, he goes on to say that if others want to dishonour us they can, and if they want to stay connected they can adapt and change. He then says that in the same way, so can we.

### What the Quote Might Be Suggesting to Us

The line makes two modest claims. The first is that a request can be honest and humble, which is a different thing from being blunt or demanding. The second is that the gain is clarity: other people "at least" get clear where they stand with us, and the line promises nothing about whether they will agree with us. Whether a clear request protects a boundary goes one step beyond the line, which says only that people can then see where they stand.

Tomasz is often asked to cover for a colleague at short notice, and he keeps saying yes while resenting it. One possibility is that his colleague has no idea there is a limit, because Tomasz has never shown one. If he made one honest, humble request, such as asking for a day's notice, the colleague could at least know where he stands. What the colleague does with that is up to the colleague.

[Flynn and Bohns](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.95.1.128) ran six studies of direct requests for help, meaning asking outright. In the first three, people underestimated by as much as 50% the likelihood that others would agree, and the last found that help seekers were less willing than potential helpers to appreciate the social costs of refusing. These studies are about asking for help, not about setting boundaries. They don't test this line.

### What Can We Learn From the Idea That ‘Honest and Humble Requests’?

In Achology's view, what you tolerate tends to carry on, so it's worth saying clearly where your limits are and giving the other person a fair chance to adjust. Achology also teaches that asking and encouraging are open to you, while making another person change is not. On this page's reading, that fits the line's careful wording: other people "at least" get clear, and what they do next is theirs.

Achology's teaching on communication adds that understanding someone is better than telling them they're wrong, since "You're wrong" ends a conversation and "Help me see what you're seeing" opens one. On this page's reading, a humble request leaves room for the other person's view, which a demand doesn't. Nothing in the line or in these teachings says a request will always be met.

### A Question That Deserves an Honest Answer

Waiting for someone to guess a limit can feel polite, and it can also leave the limit unspoken for a long time. A request is a smaller step than a confrontation and a larger one than hoping. Whatever the other person does with it, you'll know it was said. Which limit in your life have you been hoping someone else would simply guess for you?

## Page 4. Why You Can Manage Prejudice

Sometimes a judgement about someone arrives before you've decided anything, and afterwards you feel uneasy that it came from you at all. You'd like to be fair, and you're not sure that wanting to be is enough. Kain Ramsay's line on why you can manage prejudice comes down to this: "But we can learn to manage our prejudices and discipline ourselves not to discriminate."

### An Idea That’s Worthy of Your Consideration

This quote by Kain Ramsay was taken from his course, the Diploma Course in Modern Applied Psychology (DiMAP), and its first part helps clarify the second. It comes from the lecture that opens the course's final section. There he defines a stereotype as an exaggerated belief or distorted truth about a person or group, and a prejudice as a prejudgment or an attitude about a group or its members. Discrimination, he says, is behaviour that treats people unequally because of their group memberships.

Just before the line, he says we'll never be able to get over stereotyping people, because stereotypes are just words and labels that we all use naturally. The line begins with "But", so it is his answer to that: the work, as he frames it, is to overcome our own prejudices and our natural discriminations. Played back to back, the recordings of [the DiMAP course](/academy/neuro-linguistic-programming/diploma-modern-applied-psychology/) would fill 51 hours or more, and this is one of them.

### What the Quote Might Be Suggesting to Us

Read by itself, the line makes a modest promise. This page reads "learn" as practice rather than a switch, and "manage" as keeping something in check rather than removing it, so the line doesn't say prejudice disappears. The second half is about what we do: to discipline ourselves not to discriminate is to hold back the act, whatever opinion sits behind it. The lecture says outright that we can't stop stereotyping, and the line leaves that part alone.

Hana notices herself assuming that a new neighbour with a loud manner will be difficult. The assumption arrived on its own, before she had decided anything. What she does next, whether she avoids him or says hello as she would to anyone, is where the line puts the choice. One possibility is that a second meeting loosens the assumption; another is that it doesn't, and she still decides how to treat him.

[Patricia Devine's three studies](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.56.1.5), published in 1989, tested a model. In it, a stereotype switches on automatically when a member of the stereotyped group is present, and a low-prejudice response needs controlled inhibition of it. In the third study, only low-prejudice participants held back the automatically activated thoughts that fit the stereotype and replaced them with thoughts reflecting equality. The abstract says nothing about learning or discipline over time, so the study stops short of the line itself.

### What Can We Learn From the Idea That ‘Learning to Manage Our Prejudices’?

In Achology's view, believing something doesn't make it a fact, and how things look to you isn't the same as how they are. The grown-up reply to what you see, your own view included, is to ask what you might be missing. On this page's reading, a snap judgement is a view from one place, and that question is the pause the line points to.

Achology teaches that you can only change what you've noticed, and that its highest level is the habit of catching yourself judging or assuming, or taking your own view for the only one. It also sets a limit: going deeper than noticing, finding the source and doing what's needed turns into rumination, so the aim isn't to examine every judgement for ever. Notice first, then manage: on this page's reading, the line starts after the noticing, with what you do next.

A later review adds a caution. [Forscher and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspa0000160) combined 492 studies of ways to change implicit measures, which the authors define as response biases on implicit tasks. Their findings suggest such changes are possible but don't necessarily carry over into explicit measures or behaviour. The abstract is about changing implicit measures, not about managing what we do with them, so it shows only that shifting an implicit measure doesn't necessarily change what a person does.

### A Question That Deserves an Honest Answer

A first reaction can feel like a verdict, and it's easy to give it more weight than it has earned. The line doesn't ask you to stop having reactions; it points at the step after, where there's still a choice. That step is small, and it is yours to take. Which snap judgement of yours have you never once checked against the real person?

## Page 5. Why Understanding Beats Labeling People

Within moments of meeting someone, a verdict about them forms, and you're not sure whether to trust it or feel uneasy that it came so fast. It saves effort, and it can also leave you feeling distant from people you might have liked. Why understanding beats labeling people comes down to this line from Kain Ramsay: "Imagine if we just prioritized understanding people instead of assigning labels onto them."

### An Idea That’s Worthy of Your Consideration

This quote by Kain Ramsay was taken from his course, the Diploma Course in Modern Applied Psychology (DiMAP), and its first part helps clarify the second. Just before it, the lecture imagines talking openly about prejudices instead of defending ourselves against them, with a question about why someone feels so prejudiced towards a group and an offer to understand their backstory. After the line, he says he reckons this is a problem we face as part of modern society.

The line comes from a lecture that lists the main questions social psychologists ask, the first being how we relate to other people, including those we don't relate to well. [The DiMAP course](/academy/neuro-linguistic-programming/diploma-modern-applied-psychology/) as a whole needs 51 hours or more of listening, and this lecture is part of that. Notice that the line is phrased as "Imagine if", an invitation rather than a claim that understanding always wins.

### What the Quote Might Be Suggesting to Us

The working word in the line is "prioritized": it doesn't say never to form a first impression, it asks what comes first. To understand a person here means asking about their story, as the lecture's example does. Assigning a label files them under a heading you chose. The lecture also says we compare ourselves against people's differences rather than looking for samenesses and similarities, and that we all misinterpret things sometimes.

Priya meets a new colleague whose abrupt manner she files, within a minute, as unfriendly. Asking how his first week has gone might loosen the label, or it might leave it as it was, and either way she knows more than before. Her question is aimed at him, not at the category. The lecture's own picture is a simple question, asking someone why they feel as they do.

[Rubinstein and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1155/2022/5118325) proposed a theory of how people use stereotypes, and specific facts about one person (individuating information), in implicit (that is, indirectly measured) judgments. In their first two studies, judgments that fit a stereotype, made without such facts, were reversed when the individuals were portrayed as going against it. In studies 3 and 4, even with up to eight examples of individuals who went against a stereotype, there was no evidence of significant shifts in group stereotypes. The authors call their tests preliminary, and the work is about implicit judgments, so it doesn't speak to the line's wording.

### What Can We Learn From the Idea That ‘Understanding Instead of Labels’?

In Achology's view, you speak to the person, not to their job, their money or what you've decided they are, because everyone you meet is a human being at their own stage of growing up. That is close to the line's choice between a label and a person. On this page's reading, a label is a decision you've made about someone before you've asked.

Achology teaches that more information changes what a person sees, so any view, a first impression included, is better met with a question than treated as settled. The research above shows a limit. Learning about one person moved the judgement of that person but not the view of their group, so the question may need asking person by person. That last step is this page's inference, not a finding, and it doesn't mean a first impression is worthless.

A 2025 review by [Rubinstein and Jussim](https://doi.org/10.3390/soc15050136) surveys the research on how a person's group and facts about that one person shape implicit (indirectly measured) judgments of them. Among its principles, it reports that such facts influence explicit judgments, which are directly measured, more than implicit ones. So the lesson here is about implicit judgments of one person, and the review is a reason to hold it loosely.

### A Question That Deserves an Honest Answer

A first impression can feel like a fact before you've asked anything. The line doesn't say to stop having them; it asks what you put first once they arrive. Asking is slower, and it's one way to find out what the label left out. Who have you labelled so far without ever asking them one question about their story?

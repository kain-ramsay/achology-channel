## 1. What Does It Mean to Be Present, and Why Is It So Hard?

You're always halfway somewhere else. You're doing one thing with your hands and chewing on another in your head, and by the end of the day you feel you were never fully in any of it. It leaves you restless and a bit let down by yourself, because being fully present sounds as though it ought to be effortless.

What does being present in the moment mean? As Achology teaches it, it means being aware of yourself right now: you know what you're doing, the way you're going about it and your reasons for both. Your thoughts can stay busy the whole time, because this is about knowing where your attention is and why it's there, not about emptying your head. It's hard because distraction, memories and plans, and habit all pull attention away, and one study found that minds wandered in nearly half of the moments it sampled.

Knowing where your attention goes, and why, gives you something to work with besides blaming yourself for losing it. You'll be able to name what pulls it away, and to see how common that is. Then you can try a simple practice that brings it back, beginning with your very next conversation.

## 2. What Are the Real Benefits of Mindfulness, and the Limits?

Mindfulness is supposed to be good for you, and you couldn't say what that would mean in your own life. You're curious but sceptical, and you don't want to give your time to something that only sounds good. So the question is what it would give you, and what it wouldn't.

What are the benefits of mindfulness? At Achology, the answer is self-knowledge, and what you choose to do with it. Mindfulness here means looking inward, and it aims at better-informed decisions, a clearer view of your own part in how things turn out, and maturity of character. These are aims to grow towards, and nothing here promises them, because they depend on what you do.

Knowing what to expect, and what not to, lets you decide for yourself whether it's worth your time. You'll be able to see what looking inward can give you, and where its limits are. You'll also have one question about your own decisions that you can try this week, with no promise to live up to.

## 3. How Do You Know If You Lack Self-Awareness, and What Helps?

A nagging worry has settled in: that other people see something in you which you can't see yourself. Now and then someone's reaction shows you that what you intended and what they understood were different, and you can't work out why. It leaves you uneasy, somewhat exposed, and wondering what else you're missing.

So how would you know if you had a lack of self-awareness? In Achology's view, it shows up as a gap, either between what you meant and what the other person received, or between the reasons you give for what you do and the reasons really behind it. Achology treats mindfulness as a blend of self-awareness and self-reflection with self-knowledge and self-understanding: knowing, in the moment you're in, what you're doing and what's behind it. No single sign proves a lack of it, and there's no verdict to reach about yourself.

Once you can see the gap, an unexpected reaction stops being a private concern about yourself. You'll know where to look and how to ask a trusted person how you generally come across. You'll also be able to check whether the way you see yourself is accurate. Then you can decide on an appropriate response to what that shows.

## 4. What Is Person-Centred Therapy Trying to Achieve, and Why?

Lately everyone seems to know what you should do, and you've lost track of what you actually want. They mean well, and some of what they say makes sense, yet each opinion leaves you less sure of your own mind. You find yourself wishing someone would just let you think.

So what is person-centred therapy trying to achieve? The person-centred therapy goals come down to what Carl Rogers expected to happen: a person comes to understand themselves more fully and chooses their own direction. The counsellor stays non-directive because Rogers trusted that the person already has the capacity to do that. His own account of what he expected shows how far he took the idea.

You'll come away able to state the aim in Rogers's own terms and see why the counsellor doesn't advise or steer, even when advice would be easy to give. You'll also see where the approach reaches its limits. And you'll have one small thing to try in your next conversation.

## 5. Does CBT Work for Anxiety and Worry? What the Evidence Says

Your mind keeps running ahead to the worst, and you can't make it stop. Somewhere you know it's probably nothing, but the fear is quick and the reasoning is slow, and you're worn out by the whole thing. You want to believe something could help, but you're a little afraid it won't.

So, does CBT work for anxiety? The short answer is that it can help a little. The research gives a modest answer too. It shows small benefits when CBT is compared with another kind of support, and it can't promise a result to any one person who tries it, however much they want one.

Once you've seen what one recent review of the research found, and where its evidence runs thin, you can compare any promise of improvement with that evidence. You'll also see what worry is, and how two fair questions can test a thought. Nothing here asks you to accept CBT on trust alone.

## 6. What Are the Main Life Coaching Models, From GROW Onwards?

You keep hearing the same few names, GROW, the wheel of life, Egan, and nobody ever says what any of them is. You go along with it, a little uncertain of yourself, and you've started to suspect you aren't the only one who doesn't know. It would be a relief to understand what each one asks and where it originated.

So what are the main life coaching models, and who made them? The best known are GROW, which Sir John Whitmore and colleagues developed in the 1980s, and Gerard Egan's three-stage Skilled Helper, which originates in a published textbook. The wheel of life sits beside them, though its origin remains uncertain. Three more, PIE, STIR and the core identity model, come from one course's own teaching, and we found no outside author for any of them.

A model gives a conversation a shape, so it keeps moving toward something definite instead of drifting aimlessly. Once you understand which is which, you can choose one that suits a decision of your own. You'll also know which have a named author behind them and which are one course's own frameworks.

## 7. Which NLP Books Are Worth Reading, From Beginner Up?

NLP keeps catching your attention, but every time you go looking for something to read, the choice puts you off. Each title seems to promise a different way of thinking and speaking, and it's hard to tell which one is meant for a beginner. You feel a little behind before you've started, and wary of paying for books you'll never finish.

Which books to learn NLP from are worth reading, and in what order? Begin with a plain introduction, Introducing NLP by Joseph O'Connor and John Seymour. Then read two books that apply the ideas to everyday life, and leave the founders' own books for last, because each does a different job. None of them shows that NLP works, since the research reviews found little evidence for it.

Reading just one is still a sensible way to find out whether the subject interests you before you spend any more money or time. You'll know where to begin, and which of the other books can wait until the first has done its job. You'll also know what no book can promise, however well it's written.

## 8. How Do You Start Practising Mindfulness, and How Often?

You'd like to understand yourself better, and you've no idea where to begin. It seems like something other people just know how to do, and every week you put it off feels like a small failure. So you wonder whether there's a way in that's small enough to actually keep up.

How to practice mindfulness, then? At Achology, you start by looking at yourself. You ask one question about how you handled something today and why, and you come back to that question regularly, for a few moments each day. It takes only a moment with your own thoughts, at a point in your day that already exists.

One question, asked in a few moments a day, is small enough to keep up. You'll leave with one question to ask yourself and a rhythm of a few moments a day. You'll also know what tends to get in the way, so you can begin tomorrow without needing to get it right.

## 9. Is Mindfulness Scientifically Proven? What the Research Says

One week mindfulness is proven, the next it's hype, and you can't tell which to believe. You'd like to trust something that might help, but you don't want to be fooled either. So you're stuck between hoping and doubting, and a little embarrassed to admit that you don't know yet.

So, is mindfulness evidence based? For courses tested in trials, a large review found some gains over doing nothing, no clear edge over other active options, and real weaknesses in the studies. Achology.com's mindfulness is different: it means looking at what you're doing and asking why, and there the evidence is what you find out about yourself.

The answer decides how much weight you give the next headline about mindfulness. If you know what the review found and where its limits lie, you can answer anyone who asks. You'll have a way to test the other kind of mindfulness on the one person who can supply the evidence: you.

## 10. Is Mindfulness Religious, and Can Anyone Practise It?

Something holds you back from trying mindfulness, though you can't quite say what. You half suspect it's religion by another name, or that you'd need to believe the right things to belong, and you don't want to pretend. So you hesitate, feeling unsure and self-conscious, wanting a straight answer first.

Is mindfulness religious, and can anyone practise it? The word has Buddhist roots, but mindfulness without religion is what Achology teaches, and it's open to anyone. Here, mindfulness means noticing yourself as you go through the day: what you're doing, how you're going about it, and why. It asks nothing of you about what to believe, and about the beliefs you already hold it asks one thing: examine what they are and why you hold them.

Once you know what the practice asks of you and what it doesn't, you can decide about a class from what you know, not from a worry. You'll also have one question that works whatever you believe, and it's yours to use. Nothing in it needs you to hide what you believe, or to pretend to anything.

## 11. What Are Conditions of Worth in Person-Centred Counselling?

Wanting something for yourself can leave you feeling vaguely guilty, as though you'd asked for too much. You smooth things over, tell everyone it's fine, and a small shame follows that you can't quite explain, because nothing bad happened. Lately you've begun to wonder why it feels as if you have to earn your place with other people, and where that feeling came from.

So what are conditions of worth in person-centred counselling? A feeling like that may come from a belief, taken in from other people, that you're only acceptable if you think, feel or act in certain ways, and person-centred counselling calls it a condition of worth. Carl Rogers set the idea out in 1959. The approach sets unconditional positive regard against it: regard with no strings attached.

If the belief fits you, naming it can turn the guilt from a mystery into something you can look at. You'll see how these beliefs are usually picked up, how the approach pictures life when they loosen, and where the idea stops. Then you can decide for yourself what fits you and what doesn't.

## 12. Which CBT Books Are Worth Reading, From Beginner Up?

Your own thinking has been running the show lately, and you've decided it's time to understand it properly. You'd like to read your way there, but you've little energy to spare, and a long book that doesn't help would feel like another disappointment. You want the first book you choose to be the right one for you.

So what are the best CBT books for someone starting out? There isn't one best title, because the right book depends on where you are right now: a complete beginner needs a different book from someone already training as a practitioner. Achology's view is that a reading list should follow how a person learns. If you're new to this and read only one, make it Feeling Good by David D. Burns.

That matters because a book pitched at the wrong level can put you off a method that would have helped. With the right order, your effort goes into the thinking itself and not into choosing. You'll finish knowing where to begin and which titles can wait, and you won't lose an afternoon to a book that wasn't written for you.

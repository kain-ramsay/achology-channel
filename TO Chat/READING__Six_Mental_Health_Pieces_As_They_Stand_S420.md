## 1. Can Mental Health Affect Your Body, Sleep and Energy?

*Search description: How does mental health affect sleep: in both directions, research finds, and in Achology.com's view the thought keeping you awake can be checked.*



You're awake again in the middle of the night, too tired to think straight and too wound up to drift off. Your mind keeps running through tomorrow, and the more you try to stop it, the louder it gets. It's been like this for a while now, and you've started to wonder whether your sleep and your mind are feeding each other.

So can mental health affect your body, sleep and energy? A common way to ask it is "how does mental health affect sleep", and the health bodies that answer say the link runs both ways. Achology.com makes no medical claim, but it does teach something about the mind: your thoughts and beliefs shape what you feel and do, whether that is staying up rehearsing tomorrow or how you treat a tired morning.

In Achology's view, the thought that keeps you awake is a thought, so it can be checked. This matters because a worried thought can feel like a fact when you're lying awake with it. Knowing what research says, and what it can't say about you, gives you firmer ground than the night-time version of events. And knowing what a thought is gives you something useful to do with the next one.

### How does mental health affect sleep, according to research?

The [Sleep Health Foundation](https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/mental-health-and-sleep.html) in Australia writes: "The relationship between mental health and sleep is bidirectional which simply means that they affect each other, in either cause or effect." In other words, the link runs in both directions. The page shows no date, so this is the foundation's wording as read on 6 October 2026.

The [Mental Health Foundation](https://www.mentalhealth.org.uk/explore-mental-health/a-z-topics/sleep-and-mental-health) in the United Kingdom says sleep and mental health are closely related, and its page was last updated on 14 September 2021. So two bodies in two countries describe a connection that goes both ways. General information of this kind describes people in general, so it can't tell you which way the link runs for you.

A larger piece of evidence is a 2021 review of trials by [Scott and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2021.101556), published in Sleep Medicine Reviews. It pooled 65 trials, with 72 interventions and 8,608 participants, in which an intervention improved sleep. The authors report a medium-sized effect on a combined measure of mental health, and greater improvement in sleep went with greater improvement in mental health.

This evidence has limits. It reports on groups of people, not on individual sleepers, and the authors' measure of mental health was a composite one. The review also looked at one direction of the link only: what happened to mental health when sleep improved, not what happens to sleep when mental health changes. A 2025 review of trials by [Li and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-025-23709-w), covering about 54 papers and 10,196 adults, took the same one-direction approach, and its authors advise careful interpretation because most of the studies were small.

A different review looked at both directions over time. [Bacaro and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijchp.2023.100424), in a 2024 review, pooled 42 studies that followed adolescents over time, and their findings suggest that sleep, mental health and positive well-being are related in both directions. It studied adolescents, so it can't tell you what is true on your own nights.

### How do your thoughts and feelings shape a restless night?

Achology teaches about the mind, in education. What it does teach is that what a person thinks and believes shapes what they feel and what they go on to do. In Achology's view, that includes whether you stay up rehearsing tomorrow, how you handle a tired morning, and what you believe you can get done on little rest. It says nothing about why any person's body feels as it does, because that isn't what it teaches.

The mechanism is in one line from Kain Ramsay's Mental Health and Wellbeing Practitioner Diploma Course, where it appears as a [quote on focus](/learn/mental-wellness/quotes/why-you-get-distressed-by-what-you-focus-on/). It reads: "People get distressed by that which they focus on." Where your attention goes decides how you feel, so what you keep returning to is what distresses you.

A feeling always has a source: it follows from what a person is thinking about and the meaning they've given to it. A worry looks ahead to something that hasn't happened, such as tomorrow's conversation, so it can feel urgent without being true. A state is something you're in and never something you are, so a bad night is a night you're having, and it doesn't make you a person who can't cope.

### Is the thought in the night a fact?

A thought like "I'll be useless tomorrow" can arrive sounding like a report, when it's a prediction about something that hasn't happened. A reading like that feels like a fact but is only a thought, and any thought can be checked. Seen that way, the thought is something you can examine, and you don't have to obey it.

Say you wake with the thought "I'll be useless tomorrow". You check a thought like that by asking how accurate it is and how much use it is to you. A few plain questions can do that: have I managed a short night before, what is in tomorrow, and what would I tell a friend who said this to me? That doesn't make the worry vanish, and it isn't meant to, but it gives you a next choice that isn't more rehearsing.

This idea has limits. Achology makes no claim about what causes anyone's sleep problems, and none of its teaching is aimed at treating any condition. What it gives you is a choice about how to use the hour you're awake in.

### What about your energy the next day?

After a short night, a day can start with a thought such as "there's no point trying today", and in Achology's view a person who believes it behaves accordingly. They cancel the walk, skip the call, and decide in advance what they can get done. That's a belief steering the day, and it is separate from how tired anyone is.

Notice the step in the middle. The tiredness may be real, but "there's no point trying today" is a thought added on top of it, and that's the part you can look at. Asking whether it's accurate, and what one small thing you could still do, is a way of looking at that added thought, and says nothing about the tiredness itself.

None of this is a claim about the body or about medicine. Whatever a short night is doing to your body is a separate matter from the thoughts you add to it, and the thoughts are the only part Achology teaches about. For the wider picture of how Achology teaches wellbeing, [Mental Wellbeing Explained](/learn/mental-wellness/articles/mental-wellbeing/) is where to read next.

### What will you do the next time you wake in the night?

Go back to that night. The thought that arrived was real, and so was the tiredness, but the thought wasn't a verdict on tomorrow. It was something you were focused on, and where you focus is something you can look at. That is the choice in front of you at that hour, and it is a small one: to look at the thought, or to go on being led by it.

Achology holds that not everything that happens to you is yours to choose, a bad night included, though what you make of it is. The thought arriving at that hour is yours to look at, whatever the night has brought. So the next time you wake in the night and the rehearsal starts, you can see it as a thought you're having, and not a fact you've been handed.

### Put this into practice

Tonight, if you wake and a thought starts rehearsing tomorrow, name it as a thought: "I'm having the thought that..." Then ask whether it's accurate, whether it's useful, and what one thing you'll do next. In the morning, jot down how much weight that thought had once you'd looked at it.

## 2. How Do You Improve Your Mental Wellbeing, Day to Day?

*Search description: How to improve mental wellbeing day to day starts, in Achology.com's view, with the next feeling and the choice you make about it, not a new habit.*


It's late, you've finally sat down, and you're too tired to move and too wound up to rest. You've been getting by for weeks. You haven't fallen apart, but you haven't felt properly well either, and you've started to wonder whether this is just how things are now.

So how do you improve your mental wellbeing day to day? A lot of the advice on how to improve mental wellbeing is a list of habits, such as staying in touch with people, moving more, learning and giving. The National Health Service in England lists [five steps to mental wellbeing](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/self-help/guides-tools-and-activities/five-steps-to-mental-wellbeing/) along much the same lines. Achology.com's view is that the place to start is a step earlier, with the next feeling that arrives and the next choice you make about what to do with it.

This matters because, in Achology's view, mental wellness is a skill, and a skill can be learned. A feeling is only a feeling, and the choice that follows it belongs to you. That makes the moment a hard feeling arrives something you can work on, and not only something that happens to you. Once you can see that moment, you have somewhere to start on the days a list of habits isn't enough.

### What do the usual lists of wellbeing habits say?

The National Health Service in England has a page on five steps to mental wellbeing, which are connecting with other people, being active, learning new skills, giving to others and paying attention to the present moment. It says evidence suggests that five steps like these can improve mental health and wellbeing. It was last reviewed in December 2022, and its further information section points to a report on the five ways. The question is what to do on the day they don't feel possible.

The New Economics Foundation published its report, [Five Ways to Wellbeing](https://neweconomics.org/2008/10/five-ways-to-wellbeing), on 22 October 2008, written by Jody Aked, Nic Marks, Corrina Cordon and Sam Thompson. It identifies five actions around social relationships, physical activity, awareness, learning and giving. Its authors say the messages are intended to have generic appeal, while offering concrete activity-based ideas. In Achology's view, wellbeing starts with the next feeling that arrives, and a list written to suit almost everyone can't speak to the one feeling you have today.

In New Zealand, the [Mental Health Foundation](https://mentalhealth.org.nz/five-ways-to-wellbeing) publishes the Five Ways as connect, take notice, be active, give and keep learning. Its page says the New Economics Foundation reviewed the most up-to-date evidence and found that building five actions into day to day lives is important for the wellbeing of individuals, families, communities and organisations. So England and New Zealand describe much the same five actions.

A newer study tested the five actions as a programme. In 2024, [Prydz and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.2196/49050) published a randomised trial from Norway with 969 participants, some of whom took a 10-week online programme built on the Five Ways, with two webinars and text message reminders, while the others formed control groups. It found a greater increase in well-being in the intervention group than in the controls. Its results are for the group that took part, so how a list goes on one hard morning is still yours to find out.

A larger review looked at psychological interventions to improve mental wellbeing. [Van Agteren and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-021-01093-w), in Nature Human Behaviour in 2021, pooled 419 randomised trials with 53,288 people and found that mindfulness-based programmes and programmes combining several positive psychology activities did best. They add that the improvements were moderate at best and that the evidence quality was generally low to moderate.

A newer review, published in January 2026, compared well-being interventions. [Wilkie and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-025-02369-1), in Nature Human Behaviour, pooled 183 randomised trials with 22,811 people and found that most interventions improved well-being compared with groups that had no intervention or were on a waiting list. Mindfulness, compassion-based programmes, single positive psychology activities, yoga and exercise all showed moderate effects, with no significant differences between them. Programmes that combined exercise with a psychological element showed the largest effect, though that result rests on only three studies.

### How to improve mental wellbeing when a habit isn't enough?

Achology teaches that mental wellness is a skill. It's the ability to manage your mindset in the moment, and to keep control of what you do even while you're feeling something you'd rather not feel. So the starting point isn't a habit, but the next feeling that arrives and the next choice about how you respond to it. In that moment, the next thing you do is up to you.

Kain Ramsay says as much in the Mental Health and Wellbeing Practitioner Diploma Course, in a [quote on deciding](/learn/mental-wellness/quotes/why-life-is-a-constant-process-of-deciding/): "Life is a constant process of deciding what it is that we're going to do next". Read that way, wellbeing isn't a state you reach and then hold. In Achology's view, it's built from the small choices that follow each moment.

Achology doesn't hand you a list of exercises to run through, and that's deliberate. It teaches understanding before techniques, because, as Achology sees it, a calming technique can settle one moment, and knowing the source of a feeling is what changes the pattern behind it. That's why the first thing to learn is what a feeling is and where it comes from.

### What is a feeling, if it isn't a verdict?

In Achology's view, a feeling is information: it comes, and it goes, and by itself it says nothing about who you are. A hard feeling is something to read, never something to obey and never something to cover up. Take the tired, wound-up feeling from the start of this page. It points to where your mind has gone, and to the story you're telling yourself about how these weeks are going. What it hasn't done is tell you that the story is true.

In Achology's view, you learn your emotional reactions, usually without noticing, and that's good news, because anything learned can be understood. The thought riding along with the feeling can be understood too, and it can be checked: are these weeks really going badly, or do they only feel that way tonight? A separate Achology article on [thoughts and emotions](/learn/motivation/articles/thoughts-and-emotions-connection/) sets out how the two connect. You don't need to settle it tonight, only to notice that a feeling and a verdict aren't the same thing.

### Isn't this just thinking positive?

No. Achology doesn't ask you to think positive, and nor does it ask you to feel good first. A feeling is just a feeling, and you can have it without obeying it. What you do next stays yours, and it can be a small, useful step instead of a cheerful one.

Say a friend texts to ask if you can talk later, and you feel a jolt of worry. Achology's way is to let the feeling be there and notice the thought attached to it, such as "I've upset her". Then you choose your next step, which might be to reply that of course you can, ask how she is, and carry on with your morning. The feeling hasn't been argued away, and it hasn't been handed the wheel either.

In Achology's view, responsibility here is about the next step and never about blame. Nothing is wrong with you for having the feeling, and nobody else can take the next step for you. Achology presents self responsibility as an invitation, not a charge. Asking for help is one of the steps you can choose, and Achology counts it as a responsible one.

### What if the day is hard anyway?

Some days are hard whatever you choose, and the next choice doesn't have to be impressive. It might be to send the message, to step outside for 10 minutes, or to leave a decision until tomorrow. The habits on the lists can be among the small things you choose, and missing one breaks no rule. Achology's view is that wellbeing grows out of many small choices of this kind, made one after another.

Achology teaches what to do with a feeling, and it makes no medical claim, so it can't speak to what causes a condition or what anyone should do about one. What it offers is a way of reading your own feelings and choosing your next step, and its teaching stops there.

### What will you do after the next feeling?

Go back to that late evening. The feeling arrived before you'd decided anything, and feelings come and go. What changes is the minute after. In that minute, Achology's way is to read the feeling, check the thought that came with it, and choose one thing to do next, however the weeks have been.

A hard feeling can sit there while you decide. In Achology's view, it doesn't have to change before you choose well, because what you do next is already in your hands. So if the same evening comes round tomorrow and the same thoughts start, you'll know what you're deciding, and it isn't whether the weeks have gone badly but what you'll do next.

### Put this into practice

At the next feeling that gets your attention tomorrow, pause before you do anything with it. Name it in a word, such as "uneasy" or "flat", and write down the thought that came with it. Then choose one small thing to do next, and notice afterwards which had more say over your morning, the feeling or the choice.

## 3. Is Mental Health Genetic, or Is It Shaped by How You Live?

*Search description: Is mental health genetic? Research names genes as one influence among many. Here's what it says, where it stops and what's yours to work on.*




You've been told you get your worrying from your family, and some part of you has believed it ever since. When your mind runs away with you, it feels less like something you're doing and more like something you were handed. You wonder whether it has already been decided, and whether there is anything you can do about it.

So, is mental health genetic? Research from official bodies says genes and family history are one influence among many, and Achology.com adds its own view on the part that's yours to work on. The United States' National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) says a relative's history can raise your chance without settling it, while the World Health Organization (WHO) lists genetics as one risk factor among several.

A relative's remark about where a habit came from can feel like a ruling on who you are and what you're bound to become. Seeing what that remark leaves out lets you hear it as a clue instead. Achology makes no claim about genes, and the research it reports is other teaching, not its own. You'll also see why, in Achology's view, a family pattern is something to understand, never a sentence to serve.

### What do official bodies say about genes, and where does it stop?

NIMH is the United States government's lead research agency on mental disorders, and its page [Looking at My Genes](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/looking-at-my-genes) is aimed at people wondering what their genes mean for them. It says, "Some mental disorders run in families, and your family's mental health history may be an important clue". It adds that having a close relative with a mental disorder "could mean you are at a higher risk, but it doesn't necessarily mean you will develop that disorder."

The same page makes two more points. It says, "Many other factors play a role," and "Currently, genetic tests cannot accurately predict your risk of developing a mental disorder." That's a plain statement from an official body: a family history is a clue, not a verdict, and for now a genetic test can't turn it into a forecast for you.

The WHO's [fact sheet on mental disorders](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/mental-disorders) says, "Protective and risk factors include individual psychological and biological factors, such as emotional skills as well as genetics." It also says people exposed to adverse circumstances, including poverty, violence, disability and inequality, are at higher risk. So genes sit on the list beside emotional skills and circumstances, which is far from a single cause.

Twin studies report a figure called heritability, a term the [National Library of Medicine's MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/genetics/understanding/inheritance/heritability/) defines as "a measure of how well differences in people's genes account for differences in their traits." It also says, "Heritability does not indicate what proportion of a trait is determined by genes and what proportion is determined by environment." So a heritability figure can't tell you how much of your own mental health is down to genes.

A [pooled analysis of twin studies](https://doi.org/10.1038/ng.3285), published in Nature Genetics in 2015 by Tinca Polderman and colleagues, covered 17,804 traits from 2,748 publications. It reported a heritability of 49% across all traits. That's one figure across every kind of human trait, not a figure for mental health, and it comes from pairs of twins, not from any one person's life story.

So is mental health genetic for you in particular? The research these bodies describe reaches as far as general patterns, and the NIMH says that for now genetic tests can't predict your own risk accurately. That research can say that family history is one influence among several, and that some difficulties are more common in some families. It can't say what will happen to you, because a relative's history doesn't necessarily decide it.

A second WHO page reaches the same conclusion by another route. Its [fact sheet on mental health](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/mental-health-strengthening-our-response) says, "At any one time, a diverse set of individual, family, community and structural factors may combine to protect or undermine mental health." The key word is "combine". Many things act together, so no single one is a sentence handed down from one generation to the next.

All of this is other teaching, from official bodies and published researchers, and Achology doesn't claim it as its own. Achology teaches about the mind, never the brain, so it makes no claim about genes and can't tell you what yours do. What Achology can speak to is the part that's yours, and that's a separate matter from where a habit began.

### Is mental health genetic, or is it learned by watching?

Think back to being told you get it from your family. When someone says that, they could mean a gene, or they could mean that you grew up watching two people worry, and the remark can't tell those apart. In Achology's view, you don't need to settle that to make progress, and Achology makes no claim about which is true. What it does teach is about reactions.

In Achology's view, a person learns their emotional reactions, mostly without noticing, and whatever was learned that way can be looked at and understood. A child who watches a parent brace for bad news can pick up bracing as the normal way to meet a day, and carry it for decades without ever deciding to. Achology adds that this isn't blame on the parent, who learned it from somebody too. It's a reason to look at your own reactions with curiosity and not with a verdict.

Whatever your family passed down, noticing a habit as it happens is, in Achology's view, a different act from being the habit. Achology holds that a spell of mental struggle is something a person goes through, never who they are. Worrying like a parent or grandparent is a pattern you can see, not a family name you were handed.

### What part of your mental health is yours to work on?

In Achology's view, the part that's yours is your thinking, your beliefs, how you treat your own feelings, and what you then do. It doesn't say you chose your family, your history or your starting point, and it doesn't say that working on your mind is easy. It says that from here, the next thing you think and do is yours.

Kain Ramsay, an Achology instructor, says it in a sentence from his Mental Health and Wellbeing Practitioner Diploma Course, and that sentence has a page of its own: [Kain's quote on freedom](/learn/mental-wellness/quotes/the-only-area-with-any-freedom-whatsoever/). The sentence reads, "The only area of life that we have any freedom whatsoever is in our capacity to choose to be and do different." The line doesn't say everything can be changed or that choosing is easy. It says the capacity to choose is yours, whoever your family is.

Here's how Achology's view looks on an ordinary day. A familiar worry arrives, the kind your family would recognise, and you notice it as a thought you've had many times. You test the thought for accuracy and for use, then pick a single small action, perhaps a walk or a phone call. Nobody can promise that it will change how you feel, because nobody can grow for you, but it puts your own hand on the next step.

Is mental health genetic in your family? This view leaves that question to the research and to people who know your history, and it says nothing about your body. If you want to read further on living from the inside, the Achology article [Who Holds the Master Controls](/learn/personal-growth/articles/internal-versus-external-locus-of-control/) takes the idea on at length.

### What did that remark leave out?

Back to that remark: someone told you where they think it came from, and they may well be right that the habit runs in your family. What the remark left out is the second half of the sentence, which is what you do with a habit once you can see it. In Achology's view, seeing the habit clearly begins the work, and choosing what to do next is something nobody can inherit or hand to you.

The usual instinct is to put the effort into where a habit came from, which is an interesting question that Achology doesn't claim to settle. What you do with it from here is a different question, and it doesn't need a verdict on your family or a denial of its history. It asks only that you notice what you think, what you believe and what you do. Someone else can tell you where they think it came from, and only you can decide where it goes.

### Put this into practice

Next time a familiar worry arrives, say aloud or write, "this is a thought I've had before." Check it for accuracy and for usefulness, then pick one small action for the next hour. Notice how the worry feels once you've named it.

## 4. Can Mental Health Improve Without Therapy or Medication?

*Search description: Mental health without therapy or medication can grow, in Achology.com's view. The World Health Organization says care works for many conditions.*


You've been low, or someone you love has, and you don't know what would really help. You hope it can ease with time and your own effort, then worry that hoping so is a way of avoiding something you ought to face. Every answer you reach for feels either too small or too big for how heavy it is.

So, can mental health improve without therapy or medication? Mental health without therapy or medication can improve, because Achology.com teaches that anyone's inner life can develop further, at whatever age they are, and nobody is being advised to stop, avoid or alter care they've chosen. The World Health Organization publishes a [fact sheet on mental health](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/mental-health-strengthening-our-response) saying many mental health conditions can be effectively treated.

When you wonder what to do about a low stretch, in yourself or in someone you love, you'll have more than a guess to offer. You'll know what the health bodies say about care and self-care. You'll also have a plain question for any story of someone who got better alone.

### What do the major health bodies say, and what does Achology add?

According to the fact sheet from the World Health Organization, many mental health conditions can be effectively treated at relatively low cost. It adds that health systems remain significantly under-resourced and that "treatment gaps are wide all over the world". So care works for many conditions, and the gaps in who receives it are wide. That's worth knowing before you decide anything about going without.

The National Institute of Mental Health's page on caring for your mental health describes self-care as something that can support care. It says self-care "can play a role in maintaining your mental health", and that it can support the care of someone who has a condition.

The National Health Service (NHS) has a page on [5 steps to mental wellbeing](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/self-help/guides-tools-and-activities/five-steps-to-mental-wellbeing/), and it says, "Evidence suggests there are 5 steps you can take to improve your mental health and wellbeing." Its steps are everyday ones, such as connecting with other people, being physically active and learning new skills. So the official picture across these three bodies has two parts: ordinary habits that support mental health, and professional care for conditions that call for it.

None of these three pages tells you how likely you are to improve without care, and a figure for a group of people could not tell you that for yourself either. In Achology's view, a story of someone who got better alone is one person's story, so ask of it what else was happening in that person's life.

One study from 2023 does look at improvement without care. [Roach and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2023-072093) reviewed studies of young people aged 10 to 24 with depression or anxiety who were not receiving any specific treatment. The authors concluded that "after 1 year about 54% of young people with symptoms of anxiety and/or depression recover without any specific mental health treatment." That is a finding about young people as a group, and it can't predict what will happen for you or for a friend of a different age.

Asked as people type it, the question reads like a fork in the road, with professional care on one side and mental health without therapy on the other. In Achology's view, that fork leaves out a third thing, which is the way a person uses their own mind. Achology works in education, on the subject of the mind, and says nothing about the brain; its [mental wellbeing guide](/learn/mental-wellness/articles/mental-wellbeing/) sets out how it teaches mental wellbeing as practised skills built on self-knowledge. None of what it teaches counts as medical care.

As Achology teaches it, a person's mental health is the condition of their inner life, and it shows over time in the life they lead. Nothing about it is settled for life, because it keeps being shaped by what a person sees, believes, feels and does. That's why it's open to development in anyone, whatever their age, though never quickly and never easily.

### What does mental health without therapy look like day to day?

In Achology's view, it begins with seeing your own mind more accurately, because a person can't change what they can't see. Achology teaches that much of what people call a bad stretch is a pattern of thinking that has never been examined. Take the moment before a phone call you've been putting off, when your chest tightens and you tell yourself you'll make a mess of it. That line sounds like a fact, yet it's a thought, and any thought is open to being checked.

So, in Achology's view, you ask two questions: is this thought accurate, and is it useful? If you've handled awkward calls before, that's evidence against the thought, and the thought itself is making the call harder than it needs to be. Then you choose what to do next, such as writing down your first sentence or telling someone you're nervous. Achology teaches that a feeling is information to read, not an order to obey, and that a bad stretch for the mind is one you're in the middle of, never a verdict on you.

Kain Ramsay teaches the whole idea in one line in his Mental Health and Wellbeing Practitioner Diploma Course, and that line is the subject of a separate page, [Kain's quote on choice](/learn/mental-wellness/quotes/why-everyone-has-a-choice-to-change/). The words are, "Everyone can change because, as William Glasser says, everyone has a choice." The line doesn't promise that every outcome can be chosen, or that choosing is easy. What it offers instead is the idea that what a person does next belongs to them, and in Achology's view that includes the choice to read and to learn.

### What are you waiting for someone else to decide?

Go back to that not knowing. You don't have to know whether the answer is a course, a long walk or a quiet word, because you were never being asked to decide it alone. You can ask yourself, or the person you love, what is needed next, and you can say that asking for help is a responsible step. Then you can notice that the same question, what do I need next, is one you're allowed to ask about your own mind.

Kain's line is about where change starts, and in Achology's view the choice was never between help and no help. It's between waiting for somebody else to do your changing and deciding what you'll do next, with whatever help you choose to bring in. It's tempting to wait, hoping the right answer will announce itself. Nobody can make that choice for you, and nobody has to make it alone.

### Put this into practice

When a worry starts to loop tonight, write it as one sentence. Below it, judge it for accuracy and usefulness, then add one small thing you'll do next. Notice how the thought feels outside your head.

## 5. Should Mental Health Be Taught in Schools, and How?

*Search description: Should mental health be taught in schools? Achology.com says yes, as skills. Large reviews found gains; one trial found no evidence mindfulness was better.*


You can remember how it felt to be young and to feel something heavy with no idea what to call it or who to tell. Nobody taught you what to do with a thought like "I'm just stupid", and you've wondered since whether someone should have. Now you watch children go through the same thing, and you wonder whether school is the place to help.

Should mental health be taught in schools, and how? Achology.com says yes, as skills, though the evidence is mixed: three large reviews found gains for pupils in programmes offered to every pupil in a school, while one large trial found no evidence mindfulness lessons beat normal lessons. What to teach is the harder question, and Achology's view starts with the way a mind works: any thought can be checked, and every feeling has a cause.

It matters because the answer decides what young people are given then, and the World Health Organization (WHO) calls adolescence "a crucial period for developing social and emotional habits important for mental well-being". The WHO and the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO) have published global standards for health-promoting schools. You'll be able to say what the evidence shows and doesn't. You'll also have a plain question ready for a child who says "I'm just stupid".

### What do the WHO and UNESCO say about schools and well-being?

The WHO's [adolescent mental health fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/adolescent-mental-health) lists "developing coping, problem-solving, and interpersonal skills" and "learning to manage emotions" among the social and emotional habits it calls important for mental well-being. It also says that "Protective and supportive environments in the family, at school and in the wider community are important." Those are statements about skills and surroundings. Neither one describes a lesson on conditions.

In 2021 the World Health Organization and UNESCO published [global standards for health-promoting schools](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240025059). Their overview says: "No education system is effective unless it promotes the health and well-being of its students, staff and community." The publication drew on a review of school health policies from 91 countries. It's built from the experience of many countries and not of one nation.

The fact sheet and the standards' overview say why schools matter and which skills help. Neither says what a school should put on the timetable. That leaves the harder question open: what should children be taught? The research on school programmes is where that question starts to get answers, and the answers are mixed.

### What does the research on school programmes find?

[Durlak and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2010.01564.x) pooled 213 school-based programmes that taught social and emotional learning (SEL) to every pupil. They covered 270,034 students from kindergarten to high school. Compared with control groups, pupils showed "significantly improved social and emotional skills, attitudes, behavior, and academic performance", including an 11-percentile-point gain in achievement, which means the average pupil rose 11 places in a ranking of 100. The abstract adds that "School teaching staff successfully conducted SEL programs", and that problems in delivery changed how large the results were.

A newer and larger study of the same kind points the same way. [Cipriano and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.13968) pooled 424 studies from 53 countries, with 575,361 students in all. They report that, compared with control conditions, students in universal school-based SEL programmes "experienced significantly improved skills, attitudes, behaviors, school climate and safety, peer relationships, school functioning, and academic achievement."

The picture is less encouraging in a trial by [Kuyken and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1136/ebmental-2021-300396) in 84 schools in the United Kingdom (UK). It compared mindfulness lessons with normal school provision for 8,376 students aged 11 to 14. The authors found no evidence that the mindfulness programme was better than normal provision after a year. They wrote that the findings "do not support the superiority" of the programme "in promoting mental health in adolescence."

A 2025 review adds a caution about what school programmes achieve. [Hayes and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.3389/frcha.2025.1526840) pooled 71 studies covering 63,041 young people. Pupils in universal school-based programmes had significantly improved anxiety and depression, but not broader internalising outcomes, which are the troubles felt inside, not the ones acted out. The authors call the effects small, and for anxiety, programmes built on cognitive behavioural therapy principles, which link thoughts, feelings and actions, did better than those built on mindfulness.

Taken together, the studies say that teaching children skills can help. They also say that one programme can fail to help. That is useful, but it isn't the whole answer. None of these abstracts describes a lesson built around conditions and warning signs, so these studies can't settle the content question alone.

### Should mental health be taught in schools as a list of conditions?

Mental health teaching can take the form of a lesson on conditions and their warning signs. Then pupils and staff can spot who is struggling. Achology's way of helping starts with understanding the person before any name is offered for what they're going through. A list of conditions answers one question only: what name fits someone else.

Achology holds that a list can't answer the question a child asks when she says "I'm just stupid". That question is about what's happening in her own mind right now. A child who can only name conditions has learned vocabulary, not an understanding of their own mind. Achology trains practitioners to help someone see where they stand and then own the next step.

There's also a risk, in Achology's view, in starting with the list. A child who learns the names of conditions may begin to read their own passing feelings as a label for who they are. Feeling anxious then becomes being an anxious child. Achology keeps a child separate from any feeling or condition they have, and a lesson can start from there.

### What would a lesson on how a mind works hold?

A lesson built on Achology's teaching would hold three ideas. They would be taught with the same everyday examples a child already knows. The first is that a thought is not a fact, and a child can weigh one up by asking whether it fits the facts and whether it does any good. "I'm just stupid" fails both, because one wrong answer doesn't make a pupil stupid, and the sentence doesn't help her do the next one.

The second, in Achology's teaching, is that a feeling has a cause. What a person feels comes out of what they were thinking, and out of the meaning they gave to the event. So the shame after a wrong answer comes from the sentence a child tells herself, not from the answer. A child who sees that link has already begun to work with the feeling rather than be run by it.

The third, in Achology's teaching, is that the choice is always there, even when it can't be felt. Here is Kain Ramsay, speaking in the Mental Health and Wellbeing Practitioner Diploma Course: "We always have a choice, but we don't know we have a choice, especially in the early days when we're just learning we have a choice." Kain's [quote on choice](/learn/mental-wellness/quotes/why-we-dont-know-we-have-a-choice/) is about that noticing, a skill built through repetition, not something a child simply knows or doesn't.

### Who can teach it, and where does the idea stop?

In Achology's view, the adults go first. Nobody can help another person see what they haven't learned to see in themselves, so a teacher who has never checked one of their own thoughts will struggle to teach it. The Durlak review found that school teaching staff could run SEL programmes successfully. A teacher could also read the article on [the purpose of a teacher](/learn/motivation/articles/what-is-the-purpose-of-a-teacher-and-why-ask-for-what-purpose/), written by a teacher and football coach, which offers one question to ask of their own role.

The limits are plain. Achology's view sits beside the research and is not a finding of it, and nothing in the studies named shows that a lesson on how a mind works improves any outcome. Achology's view says nothing about what any country's schools are required to teach.

None of this blames a parent or a teacher. It is only about what a child has already learned to say to herself. Here, in Achology's view, responsibility looks forward to what an adult does next, and nobody is expected to carry it alone.

### What will you say the next time a child says it?

Go back to that thought, "I'm just stupid". If a child said it to you, you could tell her she isn't, and she might not believe you. In Achology's view, change comes when a person sits with an idea and asks what is true for them. A question she can ask herself, whether the thought is true and whether it helps, is a different kind of help from a kind sentence, and nobody can ask it for her. Somebody can show her that it exists.

That's the choice in front of any adult who cares about a child. Achology teaches that emotional reactions are learned, mostly without our noticing, and that some of what we believe about ourselves was planted by someone else and never corrected. In Achology's view, a school can pass on something else, but only if the adults in it have learned the question themselves. So which sentence about yourself, this week, have you never once checked?

### Put this into practice

This week, when you hear yourself say something harsh about yourself, such as "I'm hopeless at this," stop and ask two questions: is it true, and does it help? Write down your answers. Notice whether the thought still sounds as certain on paper. If a child you know says something similar, ask them the same two questions, kindly.

## 6. Why Does Mental Health Matter So Much in Everyday Life?

*Search description: Why mental health is important in everyday life: in Achology.com's view, the quality of your inner life produces the quality of your outer one.*



You hear all the time that mental health matters, and you agree, but it sounds like something for people in a hard time. Nothing is going badly for you. Still, you've caught yourself being short with people, or flat, or stuck in your own head, and you wonder whether that is part of what they mean.

Why does mental health matter so much in everyday life? Achology.com puts the answer this way: the quality of your inner life produces the quality of your outer one, and that is why mental health is important on days like that. Nothing has to be going wrong for it to matter, because the same inner life is at work on a quiet day as on a hard one.

It matters because, in Achology's view, a passing message on a quiet day deserves your attention long before anything goes wrong. The idea has limits, because working on the inside doesn't change a cruel manager or an overdue bill. And there's one small way to test it this week: catch the first thing you decide a message means.

### What does the World Health Organization mean by mental health?

The [World Health Organization](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/mental-health-strengthening-our-response) gives a definition in the fact sheet it publishes on mental health. It calls mental health "a state of mental well-being that enables people to cope with the stresses of life, realize their abilities, learn and work well, and contribute to their community." Nothing in that definition is about illness, and getting through a hard week or learning something new for work is the kind of thing it covers. The same page adds that "a diverse set of individual, family, community and structural factors may combine to protect or undermine mental health."

Achology comes at the question from the inside. Achology looks at mental health as the working order of your inner life, and over time it shows in the life you live. Each day adds to it: what a person does with what they feel, what they think and believe, and how they see the world. In Achology's view, anyone can grow it at any age, because it isn't something fixed that you either have or lack, and the [mental wellbeing guide](/learn/mental-wellness/articles/mental-wellbeing/) on this site walks through how Achology teaches mental wellbeing.

### Why mental health is important before anything goes wrong

The familiar belief is that mental health is for when trouble arrives: a bad patch, a crisis, a moment when everything seems to go wrong. That view isn't wrong about those times. It does assume the inner life stays dormant until something breaks, though, and in Achology's view it is working on every message and every decision of the day.

Take two people who get the same message from the same colleague. One reads it as a request for a quick chat about the rota. The other reads it as the start of a complaint, and spends the morning defending herself against a conversation that hasn't happened. The message is identical, but the mornings aren't, and in Achology's view what separates them is the reading each person gave it.

Now multiply that by every ordinary day. In Achology's view, whether you answer a friend warmly or tersely, and whether you put a task off or begin it, are both the inner life showing up in the outer one. That's why mental health matters so much in everyday life: everyday life is where it happens, long before anything dramatic does.

### How does your inner life shape the life you live?

Achology teaches a chain. Your view of the world sets the thoughts you have, those thoughts form what you believe and feel, and what you believe and feel drives what you do. What you do, repeated for years, becomes the quality of your life, and mental health is where that chain shows most clearly. The first link is the view you take of what happens.

Kain Ramsay teaches, in the Mental Health and Wellbeing Practitioner Diploma Course: "Life hangs in the balance of our judgments." Achology's reading of the [quote on judgment](/learn/mental-wellness/quotes/why-life-hangs-in-the-balance-of-our-judgments/) is that a verdict on another person can shift your own mood, often so fast that you never notice making it. A verdict of "she's about to criticise me" costs you a morning. A verdict of "I don't know what she wants yet" costs you nothing.

Achology teaches that a judgment is a view, not a fact, and that a view can be checked. You can ask the colleague, or remember the last six times a short message from her turned out to be about the rota. Achology holds that a feeling grows out of the thought and the meaning you've attached to it, and not directly out of the event. So seeing the judgment is where the pattern starts to change.

### What does it change in a normal week?

At work, the colleague's message is only one case. A person who reads every short message as a threat may over-prepare, avoid the conversation or arrive defensive, and the people around them may respond to the defensiveness rather than to the person. A person who reads the same message as unknown may ask a question and get on with the morning.

At home, the same pattern runs in the evening. You walk through the door having replayed an argument from the afternoon, and the person who greets you meets a face that has been somewhere else for an hour. They haven't done anything, but they receive the result, and neither of you may work out why the evening felt strained.

With money, it shows up as the envelope left unopened on the kitchen shelf for a fortnight. The bill is the same size whether you open it today or next week, and what grows in the meantime is the dread. In Achology's view, that dread follows from a judgment, which means it's something you can question.

### Can working on the inside change what happens to you?

Not directly, and Achology doesn't claim it can. The same fact sheet lists poverty and violence among the social and environmental factors that increase the risk of mental health conditions. It also lists what protects, which includes social and emotional skills and positive social interactions. A person can't think their way out of a cruel workplace, and nobody should be told they can.

What working on the inside changes, in Achology's view, is how you act within the circumstances you have. On that view, the inner life comes first and circumstances tend to follow, while waiting for circumstances to change first rarely works. That is a direction and not a promise, and it doesn't mean a hard life is a thinking error.

Take someone who dreads every meeting with a difficult manager. If they start by checking what they've assumed the manager thinks, they may prepare differently and ask better questions, and the manager may respond, or may not. Either way, the next choice, such as whether to stay, is still the person's own, and they can make it with a clearer head.

In Achology's view, none of this is blame; it's good news, since an inner life that is yours is yours to change. Responsibility, in that sense, concerns your next move and not fault for what happened, and you are never expected to manage it alone.

### Back to the message from your colleague

Go back to that colleague's message. You can't know yet what she wants to say, but you can notice what you've already decided it means, because that's where the day is being made. In Achology's view, a judgment nobody examines goes on running the day, and the day gets blamed on bad luck. The step in front of you is a modest one: spot the judgment before it takes charge of the day.

Take Kain Ramsay's words into the week. Before you open the next message, ask yourself what you're about to decide it means, and whether you can be sure that it's true. If one judgment can tip the balance of a morning, then the inner life isn't a subject for a bad patch; it's the workshop where every ordinary day gets built.

### Put this into practice

Tomorrow, the first time a message or remark makes you tense, stop before replying. Write one sentence saying what you've decided it means, then one sentence saying what you know for certain. Notice how far apart the two sentences sit, and answer only from the second.

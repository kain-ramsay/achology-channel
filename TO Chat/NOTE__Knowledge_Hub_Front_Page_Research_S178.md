## 3. What they share
1. They name how the library is arranged before showing any content.
2. Each way in says how it cuts (by subject, by what you want, by kind).
3. Every door shows proof it is full (counts, covers, headlines).
4. A door for the visitor who does not know what they want (a question, a quiz, a problem list).
5. Search beside browsing, never alone.
6. No row is a dead end.
7. The paid offer comes last and grows gently; trust sits between the free and the paid.

## 4. Suggestions for the front page
1. One line naming the system: seven subjects, four kinds, thirty six topics across them (words from Kain or Chat).
2. The chosen subject opens into its four kinds as doors, each with its real count and one real cover (the Psyche model); empty kinds handled honestly.
3. "What brings you here?" answered by the nine Problems topics, leading to topic pages and their three matched courses.
4. The Approaches family leads straight to the schools: the shortest journey to a course.
5. The signed banner, dark bar, kinds row, wall and learning path stay as they are.

## 5. Flags against what is settled (none overturns a ruling)
1. Trust: nearly every trusted site shows who writes and checks its library on the front page; the trust line left the banner (S177). It may belong lower on the page.
2. Faces as a door: an A to Z of thinkers would be a strong door, but the S306 ruling says no author hub; it could only open pieces.
3. Size: Wikipedia and Wellcome open with one plain size line; the four counts of DSRD 9 section 39.4 are no longer on the page.
4. Workbooks: the one take away door has nothing published behind it.

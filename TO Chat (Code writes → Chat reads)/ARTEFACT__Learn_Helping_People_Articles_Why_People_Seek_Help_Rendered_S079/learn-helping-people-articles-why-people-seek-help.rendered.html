Why People Seek Help, and What They Actually Need From It

24 August 2026 · Prof. Gerard Egan · 7 min read

There is a gap between why someone walks into a helping relationship and what they actually need once they are there. In more than four decades of teaching and researching the helping process, I have come to believe that closing that gap is the real work of the skilled helper. Most people arrive carrying a story. What they need is someone willing to help them understand it.

What Brings Someone to a Helper

The reasons people seek help are rarely as straightforward as they appear. A person might say they are struggling with anxiety, or a relationship breakdown, or a career that no longer makes sense. And all of that is true. But underneath the presenting concern, there is almost always something less named: a sense that they are stuck in a pattern they cannot see clearly enough to change.

People seek help, in my experience, not because they are broken, but because they have reached the limit of what they can do alone. That is not a failure. It is a remarkably human recognition.

Most of us can sustain our own navigation through familiar territory. When the terrain shifts and the old maps stop working, we look for someone who can help us read the ground.

The mistake a helper can make at this point is to rush toward solving the problem as it was presented. The presenting problem is rarely the whole story. It is the door. What the client actually needs is someone willing to open it with them, not someone who has already decided what is on the other side.

Consider a man who arrives saying he wants help managing his temper at work. That is a real problem and it deserves attention.

But if the helper begins there, with techniques for managing anger, they may never learn that the temper appeared eighteen months ago, that it coincided with a promotion he did not want and could not refuse, and that what he is actually living with is a slow, unspoken sense that he has agreed to a life he did not choose. The anger is a symptom of something the man himself has not yet put into words. A helper who solves the presenting problem here has solved the wrong problem competently.

The Three Things Clients Are Usually Asking For

Across a great many helping relationships, I have come to think that what people are asking for, underneath whatever they say, tends to fall into three categories, and it is useful for a helper to know which one is in the room.

The first is clarity. The person cannot see their own situation well enough to act on it. Too many things are tangled together, or one thing is so large that it obscures everything around it. What they need is not advice but the experience of laying it out in front of someone who will help them see the shape of it.

The second is permission. The person often already knows what they want to do, and what they lack is the sense that it is legitimate to want it. This is more common than most helpers expect.

They are not looking for a decision. They are looking for the experience of saying the thing out loud to someone who does not immediately argue them out of it.

The third is capability. The person knows what they want and believes they are allowed to want it, and genuinely does not know how to get there. This is the only one of the three where anything resembling instruction is appropriate, and it is the least common of the three at the point of first contact, though helpers frequently treat it as the default.

Reading which of these is present, and not assuming, is one of the more consequential judgements in early helping.

What Clients Actually Need

If I had to name the one thing clients need from a helper, it would be this: the experience of being genuinely understood before anything else happens.

Not reassured. Not advised. Not redirected. Understood.

There is a passage in The Skilled Helper where I describe what a good helper makes possible: not the heroic intervention, not the transformative breakthrough, but the quieter thing, which is a relationship in which the client feels safe enough to look honestly at their own situation. That safety is not a soft precondition to the real work. It is the real work, in its early and most critical form.

Clients who feel understood are more likely to explore honestly. They are more willing to surface the things they have been avoiding, to name the beliefs that are keeping them stuck, to consider possibilities they had ruled out.

The client who feels managed or assessed or corrected tends to do the opposite. They perform. They present the version of themselves they think the helper wants to see.

This is why I hold the view that empathy is not a therapeutic technique. It is a stance, and it shapes everything that follows.

Why This Matters for How We Help

If what clients need first is to be understood, then the helper’s primary discipline in the early stages of any helping relationship is not problem-solving. It is careful, unhurried listening, and the ability to communicate what has been heard in a way that shows the client they have been met.

This has practical consequences. A helper who moves too quickly to action planning, who introduces goals before the client’s story has been properly heard, will often find that the action plan does not stick. Not because the plan is wrong, but because the client did not feel understood well enough to fully own it.

It also changes how a helper thinks about time. A session spent entirely on exploration, with no plan produced and no action agreed, can look from the outside like a session in which nothing happened. It is frequently the session that makes everything after it possible. Helpers under pressure to demonstrate progress are pushed toward the visible and away from the foundational, and the cost of that pressure is usually paid three or four sessions later, when a well-designed plan quietly fails to be enacted.

The structure I have described in The Skilled Helper is not a system to follow mechanically. It is a map for a human process. The map is useful precisely because it keeps the helper oriented toward the client’s needs rather than their own need to feel helpful.

What Could You Do With These Ideas?

If you are working as a helper, or developing toward that work, I would invite you to sit with one question: in your recent helping conversations, how much time passed before you moved from listening to advising?

Not because advising is always wrong. But because the ratio often reveals where the pressure is. When we move quickly, it is frequently our discomfort with sitting in another person’s uncertainty, rather than a genuine signal that the client is ready to move.

The discipline of slowing down, of staying with the client’s experience a little longer than feels comfortable, is one of the most transferable skills in this work. It is also, in my observation, one of the least practiced. It costs nothing to try in your next conversation, and it tends to reveal quickly how much you had been supplying that the other person had not asked for.

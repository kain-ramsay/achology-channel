## 1. What this series is

Achology.com holds seven foundational beliefs. They are Achology's own presuppositions: one sequence, each belief building on the one before, that every course rests on. Kain's order and wording, exactly:

1. Every human being can grow and mature
2. Self-knowledge comes before personal growth
3. Wisdom begins with accurate thinking
4. Emotions can be understood and managed
5. Your emotional state is your responsibility
6. Personal growth comes before a better life
7. Personal growth always serves a purpose

The series is nine articles, read in order like chapters: Part 1 an introduction, Parts 2 to 8 one belief each, Part 9 a conclusion that puts the seven back together.

Kain's own words on what he wants: deeply researched, referenced and credible pieces, not just a story of what Achology believes. Each article is built on one principle, that we all stand on the shoulders of giants. Each shows the thinkers, psychologists, models and philosophies that led Achology to the belief, so the reader sees how Achology arrived at it.

## 3. The two articles

**Part 1 of 9: Introduction, standing on the shoulders of giants.** What a presupposition is. Why every way of helping people rests on some, stated or not. Why Achology states its seven openly. The seven shown as one chain, each handing on to the next. The shoulders-of-giants idea itself, from Bernard of Chartres (via John of Salisbury, *Metalogicon*, 1159) to Newton's 1675 letter to Hooke. Korzybski's "the map is not the territory" and the NLP presuppositions (Bandler and Grinder) as the model for what a presupposition is. Ends by handing the reader to Part 2.

**Part 2 of 9: Every human being can grow and mature.** Opens where Part 1 left off. Traces the belief through the Part 2 family tree, oldest first: Aristotle, Kierkegaard, William James, Rogers, Maslow, Erikson, Piaget, Kohlberg, Gardner, the Pygmalion effect (Rosenthal and Jacobson, 1968), Dweck. Shows what the belief looks like in an ordinary life, with one example carried through. Says plainly what it does not mean: not easy, not on a timetable, not a promise of any result, not a denial of what someone has been through. Ends by handing the reader to Part 3.

You do not have to give every thinker equal space. Build a line of thought, not a roll call. Where a thinker adds little, one sentence is enough.

## 4. Every article, the same way

- **Length:** 2,500 to 3,500 words each. A band, not a target.
- **Voice:** the warm base voice, no pen-name accent, speaking as Achology ("we") to the reader ("you"), as the What Achology Believes page does. This was Chat's call, not Kain's. If he wants a named byline he will say so when he reads.
- **Series panel:** head each article "Part N of 9" and close with a one-line pointer to the next part.
- **Links:** each article links to the What Achology Believes page (`/about/what-achology-believes/`) and to the other parts.
- **No demand check.** Kain ruled this series is a deliberate exception to the rule that articles answer searched questions. Do not retitle to a search phrase.
- **Paragraphs:** at least three sentences or 50 words, one shorter line allowed per section. Reading ease 60 to 70. Zero em or en dashes. Both banned word lists. "Truly" never. "Actually" at most once per article.
- **Never** write about another piece of writing ("Kain's post says").
- **Achology.com** written in full where the business is named.

## 5. Sources: the one hard rule

An idea is credited to a thinker only where their own work carries it. Every quotation is checked against the book and carries its page number. Every date and title on the family tree is a working record and is verified as you draft.

If you cannot open a book to check a line or find its page number, do not guess and do not paraphrase your way round it. Write the claim, mark it **UNVERIFIED** in the sourcing record, and say what would confirm it. Kain would rather read ten honest flags than one invented page number.

Known traps, from The Achology Stance: "between stimulus and response there is a space" is not Frankl's; "people are not disturbed by things" is Epictetus, quoted by Ellis. Do not use either as those people's words.

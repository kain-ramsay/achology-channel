How do you start practising mindfulness, and how often?

It's Sunday evening, and on Monday you told a friend you'd read through her CV before Friday. It's still sitting in your inbox, and when she messages to ask how it's going, you hear yourself typing that you've just been so busy, which is a reply you've given before. It's fine to wonder, with the phone still in your hand, whether busy is the whole reason.

If you've searched for how to practice mindfulness, the answer at Achology is that you start by looking at yourself. You ask one honest question about how you handled something today and why, and you come back to that question regularly, for a few moments each day. It needs no equipment and no special place, only a moment with your own thoughts.

How to practice mindfulness: what are you looking at?

Mindfulness, as we mean it, is being aware of yourself in the present moment: how you're conducting yourself, and the reasons behind it. It's more than being at peace, drifting off or switching your mind off, because you're paying attention to something particular, and that something is your own mind. The mind here means what you think, believe, feel and do, and it's a different thing from the brain, which is the physical organ.

The purpose is to understand yourself well enough to take more responsibility for your thoughts, feelings and actions. That's an invitation, and it's good news, and it isn't blame for anything that happened to you, only a question about what you do from here. It means examining your behaviour and your motives to discover what drives you and how you can develop.

How to practice mindfulness: what is a first question to ask yourself?

A feeling or an action rarely arrives out of nowhere. What you do and feel usually follows from what you were thinking, what you believed and how you read the moment, and looking back along that line is called backtracking. The first question is a short version of it: what did I do just now, and what was I telling myself when I did it?

Back with the CV, an answer might be that you kept putting it off because you were afraid she'd ask what you really thought of it. You're welcome to stop with that one reason for today. Say you dropped out of a weekly choir group instead, telling everyone you were too busy, and the reason you find is that you were embarrassed to be the weakest voice.

When an answer stings, you can stay with it for a moment and ask what you'd like to do about it. If the same reason turns up in more than one place, such as a fear of being judged behind both the CV and the choir, that's worth noticing. How is looking at yourself different from running on autopilot?

Autopilot is the automatic, unconscious way of thinking and acting that carries you through a day without your engaging with the choices you're making. You agree to host the family lunch again because you always have, or you say "sure, no problem" before you've checked whether it is one. Nothing in that moment feels like a decision, although the cost can be small, such as an afternoon you'd rather have kept, or larger, such as a commitment that runs for months.

Conscious awareness is the alternative: you pause and reflect before you act, and you evaluate the cost of your decisions before you make them. That's slower than a reflex, and it gives you the chance to choose on purpose, in line with what you value. With the family lunch, the evaluation can be as short as asking yourself what saying yes will involve this time.

Distraction makes autopilot easier to fall into. Phones, messages and the demands of other people pull your attention in several directions at once, and it can feel impossible to stay with one task or one person. If you notice it happening, you can ask what's pulling at your attention right now, and whether it's something you chose to attend to.

Which attitudes help you look at yourself?

How you look matters as well as what you ask. Non-judging means observing a situation without labelling it good or bad, so that "I put the CV off for a week" stays a plain description and doesn't become "I'm hopeless". Patience means accepting the pace of things without rushing, which suits the day when your question gets you nowhere and you can leave it until tomorrow. Trust means building confidence in yourself and in the habit of asking, and non-striving means letting go of the need for constant achievement.

A beginner's mind is a perspective of curiosity and openness, so you can ask about a habit you think you already understand, such as always saying yes to a certain person, as though for the first time. Humility and self-acceptance are two more attitudes to bring, and accepting yourself and other people plays a part in compassion. You could hold the delay over the CV with that kind of acceptance, noticing it without pushing it away.

Letting things be means allowing people and situations to unfold without interference. It applies to your friend's reaction to the late CV as well: you can say what happened and leave her to respond as she chooses. You can bring the same attitude to any moment when someone doesn't respond as you'd hoped.

How to practice mindfulness every day: how often, and how can you build it up?

The rhythm is a few moments each day, spent on what went well and what could have been done differently. There's no set number of minutes. You might choose a point in your day that already exists, such as when you shut your laptop at the end of work, and ask your question then.

Other teaching also comes back to regularity. The NHS page on mindfulness suggests picking a regular time, and Mind's page on mindfulness exercises and tips says: "Regular, short periods of mindfulness can work better than occasional long ones." Each of those pages goes on to describe its own way of practising, which you can read there.

One way to build it is to begin with the one question, then look back over your answers for reasons that repeat, then set where you are now beside where you'd like to be in one area of life, such as your work or a friendship. After that you can choose a decision that fits your longer-term aims over the one that feels easiest today. There's no set order or timing to keep, and if a day goes by without your question, you can pick it up the next evening, and nothing has to be made up.

What gets in the way, and how does it reach other people?

If you're wondering how to practice mindfulness when life is busy, you can keep the excuse "I've just been so busy" and add a question to it: busy with what, and what was I choosing over the thing I said I'd do? If you catch yourself being harsh on yourself, you can ask the question as you would ask a friend, wanting to understand more than to convict. It's fine to leave an answer unfinished and return to it the next day.

You can bring the same question to how you speak and how you listen. Before you reply, you can ask what's behind what you're about to say. When someone explains a problem to you, you can notice whether you're listening to understand or listening to answer, and you can say back what you heard before you add anything.

When is looking at yourself not enough?

Mindfulness practice is education and self-help, and for anything beyond that it's a health professional you'll want to talk to, such as your own doctor. If a low or worried mood stays for several weeks, or starts to keep you from sleeping well or from getting on with everyday life, please see a doctor or another qualified person nearby. If you or someone close to you may be in danger, please contact the emergency services where you live at once.

For a helpline near you, findahelpline.com lists services in more than 175 countries and says the organisations behind them verify their details with it directly. As a UK example, 999 is the number to call when someone is in immediate danger, NHS 111 is there for urgent advice that isn't an emergency, and Samaritans are on 116 123 all day and every day. In the US, as a further example, the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline takes phone calls and texts on 988.

What Makes a Good Teacher Beyond the Lesson Plan?

I sometimes wonder whether Gandhi or Mother Teresa, if either of them walked into a classroom, would have classroom management problems. Perhaps they would. Yet the thought draws all of us somewhere closer to home, to the kind of person we are when we walk into any situation. Teaching and coaching football ("soccer") were my work, so rooms full of young people were part of my days, and I've come to suspect that what makes a good teacher begins before the lesson plan does.

The plan is something we carry through the door, and someone has to be carrying it. I wonder how much of our time goes to the plan, and how much goes to the person who is walking in with it. That seems to me a good place to begin, before any technique or procedure enters the conversation.

The Person Who Walks Through the Door

Here are the questions I like to sit with, as a father and a teacher. Am I balanced, and am I full of well-placed confidence and self-esteem? Do I bring humility and a sense of purpose with me, and am I congruent, meaning that I live by my values and don't merely speak of them? None of these is a quick yes or no, and each one can be asked again on any ordinary morning, before a single young person has arrived.

When I think about presence, the names that come to my mind are Gandhi and Mother Teresa, and Nelson Mandela too. The 1979 Nobel Peace Prize went to Mother Teresa for her work in bringing help to suffering humanity, as the Nobel Prize organisation's own page about her confirms. I wonder what you'd notice first if any one of them walked into your own classroom or your own kitchen, whether it would be their stillness or the way they greet people, and whether there's one thing about them you'd enjoy practising tomorrow.

The List That Fills Every Minute

Consider everything that occupies a teacher's attention on an ordinary day. There is the forty-five minute class ahead, the attendance to be taken and the uniforms to be checked. There is homework to collect, and there are the attention and respect we hope to see from the students.

I wonder whether we're focused on what really matters, or only on the forty-five minutes in front of us. Are we watching the clock and the attendance sheet, or are we watching the people in the seats? Are we counting homework handed in, or are we thinking about what we hope for the children who hand it in? It seems to me that a list like that can fill every minute so completely that it leaves no room to ask who is holding it, and you may like to notice whether that's true of your own mornings.

A full list can feel like a full answer, and that's an easy thing to fall into. What might change in your day if you asked who is holding the list before you turned to anything on it? Whatever you find, I hope you'll meet it with some gentleness toward yourself.

What Really Matters in the Room

Ask yourself what really matters to you in a classroom, and then ask it again, more slowly this time. Many of us could give a quick answer, but how carefully have we thought it through? If the answer is that the young people should learn, then for what purpose do we hope they learn it, and what might stay with them long after the forty-five minutes have ended?

Those are considerable questions for such a small gap between two lessons, and nobody needs to resolve them in a single morning. They're offered as an invitation to keep thinking, nothing more, and you can begin with whichever one feels easiest to hold. Which would you pick first, and what makes it the easiest?

What Makes a Good Teacher Is Who Holds the Plan

Two conscientious teachers can hold exactly the same plan and walk into exactly the same room. The topic is identical, the forty-five minutes are identical, and the register is taken in just the same way. What might differ is the presence each of them brings. What does a room learn about us in its first minute, before a word of the plan has been spoken?

When we look honestly at what we want, I think we slowly come to want congruence between our values and our behaviour. That is where I'd locate what makes a good teacher. We walk into a classroom, or into an administrative meeting, with a little more presence, a little more Gandhi, a little more Mother Teresa and a little more Mandela. It isn't a matter of imitating their personalities, but of allowing our everyday behaviour to sit comfortably beside whatever we say we stand for.

What might that look like in your own week, whether you teach, coach, mentor or parent? Which of your values would a colleague or a friend name if they watched you for a day, and would you choose the same ones? If you'd like a short companion on the same thought, Why Your Character Shows Most When No One Watches approaches it from another side.

Five Questions Before You Open the Door

You can carry these into your next class, whether you teach in a school, coach on a pitch or simply care about a young person. Ask them quietly and answer them without any decoration, because the aim is to notice where you stand. There's nothing to win by answering impressively, and nothing to lose by answering candidly.

First, am I balanced today, or am I arriving in a rush? Second, is my confidence well placed, resting on something real, or is it a manner I put on at the door? Third, where is my humility, and could a student or a colleague see it? Each of these is a question and not a verdict, and you're the only person who can answer it.

Fourth, do I know my purpose for being in this room, and could I put it into plain words that a young person would follow? Fifth, do my behaviour and my values match when the room is noisy as well as when it's calm? Which of the five would you most like a trusted friend to ask you, and which would you prefer to leave for another day?

Before the Bell

Can anyone be a good teacher? I don't think a plain yes or no does justice to that question. What I'd say is that a good teacher is someone who keeps asking who is walking into the room, and who stays willing to look at the answer, however it reads on a given morning. That leaves the door open to all of us, and it leaves us with the work of asking.

What Do Students Really Want, and How Do You Find Out?

What do students really want? If you teach, coach, mentor or raise young people, you'll have heard the quick answer many times: to pass, to do well, to get a good job. It's a knee-jerk stock answer, and it's a perfectly reasonable and sensible one. I've come to wonder, gently, whether there might be something more underneath it, and whether we could find it together.

I was a teacher, and I was a football (soccer) coach. I never saw the possibilities, even though they were close at hand, in the eyes of those I worked beside. The real opportunity was in each individual person, past their abilities, their personalities and their roles, and I'd like to share what I've learned about looking, in the hope that it's of some comfort to you.

What Do Students Really Want Beyond Abilities, Personalities and Roles?

Abilities, personalities and roles are what we tend to notice first. A young person is quick or thoughtful, outgoing or quiet, in the starting line-up or on the bench, and each label is easy to read at a glance. Yet it isn't in any of them that the real opportunity sits, and it may be worth asking how it would feel to be seen as more than the list. You can probably remember a time when someone looked beyond yours and saw you more completely.

If you'd like to try something small, choose one person at your next lesson or practice session. Set the score, the marking and the plan to one side for a moment, and simply look at them, in the eyes. You may notice something there that no list of abilities ever told you. I can't say what it will be, because it's different for every person, and that's part of what makes it worth doing.

Hopes and Desires Nobody Has Figured Out Yet

Many of us aren't aware that we carry large desires within us. We haven't looked for them, discovered them or defined them, and that can be true at any age, even in the middle of a career or well beyond it. I suspect that if grown adults can reach that point without saying what they want, a young person may well not have found their own words yet either. That's only my reading, and your own experience may tell you something different.

So I wonder what a short or vague reply might be holding. It may hold nothing at all, and the student may mean exactly what they said. Or it may hold a hope that hasn't been named yet, even to the person who carries it, and then the reply is a doorway and not a wall. If that's possible, there may be room for us to treat a vague answer with a little patience, as the start of a conversation.

Think, too, of a hope that somebody noticed in you before you'd noticed it yourself. Who was that, and what did they do that let you hear it? It may have been a question, or a quiet moment, or simply their full and unhurried attention. Those memories can be a guide to what we might offer a young person in return.

Nobody Is Too Far Along to Begin Asking

It's never too late to start looking into someone's eyes and asking what they really want. That's true for a student, for a colleague and for ourselves, because a person can be mid-career, or older, and still be working out what they're here for. There's something reassuring in that, since it means nobody has been left behind by the question, and nobody needs to feel embarrassed that they never answered it years ago.

It may help to begin with ourselves. I wonder how well any of us can look into another person's eyes if we've never looked into our own with kindness and purpose. There's no hurry about it, and no correct answer waiting at the end of it. You might simply give the question an unhurried afternoon, with nobody expecting a result, and notice what gradually comes to the surface, which may be something quite unexpected and entirely your own.

Seeing Possibilities Before They Have Names

A possibility is a delicate thing to recognise, because it doesn't arrive with a label attached, and the person who carries it may not understand it either. When we look at a young person with real attention, what might we be hoping to notice? It may be a flash of interest that comes and goes within a moment, or a way of speaking about something that changes the whole face of the person talking. Nobody can tell you in advance what it will look like, which is precisely why it asks for your gentle attention and not a checklist.

How much of a school day or a training session is arranged around things that can be measured, such as results, scores and finishing times? Those matter, and I wouldn't want to wave them away. What room is there, in the same day, for a conversation about what a person hopes for, and who makes that room? It might be you, and it might take only a few kind minutes of genuine, unhurried conversation, offered without any agenda at all.

Finding Out, One Person at a Time

I'd put it this way: the answer to what do students really want lives in particular people, one at a time, and no general list can hold it. That makes asking a lovely first step, and listening the quieter second one. Justin Minkel, in an Education Week column, describes asking students and families what they need and then acting on what he hears. His column is about what students need from school, and the question here is what a person wants for their own life, but both begin with someone who's willing to ask with care.

Two other articles sit comfortably beside this one. Listening Is Not Waiting to Speak: The Discipline of Tuning In looks at the listening that comes after the asking, and Hope Does Real Work: Turning Possibilities Into Commitments picks up where a named hope can go next. When a young person tells you something they haven't said before, what will they find on your face? I hope it's welcome.

The same kindness can reach colleagues, parents and fellow mentors, who are people with unnamed hopes of their own. You might ask one of them what they really want from their work, and then wait for the reply without finishing their sentence. It costs only a few minutes, and it asks nothing of you except a willingness to look at the individual and not at the position they occupy.

A Few Questions to Keep

Here are three questions you might take into the coming week, only if they're welcome. Who around you have you been seeing mostly as a list of abilities, and what might you ask them if you had no plan for the answer? Which of your own hopes have you never put into words, and how would it feel to be asked about them by someone who then waited? Who might be quietly hoping you'll ask?

Why Does the Character of a Good Teacher Matter More Than Method?

What is the character of a good teacher, and how does it sit beside method? If you teach, or coach, or mentor, or simply walk alongside young people, I'd gently invite you to set aside a question many of us ask without noticing, which is whether a person has character, yes or no. Character isn't a yes or no thing, because we all have it, and the kinder question to ask is how much.

Picture an open biography on a teacher's desk, with a pen lying beside it. Somebody has been reading about a life, and somebody is about to write something down. Method is easy to picture in a teacher's hands, with its plans, its timings and its routines, while character is harder to picture, yet the biography is one place to look for it, since that is where we meet the lives of people who had a great deal of it.

The Character of a Good Teacher Is a Matter of How Much

When a lesson is over, we tend to ask whether it was planned well, whether the room was managed, and whether the work came back. Those are sensible questions about method, and they deserve a proper answer. Beside them sits a gentler one, which is how much of a person was there in front of the class.

If character comes in amounts, then nobody is left out of the conversation. A teacher isn't sorted into those who have it and those who don't, since each of us is somewhere along the way, and each of us is free to look at where. Think about where you'd place yourself on an ordinary Tuesday, and what would help you move a little further along.

What Can't Be Bought

We hear plenty of promises that if we pay for this education or that course, we will arrive somewhere worthwhile. Yet we can't buy what we need most, because we have to earn it. Nobody is handed character for doing a good deed, or for following what society expects of education and hard work, and there is no automatic right of passage that delivers it to us in return for good behaviour.

To my mind, method is the part of teaching that a course can hand over, and character is the part we earn. Whether one matters more than the other is something I'd rather offer to you as a question than settle on your behalf. If character can't be bought, where might it be earned, and is there someone whose character you admire, whose story you would love to hear?

When Someone Disrespects You, or Cuts You Off

Character is not losing it when someone disrespects you, or cuts you off on the street. Imagine a student who answers back in a tone that you didn't expect. The caring question isn't whether you have patience or don't, but how much of it you have with you at that moment.

Character is also not being inflexible when your values are questioned. A colleague or a parent might ask, "Why do you do it that way?" It may be worth wondering whether you could hear that as an honest enquiry and not as an attack.

Learning the Amount From Your Own Failures

Character is something we learn through our own experiences and failures, which means our own failures are part of what teaches us. Think of a day when you lost your temper, or held something tightly that you could have held more loosely. Nothing about that day has to be a burden, and it may be that it can show you how much character you had with you.

A failure stings, and I understand why we'd like to hurry past it. But if failures are among the places where character is learned, then they are worth looking at with care. A page on this site, Why We Can Grow In Intellect Without Growing In Character, takes up the gap between knowing more and behaving better. Is there a failure of your own that you've never quite looked at, and might you be willing to read it the way you'd read a chapter of someone else's life?

Finding Your Own Amount

If character is a matter of how much, it's natural to wonder how any of us would see our own amount. I don't have a scale to offer you, and I'm not sure a number would help anyone. But a few gentle questions can point in a direction, and they cost nothing to try: when did you last stay steady in a moment that might have knocked you off balance, and what were you telling yourself while it was happening?

Who in your working life has seen you on a difficult day, and what might they say about the amount of character you brought? Questions like these don't need a quick response, and they're easier to answer honestly when you give yourself a few days to sit with them, and there is no hurry to reply.

Are You Waiting for the Compliments?

If you have to tell someone you are an expert, what does that say about you? It's a short question, and it can be a tender one to sit with, particularly for people who care a great deal about doing their work well and being recognised for it. Are you waiting for the compliments to arrive, or are you relaxed in yourself?

Try asking it about an ordinary working week. Who do you hope will notice what you did, and what would you do differently if nobody ever did? No answer is needed out loud, or today, since some questions do their work simply by being asked.

What the Biographies Show

The other school is the lives of great men and women of the past and present, which we meet in autobiographies, in biographies and in texts of their own. Read several, and a common thread of character appears, because we can see the suffering, the sacrifice and the doggedness that it takes to rise to our greatest heights. We can also see that we aren't alone, and we can see what people received from choosing the path less travelled.

Take one example, and keep to the facts. The Nobel Prize committee's facts page on Nelson Mandela gives the prize motivation, "for their work for the peaceful termination of the apartheid regime", and records that from 1964 to 1982 he was confined to the prison island Robben Island. When you read a life like that, you may find yourself noticing the cost first, or what it gave, and either way it can be a quiet gift to ask which of the two you would most like to understand better.

A Few Quiet Minutes

You don't need a long retreat for any of this, only a few honest minutes, somewhere comfortable to write and a willingness to be patient with your own answers. You might ask yourself how much of the character of a good teacher you brought into your last difficult hour, and what you would like to bring a little more of next time. Ask, too, whose life, near or far, is worth reading beside your own, and what one small thing you might do tomorrow before the lesson begins.

These questions keep well, so you can carry them into next week as easily as into tomorrow, and some of them may take longer than a few minutes, which is perfectly allowed. How Do You Keep Your Purpose as a Teacher?

How do you keep your purpose as a teacher when the days fill up with lessons, marking and meetings? If you work with young people in any way, you probably began with a reason that mattered a great deal to you, and you may sometimes wonder whether you could still express that reason in words today. I'd like to offer a gentle way of looking at it, because hard work cannot hold a purpose in place on its own. Please treat the questions below as invitations to look kindly at your own working week, and to try one small thing tomorrow before the first lesson begins.

Hard Work Is the Minimum

Hard work matters enormously, and I'd never say a word against it. Teachers contribute a great deal to their classes, their colleagues, their families and their communities, and all of that effort is good and needed. Even so, I've come to see it as the minimum, and I'm sorry if that stings. It is the outer layer of a working life, the icing on the cake, and a cake made only of icing doesn't feed anyone for long.

We are often told that the effort we pour into the team, the job and the family is the only thing that counts. Does that sound familiar, and is it what you've come to believe about your own days? What would change in your working life if hard work were regarded as the starting line and not the finish, and what do you hope all that giving is for?

Purpose as a Teacher Begins With Time on Yourself

The part that pays most, as I see it, is the time you spend on yourself and with yourself. It sits outside the register, the lesson plan and the staff meeting, which makes it the first casualty of a crowded week, yet it's what allows you to give more to your family, your students and your colleagues, and even more to the person who serves you coffee. It isn't something you do once and tick off a list, and it has to find a place in your week. Where does that time already exist in your own week, and where has it been squeezed out by everything else?

Nobody can sell you this, and it has to be earned, which happens gradually through the time you give yourself. You might like to sit with a gentle question there: if nobody were watching my week, what sort of teacher would I be? It is worth asking with maturity and with genuine kindness, without harsh self-criticism and without a tidy platitude to cover the answer.

Writing Down Your Mission and Vision

As I'd put it, a goal tells you what to do next, and a mission tells you why it matters. Have you written yours down? I don't mean a goal for this term, I mean a mission and a vision for who you hope to become as a teacher, for the sake of the young people who will meet you along the way.

Once they're on paper, every goal you set can be checked against them, by asking whether it fits the person you're growing into or is only another box to tick, and whether writing your mission down might change the first question of a busy week from what needs doing to which of these tasks belongs to the person you've described. Vision, mission and purpose are what motivate people, and it isn't the monthly pay cheque, needed as that is. If you want to keep your purpose as a teacher, it makes sense to feed it directly, and not to leave it to pay and praise to do the job.

Starting Small and Comparing Yourself With Yesterday

Significant personal change rarely arrives in one dramatic moment. It is built from many small moments that are added to each day, and that's why I would begin gently and start with something small and manageable. Choose one new habit and one modest process, and put them in place before you ask anything more of yourself, because a plan that's easy to begin is a plan you're more likely to meet again tomorrow. What would a small habit look like if it were designed to serve your purpose as a teacher?

Then compare yourself with the teacher you were yesterday. There's no need to measure against a colleague down the corridor, or against some socially acceptable version of yourself that you believe you ought to be. What did you do yesterday that you could do a little better today? It's a kinder comparison, and you can make it privately, without anyone else in the room.

Consistency and Keeping Track

Consistency is the quiet partner of every small habit, and it deserves more attention than it usually receives in a busy school year. You might ask yourself how consistent you've been, and how you would know. How will you measure your progress, and how will you know you can trust what it shows?

Keeping track of it makes progress visible in a way that a good intention alone cannot. Will it be a line in a notebook, a mark on a calendar, or a brief note on your phone? Whatever you choose, it should be simple enough that you'll still use it during a very busy week, because a record that takes great effort to keep is easy to abandon. What would you like that record to show a month from now, and what would you be glad to discover in it?

When the Plan Slips

You will miss days, the habit will slip and the plan will wobble, so a week may well pass with nothing ticked off at all. What do you say to yourself at that moment? Some of us berate ourselves, and some of us decide the answer is a big, impressive effort that everyone will notice. Is the failure telling you that your purpose was never genuine, or is it telling you that the step you chose was too large?

There is another approach, which is to take the failure and divide it into something smaller and more manageable. If ten minutes was too much, what about two, and if the whole plan was too much, what's the one part of it that you can do today? It's a quieter way to go on, and because it asks for no audience, it's easy to skip past, so be kind to yourself when you do.

A Small Action for Tomorrow

Which small thing could you do every day, starting tomorrow, that would set this in motion? Keep it so small and so ordinary that refusing it would be difficult, and choose something you could accomplish even on an exhausting day, because a modest beginning is easier to repeat. It might be five minutes with your mission, one gentle question put to yourself before the first lesson, or a line in the notebook when the day is done. Start tomorrow with the smallest version of your action, and let that be enough for one day.

How Can a Coach Build Character in Sports, Not Just Win Games?

How do you build character in sports when so much of the attention around the pitch goes to the scoreboard? I was a football coach, and for a long time I gave the scoreboard my attention as well, without pausing to consider what else was waiting to be noticed. Picture a training pitch at dusk, with the cones still out and a few bags left beside the fence, and a coach gently gathering the whole group before everybody goes home. The possibilities were right in front of me, in the eyes of the people I was with, and I never saw them.

I can't hand you a method, and I wouldn't feel comfortable trying. What I can do is describe what I overlooked and offer some quiet questions, the ones I'd hope somebody would offer me. They're invitations, and you're welcome to take as long with them as you like, because few of us answer them satisfactorily on the first attempt, and I'm no exception.

What I Was Watching Instead

Here's what occupied my attention as a coach: the winning, the championships, the disappointments and the practice sessions. It also included the bonding of the team. Those things matter, and I wouldn't pretend otherwise, but they weren't where the real opportunity was hiding. Would I have recognised that opportunity, had someone pointed toward it during a practice session or a moment of team bonding?

The opportunity sat in each individual, beyond their abilities, beyond their personalities and beyond the particular role they filled for the team. On a team sheet a player is a winger or a keeper or a captain, which is a label that tells you where to stand and very little about the person standing there. I'd like to imagine who that person is when the label is taken away. I didn't ask, and I didn't look, and that is the simplest way I know to describe what I missed.

Some of what sits behind the label is hopes and desires the person hasn't yet figured out. Might a young player you coach be wondering what they want from the game, or from the years beyond it? Perhaps it would be kind to ask them, with enough patience to hear something beyond the stock answer. Perhaps that is a place to start, before any conversation about the next fixture, the next championship or the next disappointment.

Build Character in Sports by Being It First

If you want to build character in sports, I'd begin with the person holding the whistle. Which values would you like your players to carry away from your sessions? The next step is to ask whether they're values you demonstrate in front of those players, and I don't mean in a speech, or even in what you do on match day. I mean values lived in front of them, through who you are, not spoken about at them.

The way I'd put it, a player can hear a talk about respect in a single minute, yet it's the coach's manner, week after week, that shows him what respect looks like. That's my own reading of the situation, and I hold it loosely. What I can offer is the test I'd set myself: would these be good values to live in front of my players, through who I am and not only through my words and actions?

A few more gentle questions follow from that, and I'd ask them of myself before I asked them of anyone else. How consistent am I able to be, and how committed am I to the values I say I hold? What would progress look like for me, and could I compare myself with who I was yesterday, rather than with the coach down the road?

Who Is Silently Watching From the Sideline

There's a thought I keep returning to: who are you already bringing along with you, silently observing and copying everything you do? On a football pitch it's easy to picture the answer. I suspect the players watch their coach more closely than we realise, and nobody announces that they're doing it or asks your permission beforehand.

If they are copying you, what might they be copying, and have you had a chance to wonder about it? Is it the words you use in the team talk, or what you do when you believe the session has finished? Is it the way you speak to the player who made the mistake, to the referee, or to the coach on the opposite side of the pitch?

Those questions are worth a few unhurried minutes before a match, and again afterwards, when the excitement has settled and the bags are back beside the fence. What would you be glad for them to imitate, and what would you be relieved to find had gone unnoticed? Ask it with kindness, and let the answers be as uncomplicated and as unhurried as they are. Might an honest answer be the first step in learning to build character in sports?

One Small Thing Before the Next Session

It's rarely one dramatic moment that changes how we are; it's the many small and consistent moments that we can add to each ordinary day, which is an encouraging possibility for any coach with limited time. There's no shortage of opportunities on the way to a training ground, in the car park, at the first whistle or at the last. What's one small action you could take each day that would get you started, and how might you keep track of it, so that the progress stays visible to you?

If you're unsure which values you'd want to live in front of your players, How Do You Find Your Core Values? is a good first page to read. You might write a short list and read it over before the next session. Then wonder, with no harshness at all, how each one appeared in your behaviour this week, who was there to observe it, and what they might have taken from it.

Winning and the Question I'd Leave You With

What is the winning for? It's a question I'd offer to any coach, myself included, and I wonder whether the championships and the disappointments pass while the people remain. Can a team win and still leave its players unseen, or lose a match and leave every one of them feeling that somebody noticed them as a person? I don't have neat answers to that, and I'd rather you explored it in your own time than borrowed mine.

If the real opportunity was in the eyes of each person on the pitch, then a coach's first responsibility may be to look up from the clipboard. What might you notice in the next half hour of a session if you did? And what might you learn about one quiet player over a month of sessions, if you kept looking even when the league table tugged your attention back down?

What Is the Purpose of a Teacher? Why Every Educator Should Ask "For What Purpose?"

What is the purpose of a teacher? Most of us who teach, or who help young people in some other way, carry an answer close to the heart, and it sounds something like this: I want to be an educator, to help the kids develop. It's a good answer and a generous one, and it comes to us so readily. It seems to me that there's an invitation waiting just behind it.

The invitation is three words long: for what purpose? I shorten it to FWP, and I offer it as a kind question, one that can rest beside a plan, a habit or a whole career without asking anyone to defend themselves. In these pages I'd like to offer it first to the work itself, and then to the person doing it.

The Answer That Comes So Readily

What might you hear if you asked a room of people who care for young people about the purpose of a teacher? Probably something about helping, inspiring, making a difference and preparing children for adult life. None of these needs correcting. They're simply a good place to begin, and there may be more waiting underneath.

How much time have we been able to give that answer? Most of us are busy, and a ready reply to a significant question about ourselves is entirely natural and hardly a fault. So the aim isn't to discard it or to be embarrassed by it. The aim is to sit beside it for a while and discover what lies underneath.

Offering the Question to Your Own Role

Think about the different responsibilities you carry in a single week: explaining, setting assignments, marking, maintaining order, listening, encouraging. Now take one of them, and offer it the three words. Why do I mark in the way I do, why do I explain it in that order, and for what purpose do I keep the room the way I keep it?

Some of the explanations will come easily, and some, after a few honest attempts, won't come at all. Please don't worry about the second kind. A part of the role that has no purpose attached may simply be something we do out of habit or because it's expected, and you can choose to keep it anyway, now knowingly and with a purpose of your own.

Encyclopaedia Britannica's entry on the Socratic method notes that the name is now used for any educational strategy in which the teacher cross-examines the students. Questioning is familiar ground for anyone who works with learners. What I'm describing is softer and points the other way, because the one being asked is the teacher, and the asking is done with care.

Why the Purpose of a Teacher Starts in the Mirror

Begin with something ordinary. Picture yourself at a mirror, looking into your own eyes, and asking the question there, quietly, in your own time: for what purpose do I want to be an educator, and what lasting effect would I like to have on the children? It can feel a little exposed to ask it, since nobody else is in the room and there's no one to whom you need to hand a polished answer. That's precisely why the mirror is a good place for it.

The question can be shared with a colleague or a student one day, of course, but there's something good in asking it of ourselves first. If I've given my own reasons a little patient attention, I'm better prepared to give the same patience to the people beside me. You're not cross-examining yourself here, you're simply getting to know the person who'll be standing at the front of the room.

Ask it without an audience, and watch your own replies with interest. Which ones arrive immediately, and which take a little longer to appear? Neither kind needs to be judged, and both deserve a fair hearing before you decide which of them to build on.

Develop What, and How?

The word develop is where the ready answer likes to rest. What would you love to develop in the young people in front of you: knowledge, intellectual independence, confidence, curiosity, consideration for others, or something you haven't yet put a name to? Each one leads to a different way of spending a lesson, which is precisely why it's worth giving the question some unhurried consideration.

Then comes how, which is the part we skip most easily, because the first part already felt like an answer. If I say I want to develop confidence in children, what might I do a little differently on Monday morning? If the honest answer is nothing yet, the purpose hasn't become a plan, and that isn't a failure, only a soft signal to ask again and go one layer deeper.

Notice, too, the phrase lasting effect. What would you like a young person to carry out of your classroom that outlasts the term, the year and the results? There may be no tidy answer to that, and the asking is worthwhile even when the answer stays unfinished, since it turns a general kindness into something specific that you can hold in your hands.

Three Questions About the Purpose of a Teacher

It can help to keep the inquiry small and friendly, and three questions are enough to begin. Which part of my week could I explain in a single sentence of purpose, and which part might I find harder to explain? If a student were asked what my lessons are for, what might they say, and would I recognise it as mine? Is there an answer of mine I'd feel shy about saying aloud to a colleague, and what might that shyness be telling me?

Anyone who wants to look at the values beneath their answers can start with What Are Your Core Values? and then return to the three questions, none of which has a correct reply or is a test you can fail. They simply make the ready answer specific enough to be held and looked at, which is all a first round of asking can do. Write the replies down if that suits you.

What a Purpose Changes in a Lesson

When you hold a purpose you can say in a sentence, each part of your teaching can be checked against it. Why this routine, why this way of answering a child who interrupts, and why this way of ending a lesson? If a part has no answer yet, it's a candidate for a second look, and if it does have one, you may find you can say it more simply than you expected.

None of this has to be settled in a week, or in a term, or even in a year. The first round of the question is the most valuable one, because it moves you from a borrowed sentence to your own, and the later rounds tend to be quieter and more specific. They're yours to take at whatever pace suits you, and nobody is evaluating them.

One Question on the Board

Imagine writing one question on the board before anyone arrives: for what purpose? It isn't for the class to answer, because it's meant for you alone, like a small kindness you leave for yourself. Write your first answer beneath it, then the next three you can find, and leave the board as it is until the last of them sounds like a description of the young people you care about.

Question 13 · keyword: who is person centred therapy for · about 1,166 words

Who is person-centred counselling for, and when is it used?

It's late, and you're lying awake going over the same question. Should you go back to study at 40, or stay in a job that pays well but has stopped interesting you? Someone has suggested talking it through with a counsellor, and you wonder whether your situation is serious enough, or whether counselling is meant for people with bigger troubles than a choice about the future. Plenty of people stop here, because nobody has told them who the approach is meant for.

So, who is person centred therapy for? It's for people who want to be listened to and to work out their own thinking, and who are ready to do most of the talking and deciding themselves. The counsellor's job is to listen closely and not to advise. Some conversations suit it much better than others, and it makes real demands of the person as well as the counsellor.

Who is person centred therapy for, in everyday terms?

In the US, for example, the American Counseling Association's definition of counseling in general describes a professional relationship that helps individuals, families and groups work towards goals in areas that include mental health, wellness, education and career. That covers counseling as a whole and says nothing yet about this approach in particular. In the UK, for example, BACP (which stands for the British Association for Counselling and Psychotherapy) places person-centred counselling among the humanistic approaches and says Carl Rogers founded it in the 1940s.

What the person-centred description adds is who leads. BACP describes the counselling as "led by you and not directed by the counsellor", and gives its aim as helping you "become more self-aware and achieve greater independence". That suits someone who wants to be heard without being steered. It also suits someone who wants to think a choice through aloud, or to understand why they react as they do.

Take the question of going back to study at 40. You may not want anyone to rule on whether it's sensible, but you may want room to say aloud what draws you to it, what worries you and whose opinion you keep hearing in your head. That's a conversation about understanding yourself and making a choice, and for Rogers's own account of that aim you can read What is person-centred therapy trying to achieve, and why? for a fuller picture.

When person-centred counselling is used, and what it asks of you

Read against those descriptions, it's used wherever talking is the help a person wants. That includes a decision to think through, a change of direction that hasn't settled yet, a wish to be listened to without advice, or a wish to know yourself better. Because the person leads, the work asks something of you as well as the counsellor, and Rogers's 1946 paper Significant Aspects of Client-Centered Therapy lists six conditions. The first is that the counsellor holds to the principle that "the individual is basically responsible for himself, and is willing for the individual to keep that responsibility".

That condition means the direction and the decisions stay with you. It says nothing about who is to blame for what has happened to you. His fourth condition is that the limits set are "simple limits set on behavior, and not limits set on attitudes", and he adds that an adult client may not be allowed more than an hour, but there is "full acceptance of his desire to claim more time". In Achology's reading, you might say in one breath that you'd love to go back and that it would be foolish, and both would be received as things you feel.

How long person-centred counselling lasts

No single length applies to person-centred counselling, and Rogers's own text shows why. In the same 1946 paper, one client, unhappy in a dormitory, saw something of her own dependence within three interviews. Another worked through deep insights over 30 interviews. He calls it the same process in both, but they were cases from his own practice, so they're illustrations and not a standard length, an average or a research finding.

Among the things he says the counsellor relies on is that "the client is the best judge as to the most desirable frequency of interviews", so how often to meet was left to the person. In the UK, for example, BACP says humanistic therapy as a whole, the family that includes person-centred counselling, "may be short or long-term, depending on your needs".

Where it isn't enough, and when to get proper help

Rogers's own wording marks a limit: he says the experience he describes will happen "in most cases" when his conditions are present. One of them is that the counsellor uses "only those procedures and techniques" that convey understanding and acceptance. Read that way, a question that needs a verdict, such as whether a course is worth its cost, belongs with someone whose job is to give one. For a fuller account of the skills that convey understanding and acceptance, read person centred therapy techniques.

Results are a separate question from who it suits, and Does person-centred counselling work? gives a fuller account of the research. Reading about counselling isn't the same as being supported by a trained person, so if low spirits or worry have lasted for weeks, or they're making it hard to sleep, to work or to get on with the people around you, please see a doctor or another qualified professional. Do the same at any time you feel that you or somebody else may be in danger.

If it's an emergency, or you want to speak to someone right now, the directory at findahelpline.com covers verified helplines in more than 130 countries. In the UK, for example, you can ring 999 in an emergency, call NHS 111 when you need urgent medical help, or phone Samaritans on 116 123, which is free at any hour. In the US, the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline can be reached by call or text on 988.

Question 14 · keyword: cbt and person centered therapy · about 1,201 words

Can you combine CBT and person-centred counselling?

You've been asked to lead a small project at work, and for three evenings you've gone back and forth over whether to say yes. A friend says the best help she ever had came from someone who listened until she knew her own mind, and a colleague swears by writing the worry down and doing something small about it. You begin to wonder whether CBT and person centered therapy are rival ways of getting help or ones you could use together.

Yes, CBT and person centered therapy can be combined. The international Society for the Exploration of Psychotherapy Integration describes psychotherapies that "integrate theoretical orientations". It doesn't name either one, and the page that names both is a UK one, from BACP, covered below.

How CBT and person centered therapy fit together in practice

The sources closest to each approach describe the helper's part in their own words, and they don't say the same thing. In his 1946 paper Significant Aspects of Client-Centered Therapy, Carl Rogers described a relationship "permeated by warmth, understanding, safety". The Beck Institute's page Understanding CBT says CBT is based on Dr Aaron T. Beck's Cognitive Model, and lists "a sound therapeutic relationship" among its tenets.

In the UK, for example, the British Association for Counselling and Psychotherapy, known as BACP, describes integrative counselling as drawing on techniques from different types of therapy. Its page says the integrative approach "may include elements of person-centred therapy" and that the therapist might bring in elements of CBT. BACP describes an integrative counsellor as someone who "believes there isn't just one therapeutic approach that can help a client in all situations".

One project decision, heard two ways

Return to the invitation to lead the project. Rogers's 1946 paper states the view that, on the way into a person's deeper concerns, the client can be a more efficient guide than the therapist. For example, a listener who shares that view might reflect back what you've said, perhaps "part of you wants the chance and part of you is afraid of being found out", and then leave room for you to answer. You would then decide for yourself what to do with what you'd heard.

Now suppose the conversation turns to making a plan, which is a step CBT includes. The Beck Institute lists among its tenets that CBT "includes action plans (therapy homework)". For example, you might write down the thought, "I'll be found out", say what you want from the project, and agree to lead only the first meeting and note what happens.

In this example the listening comes first and the plan follows. Rogers described a client who senses that their words are "understood rather than evaluated" as one who then speaks more openly. Neither Rogers nor the Beck Institute says the two steps should come in that order. By the end of the second step you'd have something small to try on Monday.

Where the two differ, and where motivational interviewing sits

Rogers's 1946 paper describes a client who, seeing themselves more clearly, chooses new goals, and the Beck Institute says CBT sessions are structured and include action plans. Suppose two weeks later you ask what to say in the first meeting. One listener might ask what you want to say and let you decide, and another might agree a plan with you before you leave. Neither source describes these scenes, and they're only examples.

Motivational interviewing comes up in the same searches, so its own site is the place to look. The page Understanding Motivational Interviewing calls it "a guiding style of communication, that sits between following (good listening) and directing", citing Miller and Rollnick. So that page presents it as its own style and doesn't describe it as person-centred therapy. Its references do include a 2017 article by Miller and Moyers, Motivational Interviewing and the clinical science of Carl Rogers.

When combining goes wrong

A mix can go badly when a method is used at a moment that doesn't suit it, or to dodge a harder moment, and then the other person is left without what they came for. Take two scenes. Suppose a learner reaches for an action plan whenever someone's feelings make things awkward: that person is left with a plan and no chance to say what they feel. Suppose another learner keeps reflecting feelings back to someone who has asked for a practical step: that person is left with no step to take.

Rogers wrote that client-centered counseling "cannot be a trick or a tool" if it is to be effective. His words are about client-centered counseling only. Read beside the first scene, they could be taken as a reminder that a technique is there to help the person in front of you, but that is a reading, not something Rogers wrote.

Suppose one learner has only tried listening and stays quiet when a friend asks for a suggestion, while another has only tried checking and asks for evidence after every sad remark. The friend who wanted a suggestion gets silence, and the friend who wanted to be heard gets questions instead.

Reading about these approaches isn't the same as receiving help from a trained person, so if distress has lasted for weeks and is disturbing your sleep or daily life, or if anyone may be unsafe, please speak to a doctor or a qualified professional. Wherever you live, you can find verified helplines for your own country at findahelpline.com, which covers more than 130 countries, and numbers differ from country to country. In the UK, for example, 999 is for emergencies and NHS 111, by phone or online, directs you to medical help. The Samaritans answer free on 116 123, and in the US, for example, the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline answers calls and texts.

Question 15 · keyword: why do counsellors need theory · about 1,095 words

Why do counsellors need theory, and their own counselling?

A colleague tells you over lunch that she's leaving a secure job to start her own business, and something in you tightens. Before she's finished explaining, you're already thinking that it's a risky choice and that you'd never make it yourself. You haven't said a word of this aloud, but it's shaping how you listen.

That small moment holds most of the answer to why do counsellors need theory, and why they're also asked to look at their own lives. Theory gives a helper a reason to hear her explanation out before judging it, and self-knowledge is what lets the helper notice that a judgement has risen at all. Some counselling bodies name grounding in theory in their published standards. Others name personal development or self-examination, each in its own words.

Why do counsellors need theory if caring is enough?

A theory, in this sense, is a clear account of how people work and how they change, and for a helper it does three jobs. It gives a way to make sense of the person in front of you, it supplies a reason for what you say or leave unsaid, and it gives you something to check your own instincts against. Many people picture this as a map, which shows you the ground so that you aren't guessing, though a map is never the ground itself.

Counselling bodies say this in their own ways, and two examples show it. In Canada, the Canadian Counselling and Psychotherapy Association publishes Standards of Practice. They say that any intervention offered to a client "should be grounded in an established theory or have a supporting research base". In the United States, the American Counseling Association's Code of Ethics asks counselors to use techniques that are "grounded in theory and/or have an empirical or scientific foundation" (standard C.7.a).

Carl Rogers put it in his own terms in 1946. In Significant Aspects of Client-Centered Therapy he wrote that the client-centred therapist builds his procedures on hypotheses about the constructive forces in a person. He called these "rapidly becoming established as facts", but that was his own view and not a research result. You'll find the fuller theory in What Are the Foundational Principles of Person-Centred Counselling?.

Return to your colleague. If you hold the view Rogers described, that a person can find her own direction when she's heard well, you have a reason to ask her what a good first year would look like to her. You can then listen to the answer before you say what you think. Without a view like that, your own judgement is the only guide you have to her plan.

Why are helpers asked to look at their own lives?

In the UK, BACP's Ethical Framework, adopted in 2018, says practitioners should accept that "we are all vulnerable to prejudice". It asks them to value "self-inquiry, personal feedback and professional development" (paragraph 22c). It also says that supervision "is essential to how practitioners sustain good practice throughout their working life".

BACP's training page lists self-awareness among what tutors look for, and it describes self-awareness as "your understanding of yourself, your thoughts and values". The same page says that "some courses expect students to be in therapy themselves". In New Zealand, the Association of Counsellors' Code of Ethics says something similar about supervision. It says that a counsellor and supervisor share the job of noticing when the counsellor needs counselling for personal concerns.

Back at the lunch table, this is where self-knowledge does its own separate work. Theory gave you a reason to ask about her plan, but only a look at your own habits can tell you why your judgement rose so quickly, perhaps because security matters a great deal to you. Noticing that doesn't mean anything is wrong with you, since everyone's judgements come from their own values, and few people stop to look at where they came from.

Where theory and self-knowledge reach their limits, and when to get help

So why do counsellors need theory, if no theory describes every person? A theory describes people in general, and your colleague is one particular person. A helper who holds a theory too tightly can end up listening for what it predicts and missing what's actually said, so a theory works best as a guide you keep checking against the person.

Self-knowledge has limits too, since knowing yourself isn't the same as being trained. Looking at your own habits is where the work begins. BACP's training page says that trainees spend a lot of time on "independent study, placements, supervision and, in some cases, personal therapy".

Wanting to know yourself better is healthy and isn't a sign that something is wrong. If distress has stayed with you for weeks, though, or it's making daily life, rest or close relationships harder, talking with a doctor or another qualified professional is a better place to start than reading.

If you or someone near you may be in danger, reach out for help straight away, and verified helplines for more than 130 countries are gathered at findahelpline.com. As examples, in the UK you can call 999 in an emergency. NHS 111 helps with an urgent medical need, and Samaritans are open at any hour on 116 123. In the US you can call, text or chat with the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline.

Question 21 · keyword: conditions of worth · about 993 words

What are conditions of worth in person-centred counselling?

Someone asks whether you'd mind if the plans changed, and you say you don't mind at all, even though you did mind a little. You spend the rest of the evening on the sofa feeling faintly guilty for having wanted anything, and that's a puzzling thing to feel, because nothing bad happened, yet the guilt is real and lasts longer than the moment that set it off.

The idea behind that feeling has a name in person-centred counselling: conditions of worth. A condition of worth is a belief, taken in from other people over the years, that you're only acceptable if you think, feel or act in certain ways. Carl Rogers set the idea out in 1959. The approach sets against it unconditional positive regard, which is regard with no strings attached.

What conditions of worth look like in daily life

Most of these beliefs are picked up early, and they come from small daily moments and not from anything dramatic. A child who notices that a parent's face softens when they're helpful and tightens when they ask for something can decide, without anyone saying a word, that being easy to have around is what earns warmth. Teachers, friends and whole cultures teach the same kind of lesson in their own ways. The people involved were usually passing on what they'd been taught themselves, so nobody needs to be blamed for it.

Over time the outside voice becomes your own, so a sentence such as "I'm only fine if I'm no trouble" stops sounding like something other people said and starts sounding like a plain fact about you. That's why the guilt in the opening moment can arrive so quickly and feel so fair. When that has happened, wanting the plans to change can feel like a small failing and not like a normal wish.

How conditions of worth connect to unconditional positive regard

Simply Psychology cites Rogers's 1959 chapter, "A theory of therapy, personality, and interpersonal relationships as developed in the client-centered framework", as part of volume 3 of Psychology: A Study of a Science. Its page prints his definition as beginning: "A condition of worth arises when the positive regard of a significant other is conditional". The University of Vienna lists this among Rogers's six conditions for therapy: "That the therapist is experiencing unconditional positive regard toward the client."

The article on the foundational principles of person-centred counselling explains how the approach sees the self, and it's a good place to start. The piece on person-centred therapy techniques shows what that regard looks like when you're listening to someone. A 2017 paper by Murphy and colleagues says the infant "introjects the values from the caregiver and eventually comes to experience these values as their own."

What the approach aims for when the conditions loosen

The Vienna page on the fully functioning person describes the person Rogers set out in 1959. It lists these among that person's characteristics: "He will have no conditions of worth" and "he will experience unconditional self-regard." Unconditional self-regard means thinking well of yourself with no "only if" attached, so the aim is a person who no longer needs those conditions. That's a description of an aim, and it isn't a promise about how it will go for any one person.

Carry the sofa moment forward. Suppose the person from the opening tells a listener, "I said I didn't mind, but I did," and the listener answers with neither praise nor blame. That's the kind of regard the six conditions describe, and what the person does next is theirs to decide. They might still go along with the new plans, and that's a choice the person is free to make either way.

Where the idea stops, and when to get help

This idea is a theory from one tradition. It's a way of understanding people that Rogers offered, and it isn't a research finding about everyone. Whether person-centred counselling helps is a separate question, and the answer to does person-centred counselling work looks at it on its own. The idea also can't tell anyone what caused one person's feelings, because the guilt on the sofa could have many sources, and other traditions see it another way.

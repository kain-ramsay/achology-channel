Did mindfulness begin as a religious practice?

It did, in the sense that it comes out of a religious tradition. In his 2003 paper, Jon Kabat-Zinn writes that mindfulness has historically been called the heart of Buddhist meditation. The same paper also says "There is nothing particularly Buddhist about it", and it calls mindfulness an inherent human capacity, something every person has to some degree from one moment to the next. Those are the views of a founder describing his own thinking, so they tell you how he understood the practice, and they aren't a statement from any Buddhist tradition or any other faith. Question 6 · keyword: is mindfulness evidence based · about 1,180 words

Is mindfulness scientifically proven? What the research says

A friend forwards you a newspaper headline saying that mindfulness has been proven to work, and a few minutes later you're standing with your phone in your hand, wondering whether to believe it. She sounds certain, and you can't tell from a headline whether it reports something settled or a hopeful summary of one study. It's fine to be unsure about that, and you could look together at what the research itself says.

So, is mindfulness evidence based, and can it be called proven? Here's the short version, the way you might tell a friend: large reviews of trials found gains on some measures compared with doing nothing, little or no edge over other active options, and many studies with real weaknesses. Proven is a stronger word than the abstracts of these papers use, and reading what they say shows why.

Is mindfulness evidence based? What the large reviews found

Let's start with a review by Goyal and colleagues in JAMA Internal Medicine in 2014, which kept 47 trials with 3515 adults from clinical groups, found by searching up to November 2012, each with a control group doing something active. For mindfulness meditation courses, the authors report moderate evidence that some outcomes improved, and low evidence that stress and distress did. For outcomes such as positive mood, attention, sleep and weight, they report low evidence of no effect or too little evidence to say. They also found no evidence that meditation courses did better than any active approach they were tested against; you can weigh that up at your own pace.

A larger review, by Galante and colleagues in PLOS Medicine in 2021, gathered 136 trials with 11,605 adults from 29 countries, all taught in person outside clinical settings, with results measured one to six months afterwards. Compared with doing nothing, average scores improved on distress (27 trials) and wellbeing (nine trials), though the authors say this held "in most but not all scenarios". Against active options that weren't designed to have their own effects, the results were mixed, with no clear gain on distress. Against options that were designed to have their own effects, they found no clear sign that mindfulness did better.

Neither review claims proof, and Galante and colleagues write that their findings "do not support generalisation of MBP [mindfulness-based programme] effects across every setting". Their conclusion adds that other approaches to prevention may work as well. You're welcome to open either paper and read the wording for yourself, at whatever pace suits you.

Where the studies are weak or mixed

Galante and colleagues write that many of the trials they included are at high risk of bias, and that only the effect on distress remained once the higher-risk trials were set aside. Their trust in the results is moderate to very low, mainly because the trials disagreed with one another, and that matters because results that disagree are harder to lean on.

In 2018, 15 scientists published a paper called Mind the Hype. Its abstract says that "misinformation and poor methodology associated with past studies of mindfulness may lead public consumers to be harmed, misled, and disappointed", and it discusses how hard mindfulness is to define. That matters, because a review can only count trials of whatever its authors call mindfulness, so it's fair to ask whether every trial meant the same thing by it. It's a question you're free to carry into any headline you read.

Picture two friends who each spend eight weeks on something new, one in a mindfulness class and the other in a weekly walking group. Suppose both say afterwards that they feel better. A trial that compares mindfulness only with people who did nothing can't tell whether the mindfulness mattered or whether any regular group activity would have done as well. That's why the reviews keep results against doing nothing apart from results against active options, and why the two sets of results read so differently.

How mindfulness might work, and what is only proposed

Some researchers also ask how mindfulness might work, and feel free to wonder about that too. A 2011 paper by Britta Hölzel and colleagues proposed four parts: attention regulation, body awareness, emotion regulation and a change in perspective on the self. Attention regulation is what you use when you're reading on a train, notice that your thoughts are on lunch, and bring them back to the sentence. The authors suggest that the four parts work together, and that the result is better self-regulation.

A proposal is not a settled finding, so the four parts describe a possible route and not a proven one. You're welcome to try them as a way of noticing what you do in a practice, and to set them aside if they don't help. On a walk to the shops, for example, you could notice how your feet and shoulders feel as you go, which is one small way of trying the second part.

What the research can't tell you, and when to get proper help

Averages from hundreds of people can't tell you what would happen for you, and the reviews measured set courses over set weeks, so they don't cover every way of practising. The question "is mindfulness evidence based?" has to be answered one outcome and one comparison at a time. There's no need to hurry if you'd rather wait for better evidence, and you're equally free to try a practice out of curiosity.

Reading about research isn't a substitute for talking with a qualified professional, wherever you live and whatever you've read. If low spirits or worry have lasted for weeks, or they're starting to get in the way of sleep, work or relationships, please speak with a doctor or another qualified professional about it. Please also reach out straight away if you ever feel that you, or anyone near you, may be unsafe.

The directory at findahelpline.com lists verified helplines in 175 or more countries. For example, people in the UK can reach emergency services on 999, urgent advice on NHS 111 and Samaritans, free of charge, on 116 123 at any time. In the US, for example, dialling or texting 988 connects you with the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline.

Question 7 · keyword: can mindfulness meditation be harmful · about 1,268 words

Can mindfulness be harmful, and who should be careful?

It's a quiet evening, and you're on the sofa with a guided mindfulness track playing through your headphones, because a friend recommended it. Partway through, you might have noticed an uneasy feeling rising that you hadn't expected, and perhaps a small voice asking whether you're doing it wrong or whether the practice itself is the problem. There's no need to finish the track, so press pause if a moment of quiet would help, and sit with the room for a moment.

If you've searched "can mindfulness meditation be harmful", the answer from published research is that it can be for some people, although the studies disagree a good deal about how often. They find unpleasant experiences in a large share of the people they follow and lasting harm in a smaller share, and they state openly where their own figures fall short. You're welcome to go gently, to stop or pause a practice that becomes upsetting, and to talk to a qualified professional where you live if it stays hard.

Can mindfulness meditation be harmful? What the reviews found

In 2020, Miguel Farias and colleagues published a review of adverse events in meditation in Acta Psychiatrica Scandinavica. They searched five databases up to October 2019 and found 83 studies that qualified, with 6,703 people who had practised meditation. Overall, adverse events came to about 8 in 100 people, and the spread runs from about 4 in 100 in experiments to about 33 in 100 in studies that just watched people. That's a wide gap, and you might picture how different ten minutes on the sofa is from a week on a silent retreat.

In 2021, Willoughby Britton and colleagues reported in Clinical Psychological Science on 96 people who had taken an eight-week mindfulness-based programme, where an independent assessor used a 44-item interview about experiences linked to meditation. Of the 96, 83% had at least one, 58% had one that felt negative, 37% had one that hurt how they functioned, and lasting bad effects appeared in 6% to 14%. The authors say the rates of transient distress and negative impacts were similar to those of other psychological treatments. If those figures give you pause, you're welcome to set the phone down and look out of the window for a minute.

How far can those numbers be trusted?

The review's authors say that studies rarely use standard measures and that many only watch for serious events, which probably made the true rate look lower. They add that their observational data "preclude any clear causality assessment" between meditation and the events. They also say the wide gaps between studies are likely to come from their different methods and from having no standard way to assess adverse events.

Britton and colleagues name a limit of their own: the interview was described in the study's published protocol, but it wasn't a primary or secondary outcome, and no statistical analysis plan came with it. A gentler picture came from Ruth Baer and colleagues, who asked 84 schoolteachers and 74 university students about an eight-week course made for the public. About two-thirds had unpleasant experiences during the course, but 85% to 92% rated them as not at all or only somewhat upsetting, and 3% to 7% said it had harmed them, in studies with no comparison group.

It's a bit like asking a friend whether a long walk was tiring and whether it left her hurt: the two questions get different answers. Marco Schlosser and colleagues asked 1,232 regular meditators one question in 2019, and 25.6% said they'd had very unpleasant experiences that their practice might have caused, though one question can't show what caused them or how strong they were. Jared Lindahl and colleagues talked with 60 meditators in 2017 who were picked because they'd had hard experiences, so their work shows the range and not how common it is.

Who should be careful, and when?

When you ask "can mindfulness meditation be harmful", the studies point to certain situations more than to certain kinds of person. In Schlosser's survey, people who had been on a retreat at some point were more likely to report very unpleasant experiences. So were people who only did insight meditation (vipassana), a type the authors class as deconstructive.

If you're picturing a long silent retreat set beside ten minutes at home, Lindahl's paper reports that 43 of the 60 meditators (72%) had their troubles during or right after a retreat, and 23 were meditating 10 or more hours a day when they began. The paper says 72% of the people practised often within a meditation community and 75% worked with a teacher when challenges arose.

The Farias review also notes that studies run in uncontrolled settings reported higher rates than structured programmes that teach people how to cope when things get hard. These are different kinds of study, so the two can't be compared exactly. It found mixed results on whether more practice goes with more adverse events, and its authors say the data don't let them tell who is more vulnerable. It's fine to hold all of this lightly.

What you can do, and when to get proper help

You can keep a sitting short, leave your eyes open, look around the room or feel your feet on the floor, and stop whenever a practice becomes upsetting. If the sofa evening left you uneasy, you might put the headphones down, turn on a lamp and make yourself a hot drink. You can leave it for a day or a week, and you don't need to finish a track you've started. Nothing about the practice obliges you to carry on through something that feels too hard.

Reading about research can't replace a talk with someone who knows your situation. If the unpleasant feelings keep returning for days or weeks, or begin to disturb your sleep, your work or your relationships, please contact a doctor or another qualified professional where you live. Please do the same if you ever feel that you or somebody else isn't safe, and where someone is in danger, the first call is to the emergency number in your own country.

MBSR and MBCT: what are they, and how are they different?

You're reading a leaflet pinned to a library noticeboard, the one about an eight-week mindfulness course, and you find yourself wondering what really goes on in a room like that from one week to the next. So, what is mindfulness based stress reduction, and how do the others differ? Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) and Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) are each a structured group programme, taught by a trained teacher over eight weekly classes, with daily practice at home between them. MBCT adds ideas from cognitive therapy to that shape, and .b is a separate school curriculum for young people aged 11 to 18.

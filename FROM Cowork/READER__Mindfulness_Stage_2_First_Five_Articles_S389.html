Did mindfulness begin as a religious practice?

Yes, in the sense that it comes out of a religious tradition. In his 2003 paper, Jon Kabat-Zinn writes that mindfulness has historically been called the heart of Buddhist meditation. He adds that it resides at the core of the teachings of the Buddha.

The same paper also says "There is nothing particularly Buddhist about it", and it calls mindfulness an inherent human capacity, something every person has to some degree from one moment to the next. Those are the views of a founder describing his own thinking. They tell you how he understood the practice, and they aren't a statement from any Buddhist tradition or any other faith.

Question 6 · keyword: is mindfulness evidence based · about 1,113 words

Is mindfulness scientifically proven? What the research says

A friend forwards you a newspaper headline saying that mindfulness has been proven to work, and a few minutes later you're wondering whether to believe it. She sounds sure, and you can't tell whether the headline reports a settled fact or a hopeful summary of one study. It's fine not to know, and you're welcome to look at what the research itself says.

So, is mindfulness evidence based, and can it be called proven? The research backs something more modest than either claim: large reviews of trials found gains on some measures compared with doing nothing, little or no edge over other active options, and many studies with real weaknesses. Proven is a stronger word than the abstracts of these papers use, and reading what they say shows why.

Is mindfulness evidence based? What the large reviews found

One review, by Goyal and colleagues in JAMA Internal Medicine in 2014, kept 47 trials with 3515 adults from clinical groups, found by searching up to November 2012, each with a control group doing something active. For mindfulness meditation courses, the authors report moderate evidence that some outcomes improved, and low evidence that stress and distress did. For outcomes such as positive mood, attention, sleep and weight, they report low evidence of no effect or too little evidence to say. They also found no evidence that meditation courses did better than any active approach they were tested against.

A larger review, by Galante and colleagues in PLOS Medicine in 2021, gathered 136 trials with 11,605 adults from 29 countries, all taught in person outside clinical settings, with results measured one to six months afterwards. Compared with doing nothing, average scores improved on distress (27 trials) and wellbeing (nine trials), though the authors say this held "in most but not all scenarios". Against active options that weren't designed to have their own effects, the results were mixed, with no clear gain on distress. Against options that were designed to have their own effects, they found no clear sign that mindfulness did better.

Neither review claims proof, and Galante and colleagues write that their findings do not support assuming the same effects in every setting. Their conclusion adds that other approaches to prevention may work as well. The trials came from 29 countries, so the results aren't tied to one place. You're welcome to open either paper and read the wording for yourself.

Where the studies are weak or mixed

The papers describe their own limits, and those limits are part of the answer. Galante and colleagues write that many of the trials they included are at high risk of bias, and that only the effect on distress remained once the higher-risk trials were set aside. Their trust in the results is moderate to very low, mainly because the trials disagreed with one another.

In 2018, 15 scientists published a paper called Mind the Hype. Its abstract says that "misinformation and poor methodology associated with past studies of mindfulness may lead public consumers to be harmed, misled, and disappointed", and it discusses how hard mindfulness is to define. That matters, because a review can only count trials of whatever its authors call mindfulness. It's fair to ask whether every trial meant the same thing.

Picture two friends who each spend eight weeks on something new, one in a mindfulness class and the other in a weekly walking group. Suppose both say afterwards that they feel better, and both are pleased with how it went. A trial that compares mindfulness only with people who did nothing can't tell whether the mindfulness mattered or whether any regular group activity would have done as well. That's why the reviews keep results against doing nothing apart from results against active options.

How mindfulness might work, and what is only proposed

Researchers also ask how mindfulness might work. A 2011 paper by Britta Hölzel and colleagues proposed four parts: attention regulation, body awareness, emotion regulation and a change in perspective on the self. Attention regulation is what you use when you're reading on a train, notice that your thoughts are on lunch, and bring them back to the sentence. The authors suggest that the four parts work together, and that the result is better self-regulation.

The 2011 paper proposes these four parts as a way of describing how mindfulness might work. A proposal is not a settled finding, so the four parts describe a possible route and not a proven one. You're welcome to try them as a way of noticing what you do in a practice, and to set them aside if they don't help.

What the research can't tell you, and when to get proper help

Averages from hundreds of people can't tell you what would happen for you, and the reviews measured set courses over set weeks, so they don't cover every way of practising. The question "is mindfulness evidence based?" has to be answered one outcome and one comparison at a time. There's no need to hurry if you'd rather wait for better evidence, and you're equally free to try a practice out of curiosity.

Reading about research isn't a substitute for talking with a qualified professional, wherever you live and whatever you've read. If low spirits or worry have lasted for weeks, or they're starting to get in the way of sleep, work or relationships, please speak with a doctor or another qualified professional about it. Please also reach out straight away if you ever feel that you, or anyone near you, may be unsafe.

The directory at findahelpline.com lists verified helplines in 175 or more countries. For example, people in the UK can reach emergency services on 999, urgent advice on NHS 111 and Samaritans, free of charge, on 116 123 at any time. In the US, for example, dialling or texting 988 connects you with the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline.

Question 7 · keyword: can mindfulness meditation be harmful · about 1,202 words

Can mindfulness be harmful, and who should be careful?

It's a quiet evening, and you're on the sofa with a guided mindfulness track playing through your headphones because a friend recommended it. Partway through, an uneasy feeling you hadn't expected rises up, and you begin to wonder whether you're doing it wrong or whether the practice itself is the problem. There's no need to finish the track, and you're welcome to press pause.

If you've searched "can mindfulness meditation be harmful", the answer from published research is that it can be for some people, although the studies disagree a good deal about how often. They find unpleasant experiences in a large share of the people they follow and lasting harm in a smaller share, and they state openly where their own figures fall short. You're welcome to go gently, to stop or pause a practice that becomes upsetting, and to talk to a qualified professional where you live if it stays hard.

Can mindfulness meditation be harmful? What the reviews found

In 2020, Miguel Farias and colleagues published a review of adverse events in meditation in Acta Psychiatrica Scandinavica. They searched five databases up to October 2019 and found 83 studies that qualified, with 6,703 people who had practised meditation. Overall, adverse events came to about 8 in 100 people, and the spread runs from about 4 in 100 in experiments to about 33 in 100 in studies that just watched people. That's a wide gap, and ten minutes on the sofa is a different thing from a week on a silent retreat.

In 2021, Willoughby Britton and colleagues reported a study in Clinical Psychological Science. It followed 96 people who had taken an eight-week mindfulness-based programme, and an independent assessor used a 44-item interview about experiences linked to meditation. Of the 96, 83% had at least one, 58% had one that felt negative, 37% had one that hurt how they functioned, and lasting bad effects appeared in 6% to 14%. The authors say the rates of transient distress and negative impacts were similar to those of other psychological treatments.

How far can those numbers be trusted?

The review's authors say that studies rarely use standard measures and that many only watch for serious events, which probably made the true rate look lower. They add that their observational data "preclude any clear causality assessment" between meditation and the events. They also say the large variation between studies is likely to reflect the different methods used and the lack of a standard way to assess adverse events.

Britton and colleagues name a limit of their own: the interview was described in the study's published protocol, but it wasn't a primary or secondary outcome, and no statistical analysis plan came with it. A gentler picture came from Ruth Baer and colleagues, who asked 84 schoolteachers and 74 university students about an eight-week course made for the public. About two-thirds had unpleasant experiences during the course, but 85% to 92% rated them as not at all or only somewhat upsetting, and 3% to 7% said it had harmed them, in studies with no comparison group.

It's a bit like asking a friend whether a long walk was tiring and whether it left her hurt: the two questions get different answers. Marco Schlosser and colleagues asked 1,232 regular meditators one question in 2019, and 25.6% said they'd had very unpleasant experiences that their practice might have caused. One question can't show what caused those experiences or how strong they were. Jared Lindahl and colleagues talked with 60 meditators in 2017 who were picked because they'd had hard experiences, so their work shows the range and not how common it is.

Who should be careful, and when?

When people ask "can mindfulness meditation be harmful", the studies point to certain situations more than to certain kinds of person. In Schlosser's survey, people who had been on a retreat at some point were more likely to report very unpleasant experiences. So were people who only did insight meditation (vipassana), a type the authors class as deconstructive. The survey can't say why, since it was one question asked once.

In Lindahl's interviews, 43 of the 60 meditators (72%) had their troubles during or right after a retreat, and 23 were meditating 10 or more hours a day when they began. The paper says 72% of the people practised often within a meditation community and 75% worked with a teacher when challenges arose.

The Farias review also notes that studies run in uncontrolled settings reported higher rates than structured programmes that teach people how to cope when things get hard. These are different kinds of study, so the two can't be compared exactly. It found mixed results on whether more practice goes with more adverse events, and its authors say the data don't let them tell who is more vulnerable.

What you can do, and when to get proper help

You can keep a sitting short, leave your eyes open, look around the room or feel your feet on the floor, and stop whenever a practice becomes upsetting. You can leave it for a day or a week, and you don't need to finish a track you've started. Nothing about the practice obliges you to carry on through something that feels too hard.

Reading about research can't replace a talk with someone who knows your situation. If the unpleasant feelings keep returning for days or weeks, or begin to disturb your sleep, your work or your relationships, please contact a doctor or another qualified professional where you live. Please do the same if you ever feel that you or somebody else isn't safe, and where someone is in danger, the first call is to the emergency number in your own country.

If you'd rather talk to someone right now, you can search findahelpline.com, whose verified helplines span more than 175 countries. As one example from the UK, you can phone 999 for an emergency, dial 111 for the NHS urgent advice line, or ring Samaritans for free on 116 123 at any hour. In the US, for instance, the number 988 connects you by call or text to the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, and 988lifeline.org gives the details.

Question 9 · keyword: what is mindfulness based stress reduction · about 1,228 words

MBSR and MBCT: what are they, and how are they different?

You're reading a leaflet pinned to a library noticeboard about an eight-week mindfulness course, and you wonder what happens in a room like that from one week to the next. That evening a friend messages to say her son's school has started teaching something called .b, and a search on your phone offers a third abbreviation, MBCT. It's fine to ask whether all three are one thing under different names before you sign up for anything.

So, what is mindfulness based stress reduction, and how do the others differ? Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) and Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) are each a structured group programme, taught by a trained teacher over eight weekly classes, with daily practice at home between them. MBCT adds ideas from cognitive therapy to that shape, and .b is a separate school curriculum for young people aged 11 to 18. All three are taught in groups with a teacher, which sets them apart from a self-paced course you follow on your own.

What is mindfulness based stress reduction, and how does a course run?

You can read where MBSR began in our comparison of CBT, ACT, REBT and mindfulness. UMass Memorial Health's online MBSR course is one example of how a programme is set out. It lists eight weekly classes of 2.5 hours each, an all-day class on a weekend usually between weeks six and seven, and daily home practice of 45 to 60 minutes. The classes teach mindfulness meditation and gentle yoga, with discussion of the mind-body connection, and the page asks that participants be willing to attend all of the classes.

Other providers arrange things a little differently, so those numbers are one page's timetable and not a rule for every MBSR course. Daily practice means a slot in an ordinary day, such as the minutes before a video call begins, when you notice your breathing and the chair under you. It's fine to check a provider's own listing for its timetable before you plan your weeks around it.

What is mindfulness based cognitive therapy, and how is it different?

The about page on MBCT.com says MBCT is based on the eight-week MBSR programme, and it names Zindel Segal, Mark Williams and John Teasdale as its developers. Its page on classes lets you picture a week. You meet an instructor and the rest of the class for 2 hours, each class is built around one theme, and the group works through it by talking together about what each person noticed and by trying a mindfulness practice. Then you spend the days until the next class practising at home with recorded guided meditations, and the eight weeks also include one all-day session.

Put side by side, the two pages describe the same overall shape: eight weekly classes, one all-day session and practice at home between classes. What differs is what happens in the classes. The MBSR page lists meditation, gentle yoga and discussion of the mind-body connection, while the MBCT page describes a theme each week that the group talks over while trying a practice, with ideas from cognitive therapy in the mix.

Think of two weekly community classes, one that lists the activities you'll do in the hour and one that takes a theme each evening and talks it over as you practise. The class lengths differ too, 2.5 hours against 2 hours, though that may be one provider's timetable more than a difference between the two programmes.

MBCT's own pages also say who it was designed for, and that's worth reading on the site itself. Because suitability depends on the person, it's a question to talk over with a doctor or with the programme's teacher. You're welcome to bring those pages with you to that conversation, and there's no need to work it out alone.

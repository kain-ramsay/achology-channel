How do you start practising mindfulness, and how often?

It's Sunday evening, and on Monday you told a friend you'd read through her CV before Friday. It's still sitting in your inbox, and when she messages to ask how it's going, you hear yourself typing that you've just been so busy, which is a reply you've given before. It's fine to wonder, with the phone still in your hand, whether busy is the whole reason.

If you've searched for how to practice mindfulness, the answer at Achology is that you start by looking at yourself. You ask one honest question about how you handled something today and why, and you come back to that question regularly, for a few moments each day. It needs no equipment and no special place, only a moment with your own thoughts.

How to practice mindfulness: what are you looking at?

Mindfulness, as we mean it, is being aware of yourself in the present moment: how you're conducting yourself, and the reasons behind it. It's more than being at peace, drifting off or switching your mind off, because you're paying attention to something particular, and that something is your own mind. The mind here means what you think, believe, feel and do, and it's a different thing from the brain, which is the physical organ.

The purpose is to understand yourself well enough to take more responsibility for your thoughts, feelings and actions. That's an invitation, and it's good news, and it isn't blame for anything that happened to you, only a question about what you do from here.

How to practice mindfulness: what is a first question to ask yourself?

A feeling or an action rarely arrives out of nowhere. What you do and feel usually follows from what you were thinking, what you believed and how you read the moment, and looking back along that line is called backtracking. The first question is a short version of it: what did I do just now, and what was I telling myself when I did it?

Back with the CV, an answer might be that you kept putting it off because you were afraid she'd ask what you really thought of it. You're welcome to stop with that one reason for today. Say you dropped out of a weekly choir group instead, telling everyone you were too busy, and the reason you find is that you were embarrassed to be the weakest voice.

When an answer stings, you can stay with it for a moment and ask what you'd like to do about it. If the same reason turns up in more than one place, such as a fear of being judged behind both the CV and the choir, that's worth noticing, and after a few weeks you can look back across your answers for reasons that repeat. Once a reason is clear, you can set where you are now beside where you'd like to be, and choose one small step between the two.

How often should you do it?

The rhythm is a few moments each day, spent on what went well and what could have been done differently. There's no set number of minutes. You might choose a point in your day that already exists, such as when you shut your laptop at the end of work, and ask your question then.

Other teaching also comes back to regularity. The NHS page on mindfulness suggests picking a regular time, and Mind's page on mindfulness exercises and tips says: "Regular, short periods of mindfulness can work better than occasional long ones." Each of those pages goes on to describe its own way of practising, which you can read there.

If a day goes by without your question, you can pick it up the next evening, and nothing has to be made up. Our article Can self-awareness be learned, and how do you develop it? goes further into self-awareness.

When is looking at yourself not enough?

Mindfulness practice is education and self-help, and for anything beyond that it's a health professional you'll want to talk to, such as your own doctor. If a low or worried mood stays for several weeks, or starts to keep you from sleeping well or from getting on with everyday life, please see a doctor or another qualified person nearby. If you or someone close to you may be in danger, please contact the emergency services where you live at once.

For a helpline near you, findahelpline.com lists services in more than 175 countries and says the organisations behind them verify their details with it directly. As a UK example, 999 is the number to call when someone is in immediate danger, NHS 111 is there for urgent advice that isn't an emergency, and Samaritans are on 116 123 all day and every day. In the US, as a further example, the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline takes phone calls and texts on 988.

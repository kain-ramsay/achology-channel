How do you start practising mindfulness, and how often?

It's Sunday evening, and on Monday you told a friend you'd read through her CV before Friday. It's still sitting in your inbox, and when she messages to ask how it's going, you hear yourself typing that you've just been so busy, which is a reply you've given before. It's fine to wonder, with the phone still in your hand, whether busy is the whole reason.

If you've searched for how to practice mindfulness, the answer at Achology is that you start by looking at yourself, with one honest question about how you handled something today and why. Mindfulness here means being aware of yourself in the present moment, including how you're conducting yourself and the reasons behind it, and lesson 10 of our Mindfulness for Mental Health, Personal Growth and Inner Peace course opens with the words "Mindfulness is a form of self-awareness." As for how often, one of our self-reflection lessons says it can be as simple as taking a few moments each day to think about what went well and what could have been done differently.

How to practice mindfulness at Achology: what do you look at first?

Lesson 2 of our Mindfulness Practitioner Diploma Course (Beginner to Expert) covers the basics of mindfulness, and it says that mindfulness is far more than a state of peace, drifting off or switching your mind off. It describes awareness of what you think, feel and notice around you, held without judging it, and it talks about answering life's challenges thoughtfully in place of reacting on impulse. In that lesson, what you're looking at is yourself, which means your own mind.

Achology uses that word carefully, because lesson 15 of the Diploma Course separates the mind from the brain: the brain is the physical organ, and the mind is your thoughts, emotions and consciousness. So the looking is about what you think, believe, feel and do. In lesson 4 of the Inner Peace course, Kain Ramsay contrasts conscious awareness with autopilot, and he encourages us to pause and reflect before we act and to evaluate the cost of our decisions before we make them.

The aim of teaching it this way is that you come to understand yourself well enough to take more responsibility for your thoughts, feelings and actions. That's an invitation and good news, and it isn't blame for anything that happened to you, only a question about what you do next.

How to practice mindfulness: what is a first question to ask yourself?

Lesson 49 of the Inner Peace course, Questions to Sharpen Your Relationship With Truth, teaches a habit it calls backtracking. Its description says that our emotional responses are internal processes shaped by our thoughts, beliefs and interpretations, and that backtracking means taking a closer look at the causes behind an outcome we didn't want, so we can reflect on the thought patterns or assumptions that contributed and see the role we play in shaping our experiences. Here's a first question that we've put together from that idea: what did I do just now, and what was I telling myself when I did it?

Back with the CV, an answer might be that you kept putting it off because you were afraid she'd ask what you really thought of it. You're welcome to stop with that one reason for today. If it turns out to be something else, such as a week that did fill up, that's a fine answer as well.

When one question starts to feel familiar, the lessons offer more. Lesson 29 of the Diploma Course describes self-reflection as like looking into a mirror and explaining what you see, and lesson 3 of the Inner Peace course teaches the Mindfulness Full-Cycle Model, with questions to put to yourself about your biases and thought patterns. Lessons 66 to 68 of the Diploma Course introduce the Six Stages of Personal Reflection, in which Kain takes Karen through setting where she is now beside where she'd like to be.

How often should you do it?

Lesson 112 of the Diploma Course, Self-Reflection: The Five Degrees of Self-Reflecting, says the practice can be as simple as taking a few moments each day to think about what went well and what could have been done differently. If you're wondering how to practice mindfulness every day, the lesson descriptions give no number of minutes. You might choose a point in your day that already exists, such as when you shut your laptop at the end of work, and ask your one question then.

Other teaching also comes back to regularity. The NHS page on mindfulness suggests picking a regular time, and Mind's page on mindfulness exercises and tips says: "Regular, short periods of mindfulness can work better than occasional long ones." Each of those pages goes on to describe its own way of practising, which you can read there.

If a day goes by without your question, you can pick it up the next evening, and nothing has to be made up. If you'd like to read about developing self-awareness more widely, our article Can self-awareness be learned, and how do you develop it? is there for that.

When is looking at yourself not enough?

Mindfulness practice is education and self-help, and for anything beyond that it's a health professional you'll want to talk to, such as your own doctor. If a low or worried mood stays for several weeks, or starts to keep you from sleeping well or from getting on with everyday life, please see a doctor or another qualified person nearby. If you or someone close to you may be in danger, please contact the emergency services where you live at once.

For a helpline near you, findahelpline.com lists services in more than 175 countries and says the organisations behind them verify their details with it directly. As a UK example, 999 is the number to call when someone is in immediate danger, NHS 111 is there for urgent advice that isn't an emergency, and Samaritans are on 116 123 all day and every day. In the US, as a further example, the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline takes phone calls and texts on 988.

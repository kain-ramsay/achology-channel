Why Is Small Talk Important?

Why is small talk important? Think about how you met your partner or your best friend. Somewhere in the past you had a little conversation with them about nothing much, and everything else developed from that very moment.

Small talk has become the neglected and disliked poor cousin of conversation. We see it as filler for awkward pauses, something to get through at dreaded work parties and family events with the in-laws. I see it differently, and I want to explain why.

Small Talk Is the Warm-Up Before the Main Event

No one starts making connections by walking up to a stranger and sharing their deepest thoughts, struggles and beliefs straight away. Anyone who attempted it would come across as a complete weirdo. Small talk is simply the warm-up that we need before the main event.

That doesn't mean talking about the weather for two hours. It means a short, friendly start that lets two people get comfortable with each other, and it's what makes the main event possible. Think of the work parties and family gatherings you dread most: what would change if you treated the first ten minutes as the warm-up instead of the chore, and what might be waiting for you beyond it?

What We Get Wrong About It

We tend to see it as useless and "deep conversation" as valuable, and we presume that interesting people speak only when they have something profound to say. An increasing number of people no longer like talking to strangers at all, which is a pity, because any stranger you meet could be your best friend or your most amazing business partner tomorrow.

Small talk isn't the opposite of a meaningful conversation. It's the doorway into one, and it's the first tool we have for building a community around us. If you never go through the doorway, you never reach the room on the other side.

It Is About Connection, Not the Words

It isn't about the words. It's about connection. The weather, the queue and the coffee are only the envelope, and the letter inside says that you're friendly and would like to know the other person a little, which is a message almost everybody is glad to receive.

Recall a time when a stranger said something friendly to you, perhaps something as ordinary as a remark about the rain on an unremarkable afternoon. How did you feel in the moment afterwards, and did you pass it on to the next person you met? Small exchanges like that seldom feel important while they're happening, yet they're often where the next step begins.

We share our inner thoughts and feelings with our best friends and partners through meaningful, deep conversation. To open up like that with another person we need trust first, and trust building starts with the smallest of exchanges. Nobody arrives at the core of a friendship on the first day, so it helps to be patient with the first few words. Ask yourself which of your closest friendships began with a few easy sentences, and how many layers had to be peeled before you trusted each other completely.

Familiarity Becomes Trust

Building a relationship needs familiarity. It's like peeling an onion, layer by layer, until we arrive at the core where trust lives. The process doesn't have to take years: it can be very quick, and sometimes one conversation at a party is enough to lead to a coffee a week later, which goes a layer deeper.

Psychology has noticed a version of this. In his 1968 paper, Robert Zajonc wrote that "mere repeated exposure of the individual to a stimulus object enhances his attitude toward it." His paper concerns liking rather than trust, so I read it only as a hint that the familiar tends to feel friendlier, which is the ground trust grows in.

From a Greeting to Trust in Four Steps

Here's the principle I follow. A greeting creates recognition, a question creates curiosity, a few minutes of conversation create familiarity, and familiarity can become trust. Each link in that chain is small, and together they carry two strangers a long way.

A greeting lets someone know they've been noticed as a person. A question shows that you're curious about them, and a few minutes of conversation give both of you something familiar to stand on, so that the next conversation doesn't start from nothing. Each step leads naturally into the following one, which is why I find the chain so easy to remember and so easy to try out for yourself.

Picture a person who sits near you at a weekly class, someone you've never spoken to. In the first week you nod, and in the second you say hello and they say it back. In the third week you ask how their week has been and you want to hear the answer, and by the fourth you aren't strangers any more. Nothing dramatic has happened, yet the two of you now know each other a little, and that's how it begins.

How to Do It Well

It's simple, and it doesn't need a script. You listen, you ask genuine questions out of curiosity, and you show the other person that you're interested in them and care about their world. The conversation is about them, not about you and not about the small subject you started with.

We won't develop a deep friendship with every person we meet, and there will always be a select group in our inner circle. But that doesn't mean the other connections don't count. I think each of them, however brief, is a small piece of the community we're part of, and that's worth keeping in mind when a quick hello feels like too much trouble. Before you next walk past someone, ask yourself what a greeting would cost you, and what it might give them.

Try It for Four Weeks

What is your relationship with small talk like? If you don't like it, here's a challenge. For the next four weeks, look for three different small talks a day, and notice what happens.

It can be a hello to a neighbour, a remark to the person beside you in a queue, or a real question for someone you usually walk past. Notice how the other person responds and how you feel afterwards. Which of the three would you look forward to most tomorrow, and which would you least like to try?

You don't have to enjoy every one of them, and you don't have to be good at it straight away. You only have to notice what happens, and to see whether the people around you start to feel a little more like neighbours and a little less like strangers. Come back to this at the end of the four weeks and ask yourself what has changed.

Leaving the Door Open

The art of small talk isn't learning how to talk about insignificant things. It's learning how insignificant moments can create significant human connections. A greeting, a question and a few minutes of your time are enough to begin, so why not begin today? Who is one person you usually pass without a word, and what might happen if you greeted them tomorrow and asked them a real question out of curiosity?

How Do Everyday Conversations Create a Sense of Belonging?

How do everyday conversations create a sense of belonging? Start with a simple count, by thinking back over yesterday and listing every person you spoke to, from those closest to you to the one who took your payment and the one who held a door. Did the big talks with family and close friends come to mind first, while the short ones slipped away unnoticed? They may do more quiet work than we give them credit for, and I'd like to consider the reasons with you.

Why We Crave a Sense of Belonging

We humans crave safety and the feeling that we belong somewhere, and that need can be met in small ways. Friendly short chats during the day, exchanged with strangers as well as with acquaintances, can make us feel part of a community, whether it's a quick word with the cashier at the supermarket or a short talk with a colleague. Little exchanges like these add to our feelings of safety and wellbeing.

Notice how ordinary those moments are. Neither the cashier nor the colleague is asked to share a secret or become a close friend, because a few friendly words can be enough to start, and the feeling that follows may well be genuine. When did someone last greet you in a manner that left you feeling genuinely welcome?

A Mix of Conversations, Not Just One Kind

As humans we seem to need a mix of interactions on a regular basis, a little like a cocktail. Strong ties are family, partners and close friends. Weak ties are colleagues, neighbours and acquaintances. Casual contacts are the barista, the shop assistant and the bartender, and then come brief encounters with strangers, such as holding a door open or waiting beside someone at a bus stop.

I'd say only the first kind asks for deep conversation, while the rest ask for a moment, a smile and a few words. Which of those four kinds of conversation did you have yesterday, and which did you skip entirely? I'd suggest that none of them is too small to count, because each one may remind us that we're part of the world around us.

Try holding the four kinds of conversation in mind as a simple map of your week. Where is your week rich, and where is it thin? You might have plenty of close talk and almost no casual contact, or the other way round. There's no score to keep here, only a clearer picture of where a little more warmth and attention could be placed.

What Living in Ireland Showed Me

I've had the experience of living in Ireland, and for years I wondered why so many visitors describe it as a homecoming. Ireland is known for being super friendly and is called the land of a thousand welcomes. Visitors say they feel comfortable and relaxed in that atmosphere, yet the reason behind such a consistent impression eluded me for a long time.

I think it's the mix I've just described. The Irish seem to have perfected it, and a five minute exchange with someone there can make you feel that you're part of their world. That might be just the reason travellers feel so welcomed. It isn't a grand gesture, but a steady habit of small, warm exchanges that many people there seem to share.

Think about a place where you've felt at home, whether a town, a café or a workplace. What was it about the people there that made the difference? Was it grand occasions, or was it many small welcomes? Only you can say what fits your own experience.

What the Researchers Found

Researchers have also examined this question in considerable detail. In 2014, Gillian Sandstrom and Elizabeth Dunn published Social Interactions and Well-Being: The Surprising Power of Weak Ties, which reports that students felt happier, and felt a greater sense of belonging, on days when they talked with more classmates than usual. That isn't a story about close friendship, but about ordinary, everyday contact with people at the edges of our lives.

That's a reminder that the edges of our lives may matter more than we think. A sense of belonging seems to be built from close ties and casual ones alike, and the casual ones may be doing more than we realise. Which of the people at the edges of your own week would you genuinely miss if they weren't there?

When Our Heads Are Down

What do many of us do with those small moments of connection instead? We keep our heads down in our devices, or we're so completely absorbed in our own thoughts that we don't notice the people around us. Isolation can follow, and it can gradually lead us to believe that the world is against us. Once we decide that strangers are bad and a chat is a nuisance, we're on a slippery road to loneliness.

Next time you're waiting in a queue, notice where your attention is and whether you're in the room or somewhere else entirely. It's worth asking what we might be missing in the moments when we look away, and who might have offered us a smile. Even a quick glance upward, in a queue or on a pavement, gives a brief encounter the chance to happen.

This might just be part of what we now call the loneliness epidemic, although I'm certainly not suggesting that a single hello resolves everything. What I can offer is one gentle observation, that the small moments are still available to us, waiting patiently on the other side of the screen.

Loneliness and the Missing Small Moments

Would you be satisfied to converse with only one person for the rest of your life, even if every conversation with them was wonderful? I'd guess most of us would say no, and that answer is worth a moment of reflection. Loneliness may not only be about having nobody to talk with deeply. It may also be about missing the small moments in which we feel part of the human world around us.

That's why I think a sense of belonging is less a feeling we wait for and more a pattern we take part in. You may not need a room full of friends to begin, only a few moments each day when you and somebody else are both present. How might tomorrow feel with just one more of those in it?

Say It and Mean It

There's one more thing I'd add, and it comes from how much I care about saying what we mean. Picture a brief hello to a neighbour, given while you look at your phone, and then the same hello given while you look up and smile. The words are identical, but only one of them says "I see you". I think people feel that difference immediately, even if they struggle to put it into words, and what matters is a plain greeting that you stand behind.

One Small Moment This Week

Choose one everyday contact in your week, the person you see most often and barely speak to, and say a hello that you mean. Then notice how you feel afterwards, and what you can observe in their expression. You aren't trying to become friends with everyone you meet, and you're simply adding one more small thread to the mix of your week.

How Do You Ask Good Questions in a Conversation?

How do you ask good questions in a conversation? The answer begins well before the question itself is spoken. A good question is what curiosity sounds like when it's said aloud, and whoever is listening can usually sense whether it's there. We've each received both kinds: the question that made us feel interesting, and the one that made us feel processed.

There's a simple principle I follow. The other person is what the conversation is for, and definitely not me. Everything I ask comes from that, so I stay curious, I use inviting questions and I let them do most of the talking. The rest is craft, and I believe most of it can be learned in everyday conversations.

Ask Good Questions by Being Curious First

I'd say curiosity comes before technique, and technique without it can feel hollow. If I'm not interested in the answer, no clever wording will rescue the question, and the person across from me can often feel the gap. When you're genuinely interested, it tends to be easier to ask good questions, because they tend to be short, simple and comfortable to answer. So ask yourself what you'd like to know about whoever is sitting across from you at the moment, and begin with that.

A question of curiosity is different from a question of duty, and the difference is easy to hear once you listen for it. The duty version asks what someone does for a living while the asker looks over their shoulder, whereas the curious version asks how they came to do it while looking straight at them. I'd suggest the second works because the words invite a story, while the first only asks for a label.

Other writers have explored this question as well. In The Surprising Power of Questions, Alison Wood Brooks and Leslie K. John describe questions as a way to learn and to build rapport. That sits well with how I see it. A question says "I'd like to know you", and I'd expect goodwill to follow.

Open Questions Invite a Story

An open question can't be answered with a single word, so it leaves the other person free to decide where to go. Asking what something was like, or how it all began, works because it hands the person the pen. A closed question, such as whether they enjoyed the drive, can only be answered with a yes or a no, and then you're the one who has to find the next question. Closed questions have their uses, and they're handy for checking a fact, but I'd say they seldom open a conversation on their own.

Try turning one closed question into an open one today, so that instead of asking whether the weekend was good, you ask what the best part of it was. The change is small and free of cost, and it tells the other person that you'd like considerably more than a polite nod in reply. Short questions leave space for long answers, so keep yours brief.

Follow Up on What You Just Heard

The best next question is usually hiding inside the last answer. When someone mentions a detail, a name or a feeling, you can ask about that detail, and it shows them that every word was heard. A gentle invitation to tell you more about that can carry a conversation a long way, and it asks for nothing except that they keep going.

It also means giving up the question you had prepared, and I think that's a good trade. I'd say a question with no listening behind it is only a prompt. If you'd like to go further into this, Listening Is Not Waiting to Speak: The Discipline of Tuning In looks at what it means to tune in rather than wait for your turn. Let your next question grow out of the answer you've just been given.

There's also a practical reason to follow up, which is that it tends to keep the conversation moving without you having to force it. If you ask good questions by listening for the thread in each answer, you're less likely to run out of things to say, and the other person is less likely to feel managed. A follow-up is also the simplest proof that you were paying attention, which I think many people appreciate more than a clever remark.

Genuine Questions and Interrogating Questions

A genuine question is asked because you want to know the answer. An interrogating question is asked to collect information, to steer, or to prove a point, and whoever is being asked often feels the difference straight away. Rapid-fire questions, one after another, can feel like a form being filled in, even when each of them is kindly meant and sincerely asked, because nobody has been given a moment to breathe.

A question can also hide an opinion, and something like "Don't you think you should leave" is advice wearing a question mark. I'd encourage you to put it openly, asking when you're curious and stating your view when you have one, because both are honest. Mixing them up leaves the listener unsure what you're after, and a sincere question has no hidden answer it's steering toward. What question have you asked lately that was really a statement?

Leave Room for the Pause

After you ask, stop. The quiet that follows can feel remarkably long to the person who asked, yet I suspect it's often the very moment in which the other person decides what to say and how much of it to share. I'm comfortable with pauses, and I'd invite you to become comfortable with them too. If you need something to do, take one slow breath, then let the other person fill the space.

It's tempting to rescue the silence with a second question, or to reword the first before it has had a chance to land, but doing that takes the pause away from the person who needs it. One question, asked simply and followed by quiet, is a generous thing to offer. Notice how you feel when a conversation goes still, and be kind to yourself about it.

Go Deeper Only When the Door Is Open

I use my intuition to decide when to go deeper, and I'm not sure any formula can do that job for you. What I'd listen for is an opening: a subject the person keeps returning to, an answer longer than the question needed, a word said with a little more weight. When you hear one, you can ask gently about it, and if they step back, you step back with them.

Depth is something the other person offers, and our part is to notice it. A question that arrives at the right moment can feel like being understood, while one that arrives too early can feel like pressure. Which of those have you given lately, and which have you received? Sometimes the kindest question is the one you decide to hold back.

Try One Question Today

Choose one ordinary conversation this week and try a few simple things. If you want to ask good questions, begin with an open one you're curious about, and then let the other person do most of the talking. Follow up on one detail in their answer, and leave a pause after you ask, letting it last a little longer than is comfortable. Notice what you learn, and see how it feels to be genuinely interested in another person on purpose.

How Do You Say What You Mean Clearly and Concisely?

How do you say what you mean clearly and concisely? You start by knowing what you mean, which sounds obvious and is frequently the missing step. I suspect many of us begin talking while the thought is still forming, and the message then arrives as a muddle. I care about this a great deal, and I'd like to walk through how I think about it in a few straightforward steps.

Know Your Point Before You Speak

Before you speak, ask yourself one question: what should this person know, or do, when I've finished? If you can't answer in a single sentence, you aren't ready to say it yet, and that's perfectly all right, so take a few seconds to find that sentence. Is your rambling ever simply thinking out loud with an audience present? That's lovely among friends, and it can be difficult to follow in a busy meeting where everybody is waiting for the point.

Try it with something ordinary, such as needing a colleague to submit a report by Thursday. Is your point "the report matters" or "please send it by Thursday"? They're different messages, and only one of them gives the colleague anything to act on, so which one do you usually say?

Finding your point is also a kindness to yourself. Once you know what you mean, what is left to hide behind? That's worth a few quiet seconds before you open your mouth, and it can make every sentence after it easier to choose.

Choose Plain Words

The way I'd put it, plain words are the everyday words the other person already uses, which is why they tend to land. They aren't simple words for simple minds. The Digital.gov guide to plain language describes plain language as content that is clear and easy to understand, and says it is critical to helping the public make sense of their obligations and benefits. That's a fair test for any message you send: could the person hear it once and understand it, without needing a translation?

Technical jargon, overlong sentences and padded phrases tend to obscure the point instead of carrying it, so say "now" rather than "at this point in time", and say "I'll do it" instead of "I'll endeavour to look into the possibility". Compare "I was wondering whether it might be possible for you to look at this when you have a spare moment" with "Could you look at this today?", or "The report is to be actioned at your earliest convenience" with "Please send the report by Thursday." Are short sentences rude? I don't think so, because they can be easy for a listener to remember and to act upon.

I work with members on communication and facilitation, so this is close to my heart. I hold that good conversation puts the other person first, and that applies to clarity too. A clear message is built around the listener: what do they need to hear, and in which words will they hear it? For a wider look at how word choice shapes a conversation, Communication Skills and Language Patterns makes a good companion.

Say What You Mean, Then Stop

Once you've said it, please stop. This may be the hardest step, because a silence after a clear sentence can feel uncomfortable. Does it feel like failure to you? It needn't, since it gives the listener space to consider what you said and to reply.

What do listeners do when a message keeps going past its point? I suspect they drift, and the part that mattered gets lost among the parts that didn't. A single clear sentence, offered at the right moment and then deliberately left alone, can carry as far as a whole paragraph of explanation.

Do you ever add a second explanation, then a third, then an apology for the first two? By then the point can slip away under the padding. If the listener has a question, answer it, and if they don't, you're done.

What happens in you when you've made your point and nobody replies straight away? Simply notice it, without any judgement. You may find a little pull to fill the gap, and you can let that pull pass while the other person thinks.

What an Unclear Message Costs

An unclear message can carry a price. The listener may have to guess what you meant, and a wrong guess can mean work done twice or a promise half understood. Then the explanation has to be given again, and it may run longer than the first one did.

Think of the last time someone said "let's sort that out at some point" and you weren't sure who was meant to do what, or when. Did you leave unsure, with neither of you moving? A clear version such as "I'll send you the details on Friday" takes a moment longer to say and saves everyone the wondering, so which of the two versions would you personally prefer to be given?

Here's a question worth asking yourself honestly. Is there something you've left vague recently because the clear version felt uncomfortable to say? Nobody needs shaming for the answer, and it's often the first step toward a better conversation.

Clear Is a Kindness

When you say what you mean, you're offering the person listening something they can use. I'd call it respect, because they know what you're asking and what it means for them, and they're spared the work of decoding you. You can say a clear thing warmly, too, and a short sentence can be spoken softly, with a kind expression and a steady voice.

Clear speech and sincere speech also belong together. Sincerity is hard to offer through muddled words, because the listener can't tell what you stand behind, whereas a clear sentence gives them something definite they can hold you to. Whether your actions then match your words is the other half of this subject, and a subject for another day.

Begin With Your Next Message

You don't need a special occasion or any preparation to practise this, because any ordinary message will do, whether it's an email, a text or a sentence across the kitchen table. Suppose you want to ask a neighbour to move their bin before collection day. Before you send the message, find the one sentence that lets you say what you mean, put that sentence first, choose the plainest words you have, and stop when it's said: "Could you move the bin before Tuesday?" is enough, and everything after the question mark is optional.

Some messages will feel harder than others, particularly the ones where you're worried about how the other person will take them. In those moments it generally helps to begin from what you want them to understand and to work outward from there, keeping every sentence honest and every word necessary, instead of starting with the apology and hoping the point will appear along the way.

Then see what happens. Did the other person answer the thing you meant, without needing to ask what you were getting at? Did you notice any relief at leaving out the unnecessary padding, and would you like to do it again tomorrow with something a little more important?

What Does It Mean to Mean What You Say?

What does it mean to mean what you say? One way I read it is this: the words you speak, the thing you intend and the thing you go on to do all face the same way. It lives in the small sentences of an ordinary day, such as "yes, of course", "I'll call you" and "that's fine by me". If you've ever said one of those and felt an uncomfortable flicker inside that told you something different, this page is for you, and there's no blame in it, only an invitation to look closely.

Two Halves of One Saying

There's a famous exchange in Lewis Carroll's Alice's Adventures in Wonderland, which Project Gutenberg offers free to read. At the Mad Tea-Party the March Hare tells Alice, "Then you should say what you mean," and when she answers that she means what she says, the Hatter replies, "Not the same thing a bit!" One way I read the scene is that the two halves really do differ. Saying what you mean is about the words fitting the thought, while meaning what you say is about the intention being sincere and the commitment being honoured.

The first half is a matter of clear, well-chosen speech, and it's certainly worth learning. The second half makes me wonder whether a perfectly clear sentence can still be hollow, because "I'd love to come" is clear whether or not you'd love to. I'd say what sits behind the sentence is what makes it sincere, and that's the half I want to examine here. Have you ever heard a sentence that was clear and warm and still didn't quite land, because something behind it didn't match the words themselves?

What It Takes to Mean What You Say

One way I'd describe sincerity is as a quiet check you make on yourself before a sentence leaves your mouth: do I intend this, do I want this, and will I do this? Most of us answer those questions in a split second and, one way I read it, the answer is sometimes kinder than it is true, because agreement can feel more comfortable than disappointing someone. If that's so, a polite yes is often a kindness that went a step too far, and there's no shame in noticing it.

I think the difficulty comes later, when the words are left standing and the deed never follows. So a first step might be simply to become aware of your own habits, and I'd invite you to listen to yourself for a day. Which sentences do you say because they're convenient, and which do you say because you sincerely intend them? You don't need to change anything yet, and you don't need to scold yourself, because noticing is the whole of the first step.

A Yes That Means Yes

Think of the small promises and commitments that fill an ordinary week, such as "let's catch up soon", "I'll send that over" and "leave it with me". Each one is light to say, but I wonder what each one is worth to the person hearing it, and which of them you've made this week without picturing yourself doing it. Keeping it may cost you a few minutes of inconvenience, while letting it go may leave them waiting on a sentence that you'd already set aside.

What I'd try is this: if you want to mean what you say, picture the moment a promise comes due before you make it, and ask whether you can see yourself doing it. If you can, go ahead, and if you can't, you might say "I'll try", and mean only that, which is a small adjustment that gives your word a more manageable responsibility to carry.

Another thing I'd try is giving a promise a date. "Soon" and "sometime" slip out of the mouth without effort but are slippery to keep, while "on Thursday" can be kept or visibly missed. A date gives your word something definite to stand on, and keeping it demonstrates that your words meant what they said.

How People Learn Whether Your Word Holds

Nobody announces that they're keeping a record, but I think people form their impressions of each other through continuous observation. A sentence is said, time passes, and they notice, usually without any deliberate effort, whether the deed followed. I'd guess that's how anyone comes to say of another person, "If she says she'll do it, she'll do it", after a long run of small matches between what was said and what was done. Think of someone whose word you rely on, and ask what they did, over time, to earn that reliance.

I suspect the opposite happens in the same quiet way. After a few promises that never turn into anything, people may stop relying on your words and start checking them. You might never hear about it, yet your next sentence could be a little harder to believe, even when it's entirely sincere and carefully considered. Perhaps that's why small commitments matter more than they appear, and What Trust Costs, and Why It Is Worth Paying takes that idea further.

What I Call Myself

I can offer one small example from my own work as an Achologist. I don't call myself a coach, but an Achologist, because I believe my training goes much deeper, and coaching is a module I can use in my practice rather than my job title. I read my own choice as a small case of meaning what you say: a name that fits what I do, and a practice that fits the name.

It's the same with giving back to a community. When I joined Achology in 2020, senior members gave me so much, and it matters to me to pass that on. So I'm there for the members, and I facilitate around three community events a week. The way I'd put it, caring that's only spoken is half a sentence, and consistently turning up is the other half.

When You Can't Keep Your Word

Meaning what you say doesn't mean you can never change your mind. Circumstances change, and sometimes a commitment can't be honoured, however sincerely it was made. What I'd try is to say so early, in a sentence such as "I said I'd do this, and I can't, and I'm sorry, so here's what I can do instead", which is brief and honest, and spares the other person from waiting in uncertainty.

Can a truthful sentence still be a gentle one? I'd hope so, because you can release a commitment warmly and still mean every syllable, and your effort can then go into the important commitments that you do keep. One Question to Leave With

Here is the one important question that I'd leave with you: which promise did I make this week, and what did I do about it? You don't need to answer it aloud, there's no score to keep, and you may find that one small promise still waiting for you is a good place to begin. Take your time with it, and notice what changes in the way that you speak when your word and your action match.

How Do You Stay Sincere When You Speak in Public?

How do you stay sincere when you speak in public? You say what you mean, and you mean what you say, exactly as you would when chatting with a neighbour over the garden gate. The room is bigger, but the honesty stays the same size, and I'd like to show you why that matters and I hope it makes the whole thing feel lighter. If you've ever been handed a microphone and felt the urge to become somebody else, read on, because it won't take long.

A Room Is Made of People

Standing in front of a group can feel like an entirely different activity from talking to a friend. It's the same act with more listeners, and the person doing it is the same person too. Each one is hoping that what they hear is genuinely meant, which means you don't need a new self for the stage, and you certainly don't have to go and find one.

That's good news, because you may already own more of the skill than you think, and you can find out by noticing how your voice sounds as you tell one person what you mean. Think of the last occasion when you explained something important to somebody you trust. How did your voice sound, and what did you leave out because you didn't need it? Notice that it probably wasn't loud or rehearsed, and that nobody in the conversation was expecting a performance from you at all.

Do You Believe What You're About to Say?

Before you stand up to speak in public, ask yourself one simple question about every part of your talk: do I believe this? If you do, keep it and say it in your own voice, but if you don't, cut it, because a sentence you don't believe asks the audience to trust something you haven't first trusted yourself.

That's a simple test, and it's straightforward to run in the days beforehand, whatever the occasion. Read your notes aloud and listen for the line where your voice goes flat, since that may be the line you were saying for someone else's sake, and then ask whether you'd stand behind it if a friend in the third row questioned you about it afterwards. What is the one thing in your talk you'd defend without a second thought?

What to Do With Nerves

Quite a few people feel nervous before they speak, and I suspect that's a sign the occasion matters to you, not a sign that something is wrong with you. You don't have to fight the feeling, because you can turn your attention outward instead. Think about the audience you want to reach and the one important message you came to share.

When your attention is on them, there's less room left to watch yourself. The feeling needn't vanish, because you're giving your energy to the conversation instead of your own image. Ask yourself what you'd say to these people if you'd forgotten every slide, and whether you'd still know what you came to say.

If you lose your place halfway through, you can simply say so. A sentence such as "give me a moment to find my thread" is honest, and it's a perfectly ordinary thing for a person to say. A pause costs you less with an audience than pretence does, so you keep their trust by remaining openly and calmly yourself, even when the moment feels uncomfortable.

How to Speak in Public Without Putting On a Show

A show invites the audience to admire you, while sincerity invites them to listen to you. From the back row the pair may look similar, but they feel different from the front. When you're putting on a show, part of your attention goes to how you're coming across, whereas when you're being sincere, all of it goes to what you're saying and to the people who are hearing it.

So be the same person at the microphone that you are in the car park afterwards. If you'd joke with a friend about something, a light word may belong in your talk, and if you'd never say a line to someone you respect, leave it out of the talk as well. Your words need to match what you think, and people usually hear the gap even when they can't name it.

Why an Audience Trusts a Sincere Speaker

Aristotle wrote about this, and in the W. Rhys Roberts translation of his Rhetoric he said that we believe good men more fully and more readily than others. I read that as a reminder that an audience listens to the person as well as to the points. They're quietly considering whether this is somebody whose word they can rely on.

That takes some pressure off, because it means you don't have to sound impressive, only recognisably yourself and sincere in what you say. How would it feel to walk off having said only what you believe? Give that question a little consideration before you next speak in public, and let your answer influence what goes on the first slide.

Sincerity also becomes visible in the ordinary choices around the talk itself. You can thank people in the words you'd use with a friend, and you can admit what you don't know without apologising for it. When someone asks a question, answer the question they've asked, and say so if you don't have an answer. It's an unassuming, steady and respectful way to speak in public, and the only thing it needs from you is honesty.

Questions to Ask Before You Stand Up

A few questions are worth asking yourself beforehand, and you can do it on the way there. What is the one thing I mean, and do I believe every sentence I'm about to say? Write your answers down in your own words and keep them short, since a short answer tends to be easier to mean.

Then say your first answer aloud to one person, perhaps a friend or a colleague, and notice whether it sounds like you. If it does, you're nearly there, but if it sounds like a script someone else wrote, go back and change the parts that aren't yours. You're allowed to stumble on the day, because a sincere talk that stumbles once can still reach people better than a polished one nobody believes.

Afterwards, consider how much of the talk was really yours. Think about the moment you felt most like yourself, and about any moment you were reaching for somebody else's voice. Neither answer is a verdict, because both simply tell you where to begin next time, and that's all a question like this is for.

Practise in Small Rooms

Sincerity is practised, and you can start well below a stage, for instance by saying what you mean in one sentence at your next meeting and noticing how it feels to mean it. Do the same over a cup of tea with a neighbour, in the next telephone call you'd usually rush, or in any conversation you would normally hurry through.

Why Do We Make Emotional Decisions and Then Justify Them With Logic?

For about fifteen years I worked in semiconductor physics research, and I was passionate about it. The equations were elegant, the laboratories were impressive, and when a device behaved the way the theory said it should, or a laser I'd made shone brightly, it felt like a small glimpse of how things really are. I believed that if we understood the universe well enough, life would get better for everyone.

What I hadn't spotted was that many of our decisions don't go through anything like an equation. It was decades later that I realised we're not entirely logical, and that we often act on emotion and justify our actions afterwards with logic. I'll call these emotional decisions here: the choice is made first, and the tidy reasons turn up later.

What the Laboratory Taught Me

In a laboratory the measurement wins. A wishful reading doesn't survive for long, because the numbers don't care what you hoped. I suspect I carried that confidence home without noticing, as if my own choices came out of the same clean process. Everyday life has no such referee, and nobody repeats our decision with the feelings switched off to see if it still comes out the same.

A careful person who would never fudge a graph can still buy a car because the colour felt right, then list its mileage, its tax band and its boot space as the reasons. They aren't lying, because the reasons are real enough, but those reasons weren't what moved them in the first place. Have you ever caught yourself doing the same?

Why Emotional Decisions Come Before the Reasons

I read it this way: a feeling is quick, and a considered reason takes a little while to construct. By the time anyone asks us why we chose something, the choice is already sitting there, and our reasoning gets called in to explain it. From the inside it doesn't feel like making excuses but like thinking, which is exactly why it's so easy to miss. That's a common pattern behind emotional decisions: the feeling decides, and the reasoning writes it up.

You probably know someone who says "I'm only being practical" about a choice that happens to spare them an awkward conversation, and most of us have said something like it ourselves. None of this makes feelings the enemy. I'd say a decision with no feeling behind it is one nobody cares about. The trouble starts when we mistake a feeling for a finding, because "I just know it's the right job" is a feeling, and it deserves a look rather than a verdict.

Emotional decisions can feel exactly like careful thought, and that's the part worth watching. When the feeling arrives first and the reasons follow, the reasons don't announce themselves as late. They simply stand there looking sensible, and we nod along because they sound like us.

An Old Observation

The Scottish philosopher David Hume put the same point bluntly in his Treatise of Human Nature, where he calls reason "the slave of the passions" and says it can never claim any role except to serve and obey them. You can read the line in its place in Hume's own text, and nobody has to agree with every word of it. It was written close to three hundred years ago, so this isn't a modern fashion or a personal failing.

People have noticed it for a very long time, and noticing it has never been a sign of a weak mind. Doesn't it take a fairly sturdy mind to look at its own reasons and wonder where they came from? So there's no need to feel embarrassed about your emotional decisions. We all make them, and the interesting question is what to do once we've seen it.

Catching It in the Act

These days, when I notice a reaction in myself that seems out of proportion to what's happened, I look into where it came from. I bring the curiosity I used to bring to experiments. A big feeling set off by a small thing is a useful clue, because it suggests the small thing isn't the whole story.

The reasons we can list quickly are then worth a second look. They may be perfectly good, and there's no call to throw them out. But a reason that arrives in half a second, fully dressed and with a neat conclusion, is a bit like a result that matches the prediction too well. I'd imagine a physicist learns to be wary of a result that matches the prediction too well, and the same wariness suits our own emotional decisions.

This isn't a technique so much as a change of attitude. Instead of asking whether your reasons are good enough to defend, you can ask what you felt when you first made up your mind, and treat the answer as one more piece of data. It's a kinder way to be wrong, and it makes changing your mind feel less like losing an argument and more like improving a measurement.

When a Feeling Is Worth Listening To

Seeing the order of things doesn't mean distrusting every feeling we have. Sometimes a feeling is carrying something useful that the reasons haven't caught up with yet, such as a worry about a plan that looks perfectly sound on paper. The skill is to hold the feeling and the reasons side by side and let each one question the other. When both point the same way, you can go ahead with more confidence than either would give you alone, and when they don't, it's a sign to look more closely at what sits underneath the decision.

A Small Habit to Try

Pick one decision you've already made, something ordinary like a purchase, a reply you sent or a plan you agreed to. First ask what you felt in the moment before you decided, and see if you can name it clearly. Then ask whether you'd have given the same reasons if you'd wanted the opposite answer. Last, ask how big the feeling was next to the thing that set it off.

You won't always get tidy answers, and you don't need to, since most of the value is in the asking and each attempt makes you a little quicker at recognising the order in which things happened. If a decision carries real weight, such as money or a big move, it's sensible to discuss it with somebody who understands the subject. You might write your answers down, since a notebook keeps us honest and gives us a record of how we really decided.

Try it first on a low-stakes choice, because small emotional decisions are easier to look at calmly than big ones. What you chose for lunch will do perfectly well. Once the questions feel natural there, you can bring them to the bigger choices, where the feelings run stronger and the reasons arrive faster.

Thinking a Little More Accurately

My own wish is a modest one. I'd be glad if a few more of us could see our own thinking a bit more clearly, since that tends to make us kinder to other people's. A companion read is Are You a Rational or Emotional Thinker?, which looks at which of the two tends to be in the driving seat.

How Do You Question Assumptions Without Being Stubborn?

How do you question assumptions without being stubborn? My own answer comes from my years as a physicist, fifteen of them spent on semiconductors, working alongside colleagues. Some of those colleagues insisted that a p-type Schottky device couldn't be created, and I went on to make it. Here is how I think about that, and what I'd pass on to anyone who has been told that something simply can't be done.

Here's the short version. When my colleagues insisted the device couldn't be made, something in me questioned not their expertise but the boundary they had accepted. That's a small difference in wording, yet it changes how you go about the whole thing. It's the thread I'll follow for the rest of this page.

Question Assumptions by Testing the Boundary, Not the Person

Questioning a boundary doesn't conflict with respecting the people who know where it is, because the boundary and the person are two different things. What I looked at was the line my colleagues had drawn around what could and couldn't be done, and the open question was whether every possibility had really been tried. In my physics years I found myself drawn to questions that others considered settled or impossible, and this was one of them.

The way I'd picture it is a fence across a field. People stop walking at the fence because everyone before them stopped there, and after a while nobody remembers who put it up. To question an assumption is to walk up and have a good look at that fence, kindly, without shouting at whoever built it. You might find it's solid, or you might find a gate.

I Wasn't Smarter, Only Less Convinced

I'll say it as simply as I can: I wasn't smarter than my colleagues; I was simply less convinced that we had tried all possibilities. I'd read those words as a claim about doubt rather than about being right: I wasn't certain the matter was closed. Being less convinced is a modest place to stand, and a useful one, since it leaves room for the evidence to speak. When I developed a form of velocity modulation transistor, the breakthrough came from relaxing a technical condition rather than from striving harder against it.

I also developed a counter-intuitive form of surface-emitting laser using a novel etching technique. If you're curious what that family of lasers is, the encyclopedia at RP Photonics explains that the beam leaves the surface of the chip rather than its edge. You don't have to follow the physics to follow the point, which is that an idea can look counter-intuitive simply because the usual route has been the only one tried.

None of it was driven by ambition or competition, and all of it came from a fascination with what might be possible. That's a gentler motive than winning an argument, and it's one anybody can borrow. If you ever need to question assumptions among colleagues, curiosity is a good thing to bring along.

How Is That Different From Being Stubborn?

I can see why a story like mine might be called stubbornness. From the outside, a person who keeps going after colleagues have said no does look a bit stubborn. In my own book I wrote that it wasn't mere stubbornness but a willingness to see possibilities where others saw limitations. It's worth a closer look, because the two can look alike from a distance.

I read it like this: stubbornness defends an answer, while questioning an assumption asks a question. A mind that is only defending isn't looking at anything new, and nothing anyone says can change it, whereas a mind that is asking is ready to hear the reply, even if the reply is that the colleagues were right all along. So here's a gentle question for the next time you dig in your heels. Are you protecting a conclusion, or finding something out?

Small Problems Count Too

You don't need a semiconductor to question assumptions, because everyday life is full of small ones. I fixed a hi-fi system's door closer with nothing more than a paper clip. The usual route would have been to replace it or find a specialist repair, but looking closely showed that a simple bent wire could make it work. In the same way I found a safe method of glazing old greenhouses with used polycarbonate sheets, because the usual thin glass and spring clips weren't child safe.

The size of the problem matters little, because the habit of looking is the same whether you're designing a semiconductor device or fixing a hi-fi door. Anyone can try it with a drawer of odds and ends, a little patience and a willingness to question assumptions about what a thing is for.

Respect the Expert While You Ask

Respect and questioning sit together more easily than people expect. An expert has usually spent years learning where the boundaries are, and that knowledge is worth having, so listen to it carefully before you ask anything. When you do want to ask, put it as a real question, such as "has anyone tried this another way?", and not as a quiet verdict that they must be wrong.

If the answer comes back that it has been tried and here is why it failed, you've learned something valuable and lost nothing. If the answer is that nobody has tried, then you may have found a gate. You can question assumptions better when you ask warmly, because a curious tone invites help where a clever one invites defence.

A Test to Run This Week

Here's a two-step test, and you needn't run it out loud. When someone tells you that something can't be done, first ask what exactly is being called impossible and what has been tried so far. Then ask whether every possibility has been looked at or only the usual ones, and be ready for the answer to be that you were wrong.

Run it on something small first, such as a household rule nobody remembers agreeing to. Asked kindly, the test lets you question assumptions without questioning anyone's character, and it works as well at the kitchen table as it does in a meeting. Choose something where being wrong costs very little, so the habit has room to grow before it meets a bigger question. If the rule turns out to have a good reason, you've lost nothing, and if it doesn't, you may have found a gate.

Assumptions between people are a good place to practise, and the page How Do Assumptions Damage Your Relationships? looks at what they do there, while What Does It Mean to Think Objectively? is another page to read if you'd like to think about seeing things as they are.

Staying Curious About the Next Impossible

What I take from the device is a quiet lesson: I was less convinced than my colleagues that we had tried everything, and the device got made. I'd invite you to pick one thing you've always taken as fixed, be a little less convinced about it this week, and see where a calm look at the fence takes you.

How Do You Think Critically About What You Read and Hear?

How do you think critically about what you read and hear? I've had to work that out twice: first in my semiconductor research days, and then in psychology, where I had to decide for myself which approaches deserved my attention. I'm no authority on how anyone else ought to approach it, but I can pass on the plain questions I've come to lean on.

Ask Where a Claim Comes From

The first question I'd put to anything is where it came from. A headline, a post, a confident voice at a dinner party: each one arrived from somewhere, and it helps to know where. Did the person observe this for themselves, or are they simply repeating something that they were told? It's a small question, and it costs absolutely nothing to ask, although the habit of asking it can take some practice.

A claim often arrives in two layers: what somebody saw, and what they've made of it. I'd try to separate the two, because the first layer is usually more reliable than the second. You can also ask who gains if you believe it, though I'd treat the answer as a clue and not a verdict. None of this makes you unfriendly, and most people are perfectly glad to be asked where they found something.

Many of my fellow physics students and I began our careers with the same basic values, which meant finding out what's true by looking carefully and thinking the results through. I've found they carry over to everyday reading better than I expected, and they're the first thing I'd offer anyone who wants to think critically about a claim. David Hitchcock, writing in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, describes critical thinking as careful thinking directed to a goal, and I like how modest that sounds.

Test a Claim the Way a Goldsmith Tests Gold

There's an old image that I've always found memorable. A goldsmith who's handed a piece of gold doesn't simply take the seller's word for it. He cuts it, rubs it and melts it, to see whether it's what it claims to be. Nobody is accusing the seller of dishonesty, and real gold has nothing at all to fear from any of that careful examination.

Alongside that image goes some old advice that I like just as much, which is not to accept anything on blind faith, not even from the person who's teaching it. The three tests make three good questions for a claim. Cutting is asking what's underneath: what's the evidence, and does it go all the way through? Rubbing is setting the claim beside what you already know and have seen for yourself, to see whether it leaves a mark.

Melting is putting the claim under a little heat, to find out whether it still holds when somebody pushes back. A sound claim comes through all three without fuss, and a shaky one tends to show itself fairly quickly. Imagine a post telling you that one small habit will change your whole life. You'd ask what the evidence is, whether it matches anything you've noticed yourself, and what happens when someone who disagrees has their say.

Helpful Learning and Unhelpful Learning

When I moved into psychology I met a great many approaches, and I had to evaluate them critically. Not all information is of help to you, because some of it makes you wiser or clearer and some of it simply fills a shelf. Telling the two apart is what I'd call discernment, and it's a quality that's worth practising whenever you try to think critically about something you're learning.

I read it as a question about what an idea does for you once you've taken it in. Does it help you understand a person, or yourself, a little better? Does it change how you act in a way you'd be glad of? Should the answer be no, the idea may still be interesting, but it doesn't deserve much of your time.

That test applies to a book, a course, a video or a talk, and I'd happily see it applied to me. It's only my way of putting it, and you may find that a test of your own works better. What matters is that you've asked the question and not just accepted the shelf as it arrived, because to think critically is, in the end, to stay in charge of what you let in.

Stay Open When a View Challenges You

There's a second half to all this, which is that testing a claim is only fair if you're willing to be tested yourself. When I meet a view that challenges my assumptions, I try to explore it with real interest rather than defensive rejection. I don't always manage it, but I know which of the two I'd rather be doing.

A view that unsettles you may turn out to be mistaken, or it may turn out to be right in some particular way that you hadn't considered. You can't tell which until you've looked at it with some care. I suspect it's easy to test the ideas we dislike very hard and to wave through the ones we like.

Being open doesn't mean agreeing with everything, and it doesn't mean doubting everything either. It means looking properly before you decide, and the people who think critically most gracefully are the ones who can say "I'm not sure yet" without any embarrassment. If you'd like to see how easily a belief slips past as a fact, Why Do We Confuse Our Opinions With Facts? is an appropriate place to begin looking.

How to Think Critically About Tomorrow's Headline

You don't need a laboratory or any specialised equipment to try this, only a little patience. Next time something lands on your screen or in your ears and you feel a quick pull to believe it, or to dismiss it, stop for a moment and put three questions to it: where did this come from, what's under the surface and does it fit what I've seen for myself, and what evidence could shift my view?

That last question is the one I'd keep closest, because it asks you to name, in advance, what would count as good evidence against your own view. You might notice a claim you've never tested because you liked it, and you needn't be hard on yourself when you find one, since nearly everybody has a few.

To think critically about what you read and hear is to be as fair to the claim you dislike as you'd want others to be to the one you hold dear. It also helps to go slowly, because I suspect much of what reaches us is meant to be read quickly. A few quiet minutes of consideration with a claim may tell you more than a second look at the headline. There's no prize for answering immediately, and if you decide to think critically about something that matters to you, a considered reply will always be more useful than a hasty one.

How Do You Pause Before You React?

How do you pause before you react? I can only tell you how it looked for me, and it began in a classroom. I was in my teacher training, teaching a class of thirty unruly teenagers, and a mentor told me that I'd stayed "admirably calm" amidst the chaos.

That remark surprised me, and it gave me cause for hope. In my youth I'd have met that sort of disruption with frustration or anger. Somewhere along the way I had picked up the capacity to pause. I'd like to share what I understand of it, in case a little of it is useful to you.

The Gap Between What Happens and What You Do

Something happens, and then you do something about it, but in between there is a small space. A phrase I picked up from Buddhist teachers is "the gap", and it describes a brief moment between stimulus and response where there's some freedom to choose. I think most of us rush straight through it, so a habitual reaction arrives before we've had a chance to look at what is going on. Small as that moment is, it's where the freedom to choose resides, and it's available to anyone who stops to look for it.

To my mind, a reaction without a pause is a reflex, and a response that comes after one is a choice. A reflex is quick and needs no thought, and that is why it can take us somewhere we didn't mean to go, whereas a choice needs only a moment's notice and a little willingness to use it. Have you ever heard yourself say something and wondered who decided that?

How to Pause Before You React Without Switching Off

When a student was being disruptive, I felt my irritation growing, and I watched it without becoming consumed by it. That wasn't suppression of the feeling but an acknowledgement of it. The acknowledgement allowed a considered response in place of a habitual reaction. The mentor's remark mattered to me because the room was anything but calm, and I had stayed steady in it.

It helps to be clear about what this isn't, because staying calm doesn't mean pretending that nothing is wrong or pushing a feeling out of sight. The feeling is allowed to be there, and what changes is that I'm watching it instead of being driven by it. You could try it the next time something rattles you, and pause before you react by noticing the irritation as it begins and giving yourself a moment before deciding what to do next.

Not a Special State

For many years I misunderstood mindfulness. I believed it was about achieving some special state, but now I see it as simply being awake to what is, whether that is pleasant or not, and that is a much more modest idea than the one I started with. It means seeing things exactly as they are, and not as I wish them to be or fear them to be. I read that plain seeing as the ground a pause stands on.

I've found that two ideas which seem to pull against each other can sit together comfortably. One is acceptance of where we are, and the other is recognition that a great deal remains possible. When I accept the present moment fully, whether it's pleasant or unpleasant, I find that I'm paradoxically more capable of wise action that can change the moments to come.

Two Questions I Ask Myself

When I catch myself blaming outside circumstances for how I feel, I stop and ask: "What is my contribution here? What choice do I have now?" I've found that peace follows, and I've come to see this sort of self-responsibility as a liberation and not a burden.

I read them as a way of turning my attention away from what has already happened and towards what's still open. The past is history and the future outcomes aren't mine to command, so the only thing I really control is my decision in this moment. That is the place where a person can pause before reacting.

It's the quality of attention and intention we bring to the present that matters most. Each action shapes the field of possibilities for what comes next What would you choose next, knowing that? A slow queue or an unanswered message makes a gentle place to begin.

A Practice That Is Never Perfect

I wake before the sun and greet each new day with a quiet acknowledgement of how precious it is. Then I spend time in my greenhouse, getting myself ready to meet whatever the day brings with presence and compassion. I treat it as a means and not an end, a way of preparing for the day ahead, and it's never perfect. Some days my mind races through plans and worries, and other days I drift off into pleasant memories, and the value isn't in reaching some ideal state but in noticing that I've wandered and then gently returning to the present moment.

I very rarely hold a grudge overnight, and I put that down to practice more than temperament. I've learned to let resentment go, and I do it for my own freedom rather than for anyone else's sake. It lets me start the next day fresh, with a mood of optimism and gratitude.

Being Awake to Whatever You Are Doing

Being valuable as a human, in my view, doesn't mean achieving great things in the world's eyes. It means bringing a quality of presence to whatever we do, whether that is teaching unruly teenagers, conducting scientific research or simply talking with a neighbour. That seems to me to be the same skill in different clothes.

Value comes not just from what we create but from the consciousness we bring to each moment. I read that as meaning a person can begin wherever they happen to be standing, with whatever small irritation is on offer today. That moment of stillness in my greenhouse or my study is a prompt, a pattern interrupt that brings me back to the present and to self-awareness.

The practice hasn't made me perfect, and what it asks for is honest effort and the willingness to begin again. That's an invitation I can accept as often as I need to, whether the moment is a quiet one or a noisy one. Each morning offers a fresh beginning, which I think of as another chance to try the pause again, and the same is true of each ordinary moment that follows.

Is It Too Late to Learn Something New in Your Fifties?

Is it too late to learn something new in your fifties? I'd spent fifteen years working on semiconductors, I'd taught, I'd guided PhD students, and I'd set up a business of my own. Then, when I was fifty something, I realised something was missing, and I knew I had to learn about people, which meant starting again from the beginning.

Why I Chose to Learn Something New in My Fifties

I've always been drawn to understanding how and why the world works. As a boy that curiosity took me towards mathematics and the sciences at school, and later to a physics degree at the Department of Physics at Imperial College London. Among like-minded students I felt thoroughly at home for perhaps the first time, and I began my career valuing truth-seeking through careful observation. That curiosity hasn't left me, and I still find myself amazed at how anything works, and at how many people have collaborated to produce all the things we now take for granted.

Physics taught me a great deal about the material world. What it hadn't done was teach me much about people. My training had built up the analytical side of me, and the interpersonal side I had neglected. Have you ever noticed a gap like that in yourself, one that all your years of experience hadn't touched?

When someone asks me whether it's too late, I'd want to ask, too late for what? Too late to become a world champion at it is one thing. Too late to be useful to someone, or to stay curious, is quite another, and I don't know of a cut-off age for either. I only know that I started in my fifties and have continued since.

Being a Beginner Again After Years as the Expert

When I turned from physics to psychology, I had to set aside my identity as an expert and become a beginner again. That means being willing to say what you don't know. That humility is what opens us up to new understanding, and that's where beginning again starts.

Learning people skills meant practising uncomfortable conversations and facing my own social limitations. It's a different kind of effort from reading a textbook, and it asks you to be among people. Is there a conversation you've been putting off because you're not sure you'd handle it well?

When the Early Stretch Feels Slow

The old field wasn't always exciting either. Learning semiconductor physics took countless hours of careful experimentation and reading. Anything worth studying asks for a sustained enthusiasm, the kind that keeps you at it through the difficult parts, which is steadiness more than a burst of energy.

To examine that discomfort more closely, Why Is It So Hard to Step Outside Your Comfort Zone? is a worthwhile place to begin. When you picture yourself at the start of something new, which feeling would you least like to meet? Would you rather meet it early, or leave it alone?

None of this made me perfect at people, and I wouldn't expect it to. What I'd ask of any beginner, myself included, is honest effort and a willingness to begin again, whatever the circumstances. As I see it, starting again is a skill in its own right, and it gets used far more often than getting things right first time. One way to look at progress is to count the small things, such as a conversation that went a little better than the last one, or a word you finally understood.

One Deep Hole or a Hundred Shallow Ones

There's an old story about a teacher who was asked why he taught only one method when so many were available. "If you want to dig a well," he replied, "better to dig one hole a hundred feet deep than a hundred holes one foot deep." For fifteen years I dug one hole, in semiconductors, and it let me contribute something to that field. In my fifties I started a second hole, and I didn't fill in the first one.

I haven't walked away from physics. I've found that different questions need different methods, and that's something I wouldn't have understood from inside a single subject. You needn't choose between depth and variety, because a first hole and a second hole can sit side by side.

If you're feeling the pull of something new, which of your holes is the deepest? And which one would you like to start digging next? Both questions are worth a minute of honest thought, and neither has a wrong answer, so take your time over them.

Using What You Learn, and Who You Learn With

When I set up my own business, I needed to learn the practical side of domestic computers. Theory and practical application are two different things, and it was the practical application I had to understand then. I've come to think that knowledge turns into something useful when you apply it to real situations. In my physics years that meant experimental systems that tested actual theories, so the idea wasn't new to me.

It's the same with people. My developing interpersonal skills now let me support others as they grow. That's part of why learning matters, since it can flow outward to other people and needn't stop with ourselves.

It's worth asking what you'll do with a new subject once you've met it. One you only read about tends to stay a subject, while one you try out among real people has a chance of becoming a skill. What would you learn something new for, if you knew it might help someone else?

What to Ask Yourself First

How Can One Person Make a Positive Difference?

Five pieces of litter a day. That's my rule, and you might fairly wonder what good it does. A discarded wrapper here and an empty bottle there, dropped into a bag on the way to the allotment, and the path looks much the same to whoever walks it tomorrow. So how can one person make a positive difference with something that small?

I'm certainly no saint about it, because I still get cross when I find fresh litter beside a path I cleared yesterday. What I can tell you is that a positive difference is generally built from small things like that, done a little at a time by ordinary people. The path I mean is the one to the allotment, which I help to look after as a member of the allotment committee.

Why a Positive Difference Doesn't Have to Be Grand

None of us chooses the hand we're dealt at the start of life, and what matters far more is how we choose to play it afterwards, in all the ordinary hours that follow. That's a comforting thought if you've ever felt that your own circumstances were fairly modest. It means a positive difference is available to anyone who's willing to play whatever they happen to hold.

Being valuable, as I see it now, doesn't depend on great achievements in the world's eyes. It's made of ordinary choices, one at a time, that leave things a little better for ourselves and for the people around us. Choices like that are remarkably easy to overlook, because they never come with an audience, and nobody applauds a bag of litter.

I suspect they add up all the same, and they have one big advantage over grand plans: you can start this afternoon. A positive difference doesn't need a stage or a title, and it certainly doesn't ask you to have every detail of your situation worked out in advance. It asks only that you notice something that could be improved and then do a small, sensible thing about it, with a reasonable amount of care.

Begin With the Skills You Already Have

My own small acts mostly came from things I already knew how to do. I can refurbish old laptops, so I wipe the drives, install fresh operating systems and pass the machines on to local children and refugees. I can fix a bike, so when a chain breaks or a wheel wobbles, I put it right. I count digital access as close to essential these days, for learning and for keeping in touch with people.

The question I'd put to you is a simple one: what do you already know how to do that somebody near you is struggling with? It might be cooking, completing an application form, mending a hedge or interpreting a map. When my allotment neighbour grew tired last spring, I brought out the rotovator and prepared his plot without being asked.

The knowledge you've gathered over the years, for work or for enjoyment, is often exactly what another person is missing. Offering it costs you a little time and makes a real difference to somebody's week. Your own version might be considerably smaller than a rotovator, and it would still be worth offering.

A Lift to Istanbul and a Promise

Years ago I was travelling overland to Ankara with some friends, using trains and buses wherever we could find them. At the border between Greece and Turkey we found ourselves stranded, with no public transport in sight and taxis asking impossible sums. While we stood working out what to do, a stranger approached us and offered a lift to Istanbul. He wanted just one thing in return, which was a promise that we'd do the same for somebody else in the future.

The way I read it, the promise was the point, because the only way to settle that debt was to pass it on. So we accepted a lift and a responsibility together. He started a ripple, and we took on the job of keeping it moving. I think that's worth remembering whenever a small act feels too insignificant to be worth the trouble.

Moving From Consuming to Making

There's a change of outlook I've found more useful than any single good deed, and that's the move from a consuming mindset to a creating one. When we define ourselves by what we acquire, we're always waiting for the next thing to arrive. When we begin to see ourselves through what we contribute, the question shifts from "What can I get?" to "What can I make, mend or give today?"

Some of my own making is undeniably humble by any standard. I take old wooden pallets apart, pull out each nail, and turn the boards into planters, shelves and compost bins, so that what most people would see as packaging waste becomes somewhere for something to grow. None of it's remarkable, and it doesn't need to be. Creating doesn't have to mean art, either, because a solution, a friendship or a repaired chain is a creation too.

Which would you rather have at the end of a Saturday: a new purchase, or something you've made or mended that another person can use? I'm not telling you which to choose. Whichever you pick, the question deserves a quiet minute, because what we reach for on a free afternoon says something about the mindset we're living in.

A Quiet Corner at Lunchtime

When I was a school teacher, I noticed children who found the playground chaotic and uncomfortable. So I ran a lunchtime Lego club for them, as a calm space for them. The children built things, and they also developed some communication, confidence and connection along the way.

Notice how small it was. It gave those children somewhere calm to be, which is the kind of positive difference I mean. I suspect that's usually the shape of it: the need is close by, it's specific, and it's small enough for one person to meet, so you don't have to find the biggest problem in the world. You only have to notice the one in front of you.

What Small Acts Can't Do

A positive difference doesn't have to fix everything, and I don't want to oversell any of this. Picking up five pieces of litter doesn't end litter, and refurbishing a laptop doesn't end the shortage of them. None of these habits has made me perfect, either, because I still get angry at the fresh rubbish and I still lose patience with a laptop that won't behave. What I've learned is to begin again the following day, with honest effort and a reasonable amount of patience.

As a Cub Scout I was taught to do a good deed every day, and I came to see it as a practice rather than a slogan. Robert Baden-Powell, who founded the Scouts, put the same hope in his message to Scouts: "Try and leave this world a little better than you found it". That seems a fair measure to me.

Where in your week are you consuming something you could be making, mending or passing on, and if a stranger offered you a lift tomorrow in return for a promise, what would you do with it?

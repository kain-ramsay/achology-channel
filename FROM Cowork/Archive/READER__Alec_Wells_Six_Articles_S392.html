What Does It Mean to Take Responsibility When No One Is Making You? |

What does it mean to take responsibility when nobody is making you? For me, the answer begins in an ambulance garage at half past six on a mid-winter morning. The world outside is pitch black, the heavy, ice-cold rain is drumming down in sheets, and I am gathering an armful of cannulas and flushes for a truck that has been hammered all night.

The truck is weary. It needs fuel, restocking and a clean inside and out, and the controlled drug register stubbornly refuses to add up. I have fifteen mandated minutes to set all of it right before a twelve-hour shift begins, and at my station a single run to hospital can swallow two hours. Deployment can come right up to the final minute, so fourteen hours on duty is far from rare.

The crew I'm relieving look at me with a mixture of dread and hope. Nobody has asked me to start early. I do it because it's part of my professionalism, and because I'm a lead paramedic and it's expected, which makes it a voluntary act, also known as making a rod for your own back.

Responsibility Is What You Carry When Nobody Is Watching

Emergency work lives by the clock. Every moment is measured, from the response to dispatch and the time of mobilisation to the drive, the arrival at the scene and the arrival at the patient. The way I'd put it now is that duty is what the rota asks of you, and responsibility is what you choose to carry when nobody would notice if you laid it down. Those fifteen minutes were all I was given, so everything beyond them was mine to give.

Two things sat side by side that morning: my own quiet sense of professionalism, and what was expected of a lead paramedic. How would you tell them apart, in your own work or your own home? It's a gentle question, and it's worth asking before you decide what you are really carrying.

The Morning It Cost Me

The call came before I had finished restocking. It was a category one call, a collision between two heavy goods vehicles twenty-five minutes away through the dark and the rain, with an eight-minute monitored response time that blue lights could only halve. The description alone told me how serious it was likely to be. We worked that patient the way I work every job, expecting a successful handover to the hospital, and in the end I was the one who looked at my watch, noted the time and told everyone to stop.

By then I had spent twenty-five years in the emergency services, and I returned to work and attended dozens more cardiac arrests. However many patients I took to hospital, I kept coming back to that one. That morning never left me, and every time I drove through that dip in the road I could see exactly where the vehicles had been.

Over the next six years I replayed every incident I attended, laying harsher and harsher verdicts over my own work like layers of sediment, verdicts that were never even accurate. After I was assaulted three nights running while simply doing my job, I stepped back from work for a while, and by then the weight of those years had long since settled around me.

What Does It Mean to Take Responsibility Without Blame?

One line from Kain Ramsay's NLP teaching has stayed with me: "Get a cold can of grow the hell up." It sounds blunt, even brutal, but I read it as a line about the next move rather than about blame. In the hole allegory that goes with it, I found myself at the bottom of the hole.

I found the way out of it too. I got that cold can, popped the tab and listened to the hiss, then I drained it, sluicing the muck and grime from the back of my throat. I read that small, physical moment as the point where the allegory turns from describing the hole to leaving it.

Nothing I had seen, and nothing done to me at work, was mine to answer for. What I did next, and how I chose to live with all of it, was mine, including the decision to study Modern Applied Psychology and, in time, to help other people do the same. For me, that is where responsibility lives: in who holds the next move, and not in who deserves the blame.

If you're carrying something heavy of your own, please make your first call to your doctor or a suitably qualified person, someone who can look at your situation properly and in full. What I've written here is my own story from one working life, and it isn't a substitute for that help.

What My Vision Asks of Me

The word vision is a wide and generous one. It is the power of seeing with the eyes, and the power of anticipating what may come. It is also an event appearing vividly to the mind although it isn't there, and, in its fourth and gentlest sense, a vivid imaginative conception.

Recently, at an Achology workshop, I was challenged to find my collaborative value and asked, "What tone do you offer to others?" My answer was, "I am prepared to do whatever it takes to fulfil my vision; I will not give in, I will not give up, and I will go to the very bottom of the hole if that is where I have to find you." Which of those four meanings would you choose to carry?

Questions to Sit With

You don't need a road collision to find your own garage. Think of a morning in your own week that asks more of you than anyone has requested, and notice what you do with its first quiet minute. Ask yourself which responsibilities you already carry that nobody asked you to take on. Then ask which of them are really yours, and which you carry because you're afraid of what might happen if you set them down.

Then ask the harder question: what in my own life have I been waiting for somebody else to pick up, and who around me might be waiting to hear a steadier tone from me? Notice that this question leaves blame out of it and asks only who holds the next move, and whether you're ready to hold it. Give it a few quiet minutes before you answer.

Then sit with one last question, and take your time with it: if nobody were ever going to notice, which of your quiet responsibilities would you still choose to carry, and which would you finally be glad to set down? There's no rush to answer, and it's a question that keeps well overnight. Notice, too, which of your responsibilities arrived by invitation and which arrived by habit, since the two can feel exactly alike from the inside.

How Do You Stop Letting the Past Control You? |

I was holding a small family companion, a strangely affectionate and idiosyncratic soul, in a veterinary room, because I had decided to stay with him to the end. I felt I owed him that much. Yet while my body stayed beside him, my mind had wandered off to somewhere else, and to some other time. It's the clearest picture I have of a question many of us carry: what would it mean to stop letting the past control you?

What Does It Mean to Stop Letting the Past Control You?

To my mind, the past takes control when it climbs into the judge's chair and hands down a verdict before you've had the chance to look at the evidence in front of you. In that room, part of me was already wondering whether a holiday I had taken might have changed how things ended. The question came in uninvited, and it carried a gavel. Perhaps nobody had ever asked the past to sit in that chair, and I had simply offered it without noticing.

In the years that followed, I judged my own work harshly and unfairly, and I recognise that voice now. Familiar voices are easy to mistake for true ones, because they speak in the same tone as our own thoughts. If you've ever lain awake going over a conversation or a whole day, you'll know how convincing that voice can be. One gentle way to begin is to ask of any judgement you hold: who is speaking, and what evidence have they seen?

The past doesn't wait for an invitation, so stopping it from controlling us is less about keeping it out than about deciding what it may say once it's here. It can arrive in any room, at any hour, and in the middle of anything we care about. The essential work, to my mind, is learning to acknowledge it without surrendering the chair.

Patterns We Once Needed

My mentoring rests on a simple belief: a person is more than their history. You're more than what happened to you, more than the way life shaped you, and more than the ways of coping that once carried you through a hard season. I've come to think a coping pattern was once a good answer to a real difficulty, and that it may simply have outlived the difficulty it was built for.

That changes how I hear the question. To stop letting the past control you doesn't mean fighting it or pretending it never happened, because what happened stays exactly where it happened. It means noticing which of your reactions were once a shelter, and asking whether the weather they were built for is still overhead. Which of your habits of thought or feeling were once your protection, and what are they protecting you from today?

Perhaps that's why the advice to simply let it go so often feels hollow. A pattern that once did honest work for you may not step aside just because you've told it to, and it deserves some thanks before it's asked to retire. Thanking it is a way of staying on its side while you choose differently.

If you'd like to test this, think of one reaction that still surprises you today, and trace it gently back to a time when it was useful. You may find it was protecting you long ago, and isn't needed in the same way now. What you discover is yours to judge, and the gentle looking is worthwhile in itself.

Looking Honestly Without Judging Harshly

With reflection and disciplined honesty, people can begin to live with greater clarity, freedom and presence. To my mind, honesty and harshness are different things, and a stern voice in the head isn't proof that it's telling the truth. Does your harshest verdict leave any room for what you knew at the time, or for what was never yours to carry?

Reflection, as I'd describe it, is an honest look at what happened, taken kindly, the way you'd listen to a friend describe a hard day. Would you accept from someone you care about the judgements you pass on yourself? If you wouldn't, it may be worth asking who appointed you judge in the first place. Should any of this touch something painful, please sit down first with your GP or another qualified professional, because this is one life's story and no replacement for that care.

Disciplined honesty also asks for patience, because an honest account of a difficult season is rarely completed in a single sitting. It may arrive in pieces over several weeks, and each piece can be met with the same gentle curiosity you'd offer a friend. There's no deadline on understanding yourself, and nothing is lost by taking the time it needs.

What to Ask of the Past

Suppose the past could be asked for something. I wouldn't ask it to vanish, because it's part of how I became who I am, and I wouldn't ask it to be kinder than the facts allow. Perhaps I'd ask only that it tell me what's true, and then leave the verdicts to me. What would you ask of yours?

It's a small shift in who holds the pen. The past supplies the material, and you decide the next line, which is a quiet way of taking responsibility for the present without blaming anyone for what came before. You might ask the question gently, and more than once, and notice whether the answer changes as you grow more patient with yourself.

You may wonder whether all this means forgetting. To my mind it doesn't: remembering something and being ruled by it are different acts, and you can hold a memory with respect while declining to let it speak for your future. Memory can be a teacher without ever becoming a ruler, and that distinction is worth protecting.

Living Consciously in the Present

Perfection was never my aim. What I hope for, and what I hope you'll find too, is to be consciously here, in the ordinary present, free from the weight of the past and unafraid of whatever lies ahead. It's a modest hope, and I think a generous one, because the past keeps its place as something to learn from while the present becomes somewhere you can actually live.

Being present isn't a dramatic state. It can be as modest as noticing the room you're in, the person beside you and the breath you're taking, and letting that be enough for a moment. Perhaps fear of the future is the past in disguise, borrowing old evidence to predict what hasn't happened yet.

A Place to Study, and an Hour to Try

Anyone who'd like to study mindfulness in more depth will find over 33 hours of it in the Mindfulness Practitioner Diploma Course (Beginner to Expert). You can read about meeting mistakes without shame in How Do You Learn From Mistakes Without the Shame?, and about why some people stay with the past while others live for the future in Why Some People Live in the Past, and Others Live for the Future?. If you're curious about my mentoring, you'll find it at alecwells.com, and the course page has the details.

If you'd like somewhere to begin, try this for one ordinary hour. Write down one judgement you keep repeating, and read it back as though a friend had said it about themselves. Notice where the past takes the gavel, and notice what happens when you ask it, politely, to step down from the chair. You don't need a dramatic moment, only a little willingness and a few honest minutes.

How Do You Talk to Your Teenager as an Equal Without Giving Up Being a Parent? |

How do you talk to your teenager as an equal without giving up being a parent? I can only answer with a story, and it begins at a restaurant table on an afternoon when my son ordered for both of us. There comes a moment in parenthood when you glance across the table and find that the child who once babbled in a high chair has grown into an articulate, reflective and mature young person. For me that moment was startling and humbling at once, like a window opening in a room I thought I knew.

The Afternoon He Ordered for Both of Us

I sat across from my son that afternoon, and he had just ordered for the pair of us. What I noticed first was the ease in him, an unhurried way of taking part in conversation that I hadn't quite braced myself for. His observations were reflective, carrying a depth of understanding that hinted at wisdom beyond his years.

Then he asked, "Dad, have you ever noticed how people sometimes avoid eye contact when they're unsure of themselves?" He put it to me exactly as I might have put a philosophical question to a colleague, and I smiled and nodded, caught for a moment quite off guard. Here we were, two people talking about human behaviour with the grounded reflection that grows out of real observation and thought.

What I felt was connection, the kind you hope for and rarely dare to expect from someone you've spent years nurturing. Nobody was talking down to a child, and nobody was smoothing the complicated parts into something simpler. The conversation moved along easily, rich with nuance and shared understanding.

There we sat in that small restaurant, sharing a meal and laughter like old friends, and I was filled with a gentle, unhurried joy. I was looking at the person in front of me and seeing not only my son but a friend and an equal, someone who could surprise me with his insight and comfort me with his words. He was mature and self-aware, and he was also compassionate and willing to share that with me.

From Primary Teacher to Equal Partner

Parenthood has a turning point, and it's a soft one. You begin as the primary teacher and guide, and somewhere along the road you become an equal, though still an authoritative, partner in dialogue. Looking back, I suspect the role doesn't disappear so much as change its shape, the way a tree keeps its roots while its branches reach further into the light.

At that table we were, without ceremony, equals. The rules were still there, unspoken but understood, and respect was what let us share so freely. Perhaps that's what equal means inside a family: a level floor to stand on, with the walls of the house still in place.

How Do You Talk to Your Teenager Without Talking Down?

Early on I recognised that if I wanted my son to become someone I could talk with, and not just to, I would have to be intentional about how I modelled communication. Achology gifted me that. What grew from it is a young person who has been raised to speak and also to think, who knows his thoughts matter, his insights are valued and his perspective is welcome.

Ease like that doesn't arrive overnight. It's cultivated through years of open dialogue and mutual respect, and it's the product of raising someone to be heard as well as to hear. Wanting to talk to your teenager with them rather than at them, you might begin with a plain question: does the way I speak invite a reply, or does it close the subject before it has opened?

Boundaries as Markers of Respect

Raising a child to this point calls for constant boundary-setting, a delicate dance of establishing limits while leaving room for independence, and of enforcing rules without building a rigid frame that stifles curiosity or personal growth. It's a matter of knowing when to guide and when to listen, and when to step in and when to stand back. Boundaries give a young person a sense of security, and they serve as markers of respect too.

As a parent I've learned to tell authority from authoritarianism. One demands respect, and the other expects submission. What I suspect is that the first keeps a door open for the other person's voice, while the second bolts it shut.

None of this removes the need for guidance. There are still lines to be drawn and standards to be upheld, and they stand not as barriers but as signposts. They say, "I'm here, and I'm listening. I respect your thoughts, but I'm still your parent."

The Day I Asked My Son for Counsel

At some point you may find yourself turning to your child for advice, which feels both unfamiliar and right. I had been grappling with a decision about work, mentally chasing my tail around a problem that refused to be pinned down. I casually asked my son what he thought, and, with my consent, he read the message stream.

He said, "You have committed nothing to these people, and they are trying to pressure you into something that doesn't work for you. If you see it from that perspective, what happens?" A stillness settled over us after he spoke. He had put the solution into words in direct terms, free of convoluted language and bias, and in that moment I knew our relationship had evolved.

The ability to seek counsel from your own child, and to receive it, isn't something that happens overnight. It comes from having raised someone to think as well as to speak, and to know that their perspective is welcome. There's an unexpected comfort in relying on your child in this way, whether the counsel concerns relationships, career choices or the uncertainties of life. Far from shrinking my place as a parent, it seemed to deepen it, as though the threads of connection had at last been woven tightly enough to make a fabric that supports both of you.

Questions Worth Asking at Your Own Table

No special restaurant is needed to begin, because you can talk to your teenager anywhere the two of you can sit and be heard. Ask yourself when you last asked the young person in your life what they had noticed, and then waited for the whole of the answer. Ask which of your rules keep them secure, and which you keep only because you'd rather not be questioned.

Consider, too, whether the lines you draw read to them as signposts or as walls, and whether they could tell you which. Ask what it would feel like to be spoken to exactly as you speak to a respected colleague, with the same patience for a half-formed thought and the same willingness to be surprised. Those questions have no tidy answers, and they may well take a few quiet evenings.

Then ask the gentler question: if they offered you counsel tomorrow, would you be ready to hear it? For me it has come full circle. Where once I was his only guide, now he can guide me too, and that is a reward unlike any other.

What Does Leading Under Pressure Actually Teach You? |

If you've ever found yourself leading under pressure while everything around you was loud, you'll know that what matters most tends to happen quietly. I spent more than thirty years in frontline public service, with mentoring, training and operational decision-making running through them, making decisions other people relied on. Here I'd like to walk slowly through the leadership decisions on one winter call to a serious road collision, because looked at one by one they say more than the night's noise ever did.

Leading Under Pressure Starts With Where You Stand

On the drive there, one first rule kept me company: get to the patient, and do not become another patient. The road was a single carriageway with no street lighting, and I watched for the cars that would begin turning round in front of me. When I arrived, I turned the ambulance so that my way out faced clear road and the working end of the vehicle faced the casualties.

I did it without stopping to think, the way a practised hand reaches for a rail on a staircase. I've come to think that this is the first thing a leader owes the people around them: a position from which to act that doesn't put everyone at greater risk. Where do you stand, in your own work, before the pressure begins?

There was a reason for the care. Drivers who find a road blocked ahead of them will try to turn round, and many an ambulance has had its own crash within sight of its patient. I think knowing that beforehand is part of the preparation.

Telling the Truth About What Is Coming

Once I'd parked, I gave the control room a brief situation report and confirmed that backup was on its way. The wait for it was going to be long, and the air ambulance wouldn't be flying in that weather. Those were simply facts of the night, and they went into the report along with everything else I knew, because a report that leaves out the uncomfortable part only pushes the discomfort further down the line to someone who has less time to deal with it.

It's a modest act, and yet it marks a line between leading and hoping, and it lets everyone downstream of you choose their own next step with their eyes open. A report says what is known, what is missing and what help is still some way off. If you're curious how little a message needs to carry, Why Does Saying Less Make You More Influential? explores that from another angle, and it may leave you wondering which of your own messages could be shorter.

Handing the Wider Scene to Someone Who Can Hold It

A man in military uniform met me at the roadside. The UK was in combat operations at the time, so there was a fair chance he'd have combat medic experience, and the brief, concise handover he gave me confirmed it. That gave me the confidence to let him manage the wider scene, the traffic, the witnesses and the arrival of the police, while I moved on to the casualty myself.

To my mind, this was the quietest and most important decision of the whole call. His few clear words earned my trust, and once I'd given it, my attention was free for the one task that could not be handed to anyone else. Nothing about it was dramatic, and leading under pressure rarely is, which is perhaps the part most worth remembering.

Whom could you trust with the wider scene in your own work, and what would it take for you to ask? Handing something over does not make you smaller as a leader, and it frees you to do the part that is yours. The article Why Nobody Follows a Leader They Cannot Read looks at the clarity that makes that trust possible.

Reading the People Around You

Around the casualty stood a crowd of people, and I had to shoulder through and raise my voice to be heard over the competing voices. The effort it took told me something in itself. They were task-focused, working at whatever they could see to do, and they were struggling to process something far outside their ordinary days.

That was information, and it raised my own level of preparation a notch before I'd consciously decided to raise it. Some of the bystanders had been holding the casualty's head steady with great determination, which was their way of helping. If you're leading under pressure with a team, what might the behaviour of the people in front of you be telling you when a deadline tightens or plans come apart? Watching for a moment before speaking often shows more than the first instruction would, and it leaves me with a question for you: have you ever been glad, while struggling to take in something hard, that someone simply noticed?

Clear Instructions and Quick Decisions

When the time came to act, I asked my colleague to fetch the scoop or the long board, whichever was available. There was no time to inventory the vehicle, so I named the equipment I needed and left her to find the one that was there. The task was clear, its purpose was clear and the choice of tool sat with the person fetching it, which is a fair description of delegating well anywhere. You say what is needed and why it matters, and you let a capable person work out how, since they are standing closer to the shelves than you are.

Then came an instant decision to move the patient into the ambulance, because the back of the vehicle was the best place to begin the work in earnest. Underneath it was a priority every paramedic is taught, in the plainest words I know: air goes in and out, and blood goes round and round. That was my priority, and everything else was arranged around it. Which priorities in your own work are so well known to you that you needn't search for them when the moment is loud?

Choosing One to Try

Each of these choices was small: where to stand, what to report, whom to trust with the wider scene, what to ask of a colleague, what to do first. None of them required a loud voice, and each of them made the next decision easier to take. Looking back, it was less about bravery in the moment than about a run of quiet decisions made in a sensible order, each one steadying the people who had to take the next step. When you next find yourself leading under pressure, try choosing one of them, perhaps the report or the handover, and notice what changes when you make it on purpose rather than by reflex.

Why Am I Here? Finding Purpose in Life After Thirty Years of Service |

When I was asked to contribute a chapter to an anthology book about conquering fears, I found myself writing about a question that I suspect you've met too: why am I here? In my experience it's a question that everyone on a path of personal growth meets at some point, often on a still evening when the noise of the day has finally stepped aside. I'm writing as someone who gave more than thirty years to frontline public service, and I'd like to suggest that finding purpose in life may be less about locating one grand answer and more about learning to ask the question gently and honestly.

Before we go any further, I should say that this isn't a diatribe against organised religion, or against anyone's beliefs. What I'd like to offer is a single idea that I find, in some form, at the heart of every mainstream belief system: anything that is not love is a form of fear. It's a simple sentence, yet it rearranges a great deal of our understanding once we allow it to settle, so please hold it lightly for now, like a lantern carried into a dim room, while we see what it illuminates.

Why Am I Here? The Question That Finds Us All

The question rarely arrives with any ceremony. It slips in while you're washing up, or driving home, or standing in a doorway at the end of a long day, and it asks, quite politely, what all of this is for. Many of us respond by going faster, taking on more and planning further ahead, in the hope that the noise of our own activity will drown out the asking.

I'd invite you to try something different, which is to let the question stay and to become genuinely curious about it, because its arrival isn't a sign that something has gone wrong. It's one of the most natural questions a person can ask, and it deserves a patient hearing, the way an old friend does when they knock at an odd hour and you open the door anyway.

Junctures, Free Will and the Choice Between Fear and Love

Here is how I see a life unfolding. Life provides us with opportunities to fulfil our part to, with and for other people, and the decisions we make with our free will lend and lead us to certain junctures. It's then up to us how we face the situation, and we can meet it from fear, which keeps us apart from one another, or from love, which keeps us connected.

Fear can be loud, and it can also be very quiet, dressed up as hurry or as the need to be right in every conversation. If whatever falls short of love is a form of fear, then it may be worth asking which of the two is speaking when you feel the pull to withdraw. For my own part, I choose love, and I'd ask you, gently, which you'll choose.

Those three small words, to, with and for, are worth a slow look of their own. Who in your life are you doing things for? Who are you doing things with? Those aren't questions I can answer for you, and I wouldn't want to, because the decision at each junction is yours, and noticing which place you're standing in is a fine beginning.

Finding Purpose in Life Through Selfless Love

There are many words for love, and plenty of writers have explored them at length and with great eloquence. The one I hold closest is selfless, unconditional love, the kind that exists regardless of changing circumstances, and in my own opinion it's the love that allows every other form of love, compassion and empathy to exist at all. Choose whichever name for it you like, because the name matters far less than the meaning.

If that's so, might finding purpose in life have less to do with searching inside ourselves for some hidden answer and more to do with noticing where love and service meet the people who are standing in front of us? I offer that as a question and not as a verdict, and only you can tell whether it fits the life you're living. These days I mentor, facilitate training and help look after the Achology community, and I find it a natural place for that kind of love to go to work.

It may help to bring to mind a time when someone gave you their attention or their effort without asking for anything in return, and to stay with that memory for a moment while you notice how it felt to be on the receiving end. Then consider what you might pass on to someone else today, however small it is.

The One Question I Ask Myself

When I look back over the experiences I've shared in this life, there's only one question I ask myself: have I played my own part as fully as I could? As I'd describe it, it's a question about my contribution and not about the outcome, and that gives me something steady to hold when the outcome was never in my hands.

Try it on a recent day of your own, choosing one moment, large or small, and ask yourself the same question slowly and in your own words, without hurrying towards an answer. If the answer is yes, let yourself rest in it for a while. If it's not quite, you might simply notice where the next opportunity to do your part is already waiting.

If the question brings up something heavy, a talk with your own GP, or with a trained practitioner you trust, is the right first step, because they can give your circumstances the careful attention they deserve. This page is one person's perspective, offered in good faith, and it can't take the place of that care.

Reading Invictus Again

For years I read Invictus, the poem by William Ernest Henley, as a hero's tale of walking through adversity. Its last two lines still ring out: "I am the master of my fate, / I am the captain of my soul." You can find the whole poem at the Poetry Foundation, set out in full in its original form.

Now I read it through the eyes of love, with a heart in service, and the picture it paints is very different indeed. I won't tell you what you'll find there, because you may well discover something of your own in the lines. I'd simply ask you to read it twice: once as the story of a person standing alone against the night, and once as the story of what that person might be offering to the people around them. Does anything shift?

What Is Self-Leadership, and How Do You Practise It? |

Self-leadership is a phrase with a great many owners, like a coat passed from hand to hand until nobody remembers whose it was, and what is self-leadership to one person may sound like a slogan to the next. I don't wish to add another slogan to the pile. My working life has carried me through frontline public service, mentoring, training and, more recently, governance within the Academy of Modern Applied Psychology, and the phrase principled self-leadership is the one that shapes my mentoring work today. I offer my own reflection here, softly, and you're free to keep whatever rings true and set the rest down.

What Is Self-Leadership in Plain Words

If I had to say it in a sentence, I'd say that self-leadership is the steady practice of taking charge of my own conduct with disciplined honesty, rather than waiting for a circumstance or another person to do it for me. It is quiet and mostly unseen, like the first grey light easing into a room before anyone has noticed the morning, because nobody can watch the moment you decide to be straight with yourself. That's my own reflection, drawn from the words I use to describe my mentoring, and I'm not presenting it as a theory, a model or anything proven.

Several ideas sit inside that one phrase. There is self-awareness, which is the noticing; personal responsibility, which is the owning; resilience, which is the continuing; and inner authority, which is the quiet sense of being answerable to yourself. Each of them is something I care about helping people develop, and I suspect, without any evidence to offer, that they work better together than apart.

Disciplined honesty is, to my ear, a demanding pair of words, and I'd like to soften them a little. Discipline can sound severe, yet I'd hope it means returning to the truth patiently, as many times as it takes, without punishing yourself along the way. Honesty, likewise, needn't arrive as a verdict, because it can arrive as a quiet acknowledgement of where things stand today.

Why I Call It Principled

The word principled matters to me because a principle is a line drawn before the pressure arrives. Within the Academy I chair the Society of Modern Applied Psychology Board, where I support the authentication and verification of Achology's CPD, and I lead work connected to governance, trust and safety. I also host the Code of Ethics, and I help to maintain the integrity and accountability of the wider learning community.

Perhaps it's fair to say, and I offer it only as a thought, that a community can ask for integrity from its members but can't supply it on their behalf. Whether each person holds to a shared code is decided in private, one choice at a time. If that's so, then governance and self-leadership are close cousins: one is agreed together, and the other is practised alone. A code of ethics is a set of shared words, and I'd like to carry mine honestly, though I can't claim to know how anyone else carries theirs.

Leading Yourself Before You Lead Others

I facilitate public and member-only training and I support mentorship groups, and in rooms like those it would be easy to imagine that leadership means directing other people. I'd wonder instead whether it begins further back, with the plain question of who is leading the person who leads. I recall deciding, in my own family life, to be deliberate about the way I modelled communication, and I mention it as one small example of self-leadership turning up in an ordinary place.

Trust seems to me to grow from that sort of consistency, though I'd gladly be corrected on it. What is self-leadership, if not the thing that makes a leader honest with themselves? A group led that way has, at the very least, a leader who isn't hiding from their own mistakes. You might ask whether the same is true in your own circle, whether that's a family, a team, a friendship or a quiet room of one.

What Practising It Might Look Like

I won't offer a programme, and nobody should promise you that a handful of questions can do the work that a life must do. What I can offer are some questions, each borrowing a word from my own description of this work. On the side of self-awareness, you might ask what you're feeling right now and what that feeling seems to want from you. On the side of personal responsibility, you might ask which part of a present situation is yours to answer for, and which part belongs to somebody else.

Disciplined honesty invites a slightly braver question: is there something I've been telling myself that I wouldn't say aloud to a trusted friend? Resilience asks a gentler one: when something doesn't go the way I'd hoped, what is a small next step that's both kind and honest? Inner authority asks perhaps the quietest of all: whose voice am I following as I make this decision, and is it mine?

Take these slowly and in your own time, and please treat them as reflection and not as treatment. Should any answer feel heavy or frightening, a conversation with your own doctor or another qualified person will serve you far better than an article can. There's no hurry, and there's nothing here that you need to get right.

Small Places to Look

What is self-leadership for in an ordinary week, given that it rarely announces itself, and where might you look for it in small places? Think of a conversation you're dreading, and ask what a respectful and honest version of it would sound like. Think of a commitment you made, and ask whether you're keeping it because it still matters to you or only because you said yes. Think of a decision you're avoiding, and ask what you'd need to know, or to accept, before you could make it calmly.

Trust and safety, which form part of my governance work, also have a personal side that I find worth a question. Do you feel safe being honest with yourself, and what would make that easier? Are you someone you'd trust with a difficult truth?

Those questions have no right answers, and I'd sooner you sat with them than hurried past them. If you find an answer that surprises you, treat it with the same courtesy you'd offer a friend. Let it be information before it becomes a judgement, and let it stay gentle for as long as you need.

Self-Awareness and the Limits of Looking Inward

Self-awareness appears in my description of this work for a reason, and Self-Awareness Is the Starting Point of All Real, Personal Growth explores that idea in full. What is self-leadership without honest noticing for it to lead? I suspect very little, whereas with that noticing there's at least a clearer picture of where you're standing.

There's a fair counter-question too, about whether attention can turn inward too often, and Can You Be Too Self-Aware? takes that up gently. The aim in my mentoring is lives that feel authentic, sustainable and self-led, which I take to mean lives in which awareness serves the living and doesn't replace it.

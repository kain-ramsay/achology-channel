Question 10 · keyword: how to practice mindfulness · about 1,195 words

How do you start practising mindfulness, and how often?

You've just pulled into the car park at work and switched the engine off, and you're sitting with both hands still on the steering wheel for the two minutes before you go in. Your mind has already run ahead to the first meeting and the emails waiting for you, while the car itself is quiet and the windscreen is slowly misting over. It's fine to wonder whether a pause like that could be the start of anything, or whether mindfulness needs a cushion, an app and half an hour you don't have.

If you've searched for how to practice mindfulness, the answer from the sources is that you begin with one ordinary moment, such as those two minutes, and you give it your attention on purpose. How often and for how long is where the sources differ: the NHS page gives no figure, Mind says regular short periods can work better than occasional long ones, and the UMass Center for Mindfulness sets between 45 and 60 minutes of homework for its eight-week courses.

How to practice mindfulness: what is the first step?

The NHS page on mindfulness puts the first step like this: "Reminding yourself to take notice of your thoughts, feelings, body sensations and the world around you is the first step to mindfulness." That means you don't have to add anything to your day yet, only to give a little more attention to what's already in it. The same page suggests choosing a regular time for this, for example the trip to work or a stroll during the lunch break, and deciding that during it you will be aware of the sensations around you.

You might use the two minutes in the car for exactly that. You could feel your hands on the wheel and your back against the seat, and listen to whatever sounds are near the car, until it's time to go in. If the car park isn't part of your day, any short and familiar stretch will do, such as the walk from the front door to the gate.

If the day is crowded, Mind's page on mindfulness exercises and tips suggests choosing just one or two daily activities to do mindfully, and it says: "Try to build up your practice bit by bit." You needn't change your whole morning, and hanging out the washing would do as well as the car park.

How often should you practise, and for how long?

If you're asking how to practice mindfulness every day, each source is worth reading for the kind of page it is. The NHS page names no number of minutes or days, and Mind's page says: "Regular, short periods of mindfulness can work better than occasional long ones." The free meditations page on the Oxford Mindfulness website lists four free guided practices by name and invites you to take a few minutes to practise, without giving lengths in minutes.

The UMass Memorial Health Center for Mindfulness lists between 45 and 60 minutes of homework for its MBSR and MBCT courses, which it teaches in weekly classes. Our article on MBSR and MBCT: what are they, and how are they different? sets out how those programmes run, and if you're beginning on your own, you're welcome to choose a length that fits your day.

Two of the four pages mention regularity, in the NHS's regular time and Mind's regular short periods, and only the UMass page uses the word daily, for assignments inside its own courses. You might picture a houseplant on the kitchen counter, given a small glass of water each morning where another might be given a large jug once a month. You could try the two minutes in the car on the days you drive in, see how that sits with the rest of your week, and add time only if you'd like to.

How to practice mindfulness when your mind wanders

In the car, a wandering mind might mean noticing that you've started rehearsing the meeting, and then returning to your hands on the wheel. The NHS page, under "watch your thoughts", suggests regarding thoughts as mental events that come and go and not trying to get rid of them. On any day when your thoughts run ahead, you can do the same, and there's no need to push them away.

Body scans, mindful eating, walking and breathing are each a practice you can try later, and our article What are mindfulness skills, and how do you practise them? describes them. You're welcome to stay with the car park for as long as it suits you.

Some people find a practice uncomfortable, and the research on that is laid out in our article Can mindfulness be harmful, and who should be careful? You can open your eyes, look around you, or stop altogether whenever a practice becomes too much, and there's no need to finish a sitting that has started to feel difficult.

When is a practice not the right help?

Mindfulness practice is education and self-help, and for anything beyond that it's a health professional you'll want to talk to, such as your own doctor. If a low or worried mood stays for several weeks, or starts to keep you from sleeping well or from getting on with everyday life, please see a doctor or another qualified person nearby. If you or someone close to you may be in danger, please contact the emergency services where you live at once.

What are the real benefits of mindfulness, and the limits?

A neighbour leans over the garden fence and tells you she has begun a mindfulness class, and she looks well. It's fine to be curious without being convinced, and there's no need to decide anything until you've seen what the sources say.

The benefits of mindfulness that people describe include more patience and more awareness of their own feelings, and the NHS says that paying attention to the present moment can improve your mental wellbeing. What a large trial in schools found

Picture 85 schools, with a coin toss deciding which of them teach a course of 10 mindfulness lessons and which carry on with their usual social and emotional teaching. A year later, the students, who were aged 11 to 14, answer the same questions in both groups. That's the design of a large trial of a school mindfulness programme, whose abstract you can read at a university repository.

The abstract says that the analysis covered 84 schools and 8,376 students, and that it "found no evidence" that the mindfulness programme did better than the usual teaching at one year. That held for each of the three main measures it names. The authors write that their findings "do not support the superiority" of the programme over usual teaching, and they say there's a need to ask "what works, for whom and how".

The trial tested one set programme with 11 to 14 year olds, so it can't speak for a class for adults like your neighbour's. Its abstract also says there's a need to consider "key contextual and implementation factors", which points to how and where a programme is taught. You might keep that beside a friendly letter when you ask what the benefits of mindfulness could be for you.

Question 12 · keyword: mindfulness skills · about 1,170 words

What are mindfulness skills, and how do you practise them?

It's a Sunday afternoon, you've searched for mindfulness techniques, and your screen now shows a list so long that the scroll bar has shrunk to a sliver: body scans, breathing exercises, mindful colouring and walking meditations. You've opened a dozen tabs without reading any of them, and you might be wondering "what are mindfulness skills" underneath all these names, and which one is meant for somebody just starting. Feel free to close most of the tabs and keep one.

Two abilities sit under nearly every practice on that list: noticing what's here right now, such as a sound or the feeling of your hands, and bringing your attention back after it has wandered off. That's a reading drawn from several sources, and none of them defines mindfulness skills in these words. The NHS and Mind in the UK, and the Mayo Clinic in the US, each describe simple practices in their own words, and you're welcome to try whichever one appeals to you.

What are mindfulness skills? Noticing, and coming back

Start with the NHS, which describes both mindfulness skills in one sentence: mindfulness meditation, it says, means sitting in silence while you attend to your thoughts, the sounds around you, your breathing or parts of your body, and returning your attention each time your mind wanders. Mind gives a matching instruction for its meditation exercise: when your mind starts to drift, try steering your attention back to what's happening now. Say you're halfway down a page and realise you haven't taken in the last paragraph. The moment you notice, and go back to the start of it, is the coming back.

Noticing is just as ordinary as coming back, and the NHS says that we can "notice the sensations of things", which could be something as small as sunlight lying across the wall beside your desk. Look at where it's brightest and where it fades, and watch it change when a cloud passes.

These short practices are a starting point and not a taught programme. NHS Scotland's Right Decisions service lists ten "core practices you will encounter on an 8-week mindfulness course", among them a body scan, mindful movement and a mountain meditation, and it says they are "considered to be the foundations for a regular mindfulness practice", which is a lot to take on at once, so you needn't try that many.

The body scan and mindfulness breathing

Mind describes the body scan as a practice where you "move your attention slowly through different parts of your body", starting at the top of your head and going "all the way down to the end of your toes". Mayo Clinic says you can lie down or sit comfortably, begin at your head or at your toes, and "avoid judging your feelings and thoughts".

Picture the end of a long day, with you lying on the rug in the spare room and your arms by your sides. You start at your toes and notice that they're warm inside your socks, then you move slowly up through your ankles and calves. When you catch yourself thinking about the washing up, you go back to the part of your body you'd reached, and there's no need to feel bad about the detour.

Mayo Clinic's mindfulness breathing starts with sitting comfortably somewhere quiet, with your eyes closed or, in its words, open "if it's more comfortable". You breathe in "a long, deep, slow breath through your nose", breathe out through your mouth, repeat that, and focus on your breath. Press pause and go back to your usual breathing if this feels uncomfortable.

Mindful eating and mindful walking

Mind describes mindful eating as "paying attention to the taste, sight and textures of what you eat". Take one segment of an orange, and look at how the light catches its skin before you begin. Notice the smell as you peel the pith away, then bite and follow how the taste changes as you chew. If your mind drifts to your inbox, bring it back to the next segment, since there's no rush.

For walking, Mind says that on a mindful walk "you might notice the breeze against your skin, the feeling of your feet or hands against different textures on the ground or nearby surfaces, and the different smells around you". Mayo Clinic asks you to "walk slowly and notice the feeling of each step". Try it along the hallway to your kitchen, slow enough to feel the floor under each foot, and stop when you reach the door.

Where to begin, and when to be careful

Pick the practice that sounds the least like hard work, and stay with it for a few days to build your mindfulness skills before you try another. Keep your eyes open and look around the room if a practice becomes upsetting, and press pause whenever you like. Practices can bring up feelings you weren't expecting, and the article Can mindfulness be harmful, and who should be careful? reports what published studies found, so read it before a longer sitting if you'd like to.

If you'd like a guide, the answers to Course, book or app: how should you learn mindfulness? and Can you learn mindfulness online, or even for free? cover where to look, and that question can wait for another day if you'd rather practise first.

Practising mindfulness skills can't take the place of talking with someone qualified. If your mood has been low or worried for several weeks, or has begun to disturb your sleep, please book a talk with a doctor or another trained professional in your own area, and reach out immediately if you think you or someone close to you might be in danger.

Findahelpline.com holds verified helplines for more than 175 countries, and you can search it for one near you. In the UK, for instance, you can ring Samaritans on 116 123, free of charge, day and night, call NHS 111 if you need medical help immediately, or dial 999 in an emergency, while in the US the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline can be reached by calling or texting 988.

Question 13 · keyword: zen vs mindfulness · about 1,143 words

Mindfulness, Zen or meditation: what is the real difference?

On the noticeboard at your local gym, the weekly timetable lists yoga, mindfulness and meditation in three neat rows, and you wonder whether they're three different classes or one class with three names. You might have heard someone mention Zen, vipassana or Transcendental Meditation as well, and you wonder where zen vs mindfulness would fit on that same timetable. You're welcome to photograph the timetable and think it over at home, since there's no need to settle it at the reception desk.

They're related, but they aren't the same thing, and each group behind them describes itself in its own pages. Jon Kabat-Zinn's 2003 paper describes mindfulness as awareness that comes from paying attention in a certain way. Zen, vipassana and Transcendental Meditation are each described by their own groups as set practices with their own steps, while a breathing exercise for calm is different again, because it asks you to let your breath flow deep into your belly without forcing it. Each source describes only itself, so none of them can say which is best.

Zen vs mindfulness: what each one's own words say

In his 2003 paper, Jon Kabat-Zinn defines mindfulness as awareness that comes from paying attention "on purpose" and "nonjudgmentally", in the present moment. The UK's NHS says it in fewer words: mindfulness is noticing, moment by moment, what is happening both inside you and around you. Kabat-Zinn adds that meditation techniques, "however important and essential, which they are, are also merely launching platforms or particular kinds of scaffolding". So the techniques are a way in, not the whole of mindfulness, rather as a recipe is a way into tasting your food.

For the Zen side of zen vs mindfulness, the Soto Zen website, published as SOTOZEN.COM, says that "The essence of the Soto Zen School was transmitted from China, eight hundred years ago, during the Kamakura period by Koso Dogen Zenji". Its steps for zazen, which is Zen sitting, ask you to sit upright with your eyes slightly open, and they say "Do not try to control your breathing". Say a friend invites you to sit the way that page describes, with your back upright, your eyes slightly open and no effort to steer your breath. That's one school of Zen, so its pages describe that school and can't speak for the rest.

Kabat-Zinn writes that mindfulness is "the fundamental attentional stance underlying all streams of Buddhist meditative practice", and he names the Mahayana (Zen) schools of Vietnam, China, Japan and Korea among those streams. That's his account as a writer on mindfulness, and it isn't a statement from any tradition, so you're welcome to read his paper yourself for the sentence in full.

Vipassana and Transcendental Meditation in their own words

Say a friend tells you she has booked a weekend retreat to learn vipassana, and you'd like to know what the word means. The Insight Meditation Society says on its About Us page that its teachers offer guidance in "Buddhist meditations known as vipassana (insight)". It adds that the context is the Buddha's teachings, and that "these practices are universal". That page doesn't compare vipassana with mindfulness, so it can't answer that.

The Transcendental Meditation (TM) website sounds closer to a course than a book. It says the technique "can only be learned from a certified TM teacher", much as you might learn to swim with a teacher and not from a book. It calls TM a technique that brings about relaxation "without any effort", and it says TM practice "is not a religion". It also says the international TM groups have teachers in more than 100 countries.

The TM website never uses the word mindfulness, so it doesn't say how TM compares with it. Whether any of these fits your own faith is for you and your own tradition to decide, and our article Is mindfulness religious, and can anyone practise it? explains why that question belongs to each faith's own teachers.

Mindfulness or relaxation: how do they differ?

Relaxation exercises are a third kind of thing altogether. The UK's NHS breathing exercise page describes a calming breathing technique that takes just a few minutes and can be done anywhere. It asks you to let your breath flow deep down into your belly without forcing it. Say you're waiting for the microwave to finish and you spend that time letting your breath sink low into your belly: that's a breathing exercise.

Compare it with what the NHS says on its mindfulness page about meditation. It involves sitting silently and paying attention to things such as thoughts, sounds and the feel of your breathing, then returning your attention each time the mind wanders off. In the same microwave moment, you'd notice your breath, the sounds in the kitchen and the thoughts passing through, and you'd bring your attention back whenever it drifted.

None of these sources says which one suits you, so how you weigh zen vs mindfulness, or any of the others, is yours to decide, ideally with a teacher of the practice you're curious about. You're welcome to try a short taster of two of them, and there's no need to commit to one for good.

These practices are ways of studying your own mind, and they can't stand in for the help of a qualified professional. If a low mood or a worry lasts for weeks, or begins to disrupt your sleep or your everyday life, please talk to your doctor or a qualified professional near you. The same goes if you think that you or anyone else might be in danger, and then the first call is to your local emergency number.

As UK examples, 999 handles emergencies, NHS 111 covers urgent medical help, and Samaritans answer without charge on 116 123, night or day. Question 15 · keyword: being present in the moment · about 1,155 words

What does it mean to be present, and why is it so hard?

You finish a long phone call, put the phone down, and realise that you weren't really listening to the last few minutes. You heard the voice and said "mm" in the right places, but what was being said has left no trace, because your thoughts had already gone off to whatever you needed to do next. If that sounds familiar, you might be wondering what being present in the moment really means, and why it slips away so easily. You're welcome to take the question slowly, since it doesn't need an answer before your next task.

Being present in the moment means having your attention on what's happening around you and inside you right now, and not somewhere else in a memory or a plan. It doesn't ask you to empty your mind, because you can think about the conversation you're in and still be present for it. As for why it's hard, one study that sampled the moments of 2,250 adults' days reports mind wandering in 46.9% of the samples, which it measured by asking people whether they were thinking about something other than what they were doing.

What does being present in the moment mean?

Imagine a friend telling you about her week across a table while, somewhere behind your face, you're deciding what to say next. You can hear her words and nod in the right places, and yet part of you has moved on to the moment when she stops talking. Being present would be listening to the sentence she's saying now, and letting your reply arrive once she has finished.

Being present doesn't ask you to stop thinking, and you're allowed to think as much as you like about what's in front of you. Thoughts about the conversation, the food on your plate or the road ahead are all part of where you are. What takes you away is thinking that has left the room, such as replaying last week's meeting or drafting tomorrow's list, and it's fine to notice that without scolding yourself.

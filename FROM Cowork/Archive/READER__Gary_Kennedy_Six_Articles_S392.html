What Is a Saviour Complex, and How Do Helpers Get Caught in It?

What is a saviour complex? The short answer I'd give is that it is a need to rescue people so that we can feel worthy ourselves, and I know it from the inside because I wore one without noticing. I've always been a listener, and for as long as I can remember people have talked to me, friends and strangers alike, which left me feeling valuable. As I listened I would also offer my advice and my little insights, and I developed something of a reputation.

How a Helper Slips Into It

Nobody deliberately sets out to become a rescuer, and I didn't either. I stopped being only a listener and became the expert, and my work as a mental health nurse fed that image. I read that shift now as a small one, because it grew out of something good, which is probably why it was so easy to miss. A helper who is trusted and thanked has every reason to believe that the road is going well, and every reason not to examine what the thanks is quietly feeding.

Later, I began to feel that I had to be the one who put other people's troubles right. It was no longer their issue. It was mine. There were times when I got caught up in their dramas, became overinvolved and, quite frankly, interfered, and I can't say that I noticed the moment it began.

What a Saviour Complex Feels Like From the Inside

It became a weight. I needed the attention, and I had to be the fixer, the guru. It was a burden, and it was also an intoxicant, which is a strange pairing, but I suspect it explains why the habit is so hard to put down: a weight we resent and a reward we crave can live happily in the same person.

That pairing is, I think, what a saviour complex feels like when you're the one carrying it. Does any of that sound familiar to you? Are you ever tired from holding everyone's trouble, and yet uneasy at the thought that nobody might hand it to you? I don't offer that as a rule about anybody else, and I'm only asking what it is like for you.

When Someone Calls You Wise

There was a moment when a client treated me as a "God of Wisdom", and I found myself leaning right into it. It didn't feel like a decision. It felt like being recognised, finally, for what I had hoped I was, and I suspect that's why the temptation is so quiet.

I had become the agent rather than the mirror. The way I'd put it is that a mirror allows people to see themselves, while an agent steps into the picture. There was no real service in it, although that is what I told myself it was. I was wearing a crown, and I was the hero of the room, not them, which is an uncomfortable thing to admit about a person I wanted to help.

Whose Story Is It?

We've all grown up with the story of a simple soul who sets out, overcomes obstacles, slays a dragon and comes home changed. Bilbo Baggins is among the most widely known, a respectable creature who overcomes his reluctance about adventure and becomes a resourceful adventurer. The Joseph Campbell Foundation describes the hero's journey as a pattern of separation, initiation and return, found in stories across many cultures.

In a helping room, the person who came in is the one on that road. I read a saviour complex as a helper quietly taking the dragon for himself, so that the other person's story begins to feature the helper as its hero. I can't prove that's the whole of it, and I'd welcome your own reading. But I notice that the crown never seemed to fit me better than when somebody else's dragon was in front of me.

Every Strength Has a Shadow

Gerard Egan, whose Skilled Helper model I've leaned on, makes a point that I've kept close: every strength has a corresponding shadow. As I understand it, a caring and capable helper can, without any ill intent, slide into being an all-knowing rescuer. The strength and its shadow aren't two different people. They're one person on two different days.

Why would a good helper put the crown on at all? I suspect it's because underneath sits a fear that we might be useless, unneeded or only a witness. If the other person's progress is where I find my sense of significance, then their progress isn't really theirs any more. I don't say this to be hard on helpers, since I count myself among them, and it's a kindness to ourselves to see that fear clearly.

Telling Care From a Saviour Complex

It would be a mistake to conclude that every helper with a generous heart has a saviour complex. Wanting to help is a good thing, and I wouldn't want anyone to read this and feel ashamed of it. What I've come to watch is the direction of the benefit: whether the help is for whoever has come to me, or whether I'm quietly using them to feel needed.

I can't give you a test that settles it, and I'd be wary of anyone who claimed to have one. What I can offer are a few honest questions. When someone brings you a problem, whose problem is it by the time you've finished, and when they thank you, how much of the warmth do you want to keep? Do you leave room for them to find their own answers, or do you fill every silence because you can't bear to be a witness?

Perhaps a fair way to think about a saviour complex is to ask whether the help could survive being entirely unnoticed. If nobody would ever know what I had done for this person, would I still want to do it? I find that question uncomfortable, which is usually an indication that it deserves a little longer, and a considered look at your own honest answer.

Questions for Your Own Helping

I'd guess you don't need to be a nurse or a priest to be caught in this, and that a friend, a parent, a manager or a volunteer can be handed the crown just as easily. If you notice a little of yourself in these pages, please take it as information and not as a verdict. I suspect noticing is where loosening the grip begins, and I also suspect that an honest outside view is difficult to replace, so it may be worth describing what you've noticed to somebody you trust, and listening carefully to what they say in return.

If you're supporting someone through something serious, please make sure they also have a doctor or another suitably qualified person beside them, because no friend or mentor, however wise, should be the sole support for a heavy load. If you'd like to read more on how advice lands, Why Good Advice Rarely Inspires People to Change Themselves is a thoughtful place to begin.

Where to Read Further

Egan's work is the subject of The Skilled Helper Training Course (with Prof. Gerard Egan). Its course page lists 28 lectures that add up to over seven hours, and the course checkout page holds the details.

Whatever you decide, I hope you will take the crown off gently when you notice it. I suspect the crown often comes from wanting to be useful, which is a fair place to start. After that, I'd rather we learned to hold our advice loosely and to let the people we help stay the heroes of their own stories, whether or not anyone ever calls us wise.

What Is Jung's Shadow, and Why Does It Matter to Anyone Who Helps People?

What is Jung's shadow? The shortest answer I can give is that it is the part of yourself you would rather not see. It matters to anyone who helps people, because a helper can be the last to notice it. I was a listener even when I was young, and somewhere along the way I became the expert as well.

Working as a mental health nurse helped to build that picture of me. I suspect an expert's chair is a comfortable place to sit, and also a very good place to hide from yourself. That's why I want to explain Jung's shadow in ordinary words, and to ask what it might mean for those of us who spend our days with other people.

What Is Jung's Shadow in Plain Words?

Jung gave his own short definition: the shadow is "the thing a person has no wish to be". You can find it in the Pacifica Graduate Institute's library guide to Jung's Collected Works, whose dictionary entry notes that Jung held that everything substantial casts a shadow. The guide also says that the shadow isn't only the unpleasant side of us, because it can hold good qualities that we have pushed out of sight as well. I'd describe it this way: Jung's shadow is whatever we have tucked away because we were afraid of what it might say about us.

It is important to notice that this concerns what we hide from ourselves and from other people, and that it has nothing whatsoever to do with being a bad person. A shadow belongs to everybody, and I doubt that anyone escapes having one, however accomplished or well regarded they may be. If you'd like a wider look at the idea before you go on, What Is Your Shadow Side, and What Did Jung Actually Mean? takes it up from another direction.

The Night Jung Was Followed

Jung once dreamed that he was being followed down a lonely street at night. The pursuit frightened him, and when he finally spun round to face whatever was following him, he discovered that it was his own shadow. We have a phrase in Britain for someone who lacks courage, that they're scared of their own shadow, and the ironic point of it is that the thing we fear has been ours all along.

Why do we run away from it, and what is it that we imagine we are avoiding? The way I see it, we hide the shadow because we don't feel in control of it, and so we keep our distance from anything we can't govern. We fear that if we owned it as a part of us, we would be abandoned, or that it would take us over completely. If I admitted my fear of being alone, perhaps I couldn't bear the idea and would fall apart, and if I let my anger show, perhaps it would turn out to be wholly destructive, and so I would rather not find out.

These aren't small worries, so we bury them deep and hope they never see daylight. If either of those feels too heavy to look at on your own, please talk it over first with a GP or some other suitably trained professional. There is no shame in asking for company while you look, and a qualified person is well placed to offer it.

Why Jung's Shadow Matters to Anyone Who Helps People

A helper lives close to other people's troubles, and that can be a fine way to avoid looking at our own. I offer this as a question rather than a rule: what might I be afraid of finding out about myself if I stopped being useful to everyone? Perhaps I'd have to sit with being unneeded, and perhaps I'd have to sit with nothing to fix and nobody to fix it for, with only the quiet that followed.

The way I read it, this is why Jung's shadow matters so much to people in the helping professions and to mentors. Whatever a helper hasn't looked at in themselves tends to travel into the room with them, unannounced and unexamined. You can't be a steady listener for another person while you are quietly managing something you haven't yet admitted to yourself, because part of your attention is always busy elsewhere, guarding the door. What would you be protecting in that situation, and who would the protection eventually cost?

The King and the Fool

There's an old pairing in stories and in real life: the king and the fool. The jester was there mainly to entertain and to make the king laugh, yet he could also say things that nobody else could, or dared, to say, because the fool shows the king what the king cannot, or will not, see. For an unwise leader, he is a danger. I'd guess a wise leader welcomes him.

The King's New Clothes tells it well. A king is tricked into believing he is wearing beautiful clothes when he is really naked, and the one who says so is a simple child. The child plays the part of the fool. In that story the truth arrives from the very person we'd least expect.

What Am I Protecting?

If we're hurt when a truth about ourselves comes out, or we move quickly to shut down someone's criticism, there's a question worth asking: what am I protecting? Most of us have rather fragile egos, and I include myself in that observation without any reservation whatsoever. The way to help an ego, I've come to think, isn't to defend it but to let it meet the truth, so that we learn acceptance instead of armour.

I see life as a journey, and the shadow is one of the things we carry along it whether we look at it or not. It may be that carrying it in the dark is heavier than carrying it in the light, though that is only my reading. I read that as an invitation and not a blame: nobody is being told they have failed, only that there is something here worth meeting, at your own pace. Jung's shadow isn't an accusation, and I'd rather we thought of it as company on the road.

Finding Your Own Fool

Who is the fool in your own professional or personal life? Perhaps it's a friend, a colleague or a supervisor who is able to say the thing that you find so difficult to say to yourself. If nobody ever makes you feel slightly naked and foolish, it may be worth asking whether you've arranged your life so that nobody can. That isn't an especially comfortable question to ask of yourself, and it certainly doesn't need an answer today, or even this month.

It is possible to try this in a small and manageable way during the coming week: the next time something a person says stings, pause before you answer and ask yourself what you're protecting. You don't have to change anything immediately, and you don't have to agree with what was said. Just notice whatever rose up inside you in that moment, because that is often where the shadow is standing.

Somewhere to Go Next

To learn more about the man who gave us the idea, Who is Carl Jung? His Biography, Ideas and Life Works sets out his life and thinking, and anyone wishing to study the subject at length and in an organised way will find it in the Diploma Course in Modern Applied Psychology (DiMAP), which comprises 51+ hours of teaching in 175 lectures. Why Do We Give Up So Easily Halfway Through Learning Something?

Why do we give up so easily halfway through learning something? It's a reasonable question, and I don't think the honest answer to it is anything to be ashamed of. The beginning of anything unfamiliar tends to be bright, with fresh ideas arriving quickly and the finish line felt as a pleasant, distant possibility. Then the brightness gradually fades, and what remains is a long stretch of ordinary, unglamorous effort.

The Long Middle Stretch

Every course and every craft has a middle, and it is seldom the part that anybody chooses to put on the front of the brochure. The early enthusiasm has worn off, the finish line isn't anywhere in sight, and the beginning is far enough behind you that looking back at it no longer feels like progress. Part of my role as an Achology elder is to support members through this particular stretch of a course, because it's precisely where most of them will need that support.

I know that stretch well, and that's partly why I'm writing about it. The middle of a course asks for something different from the beginning. The beginning runs on novelty, which does a good deal of the work for you without being asked, while the middle asks you to keep turning up when nothing is doing that work on your behalf. Have you ever noticed that the moment at which you'd most like to stop is often not a moment of failure at all, but simply a moment of flatness?

Who has already walked this stretch before you, and what would they say about it? Is there anyone you could ask, someone who has been through the flat middle of something long and come out the other side? And when you look around, does it feel as though everybody else skipped this part, or might they simply be quiet about it? Questions of this kind can make the middle of a long undertaking feel considerably less isolating.

When the Insights Stop Feeling New

Think back to the first insight that made you sit up and say that's exactly right. Has a point come when the insights keep arriving but the jolt has gone, and although they're still true and still useful, they've stopped feeling new? From where I sit as a mentor, this is the point at which most people quietly give up on something they had started.

I read that quiet giving up as something rather close to a misunderstanding. Noticing that an idea is no longer new is surprisingly easy to mistake for a sign that the learning has stopped working altogether. A new idea may only give you its initial jolt once, and what follows is the slower business of living with the idea until it shapes what you do. So I'd gently ask whether the flatness you feel is telling you that the course is failing you, or only that you've moved from meeting ideas to practising them.

If you happen to be in that stretch at the moment, try to remember what originally drew you in. Those early ideas were exciting because they were unfamiliar to you, and they remain yours whether or not they still feel exciting. Going back over a lecture that thrilled you some months ago can show you how much has changed in the meantime, even though it doesn't feel like movement from the inside.

Why We Give Up So Easily When We Fix on One Leg of the Road

Life is a journey, and I believe it is there to be lived fully, which is the view that shapes how I look at anyone who has stalled. Many people become stuck because they don't recognise life as a journey at all. They look at one leg of it, pay attention to how they feel about that leg, and gradually become fixed on the feeling itself. A dull stretch of the road can start to feel like the last word on everything ahead.

Picture a long walk between two villages, where the flat middle may well be dull and the dullness is real. Yet it only tells you about that stretch. It doesn't tell you where the road eventually leads, how far you've already travelled or what is waiting around the bend. Perhaps that explains why we give up so easily halfway: the feeling about this leg is loud, and the rest of the road is out of sight.

Notice, too, that none of this is meant as blame of any description. I'd say stopping halfway isn't a verdict on your character. It's an entirely understandable human response to a stretch of road that offers very little visible reward for the effort being put in. Seeing it that way makes room for a kinder and considerably more useful question: what would help me take the next step?

What the Feeling Is Telling You

I'm not suggesting that you argue with a feeling or try to push it away. A flat, restless or doubtful feeling in the middle of learning is worth noticing, and it deserves a fair hearing. The question I'd put to it is what it's actually about. Is it about the whole of what you set out to do, or only about this week's leg of it?

It's worth remembering that a feeling belongs to a moment, and a journey is made of many moments. The learner who feels flat on Tuesday may well feel curious again by Friday, and neither of those feelings is the whole of that person. When we treat the feeling about one leg as though it were permanent, we tend to stop walking altogether, and then the feeling gets the last word. Letting a feeling speak without allowing it to decide is a small but valuable skill, and it can be practised like any other.

Some simple questions can help you to tell the difference between the two. Does the feeling arrive with an identifiable reason, or does it simply settle over the work like weather? Would you have felt the same a month ago, when the path was new, and if a friend described this same stretch to you, would you tell them to quit, or would you tell them they were somewhere in the middle? There's no correct answer to any of these questions, and each of them is worth a few unhurried minutes.

Questions for the Middle of Your Own Road

Take something you started and haven't yet finished, and consider honestly where you are on its road. How far have you travelled since the first day, and what do you understand now that you didn't understand then? What did you originally want from it, and is that wish still yours today? If you often find you give up so easily at about this point, it may be kind to ask what the starting wish was worth to you.

Then consider what the smallest possible next leg might look like. It isn't the whole road, only the stretch immediately in front of you, and you can be completely honest that it's slow and unexciting. If the heaviness you feel is more than a dull patch in your learning, it would be wise to talk to your GP, or to someone else trained to help, before leaning on anything you've read here.

Standing Halfway, Looking Both Ways

You might find it useful to read about How Do You Know If You're Growing or Standing Still?, which approaches the question of progress from a rather different direction. If the flat feeling has spread beyond one project, What to Do When You Feel Stuck in Life? is a sensible next thing to read. How Do You Keep Going When You've Lost Motivation?

Imagine the day when something you began with considerable energy no longer pulls at you. The plan is still written down and the reasons are still good ones, yet the spark that made it effortless to begin has drained out of it. Many people call that lost motivation, and it can seem as though nothing whatsoever can be accomplished until the feeling reappears. I'd like to offer an alternative question, namely what you still have available to you today, and I should say openly that it comes from my own view of life and not from any rule.

Why Does Lost Motivation Feel Like a Full Stop?

When lost motivation arrives, what do you find yourself telling yourself about it? Is it a pause, or does it sound more like a final verdict on you and on the thing you started? Notice the words you instinctively reach for, because they show what you believe the feeling is able to decide. Have you ever handed the feeling the last word without quite choosing to?

I'd ask you to hold one more question beside that one. If the feeling were taken out of the picture for a moment, what would still be true about the work you began? Your original reasons for starting would still belong to you, and your time, your knowledge and your understanding would still be there. The feeling has departed, but the person who began the work hasn't gone anywhere at all.

Commitment or Waiting for a Feeling?

I'd suggest that commitment is a different thing from waiting for a feeling. A feeling arrives according to its own timetable, and none of us can order one on request, whereas a decision about how you'll use what you have is something you're able to make today. Where we can only hope for motivation, we are able to deliberately choose commitment. That's the nearest I can come to an honest answer about how to keep going when lost motivation has taken hold.

What would you do today if you decided to commit and not to wait, and what is the smallest piece of the work that sits entirely within your own control? And who would you be by the end of the week if you had made that same decision each morning, with no promise about how you'd feel while making it? It's a fair question whether lost motivation would matter quite so much once that decision had already been made.

Working With What You Already Have

My own view is that people thrive and grow when they commit to doing their best with what they have. That means their own resources, their knowledge, their wisdom, their insight, their understanding and their time, used on the path they're already on and not on the one they wish they'd been given. It doesn't call for a perfect plan or a sudden return of enthusiasm, and it doesn't require any special circumstances. It calls for your best, using what's in your hands today.

It can help to name those things individually, because each of them is a genuine resource. What do you know now that you didn't know when you began, and what have you understood since, even if it didn't feel dramatic at the time? How much of your time is genuinely yours today, and what would doing your best with a single hour of it look like?

Notice, as you read them, that none of these questions asks how you feel. They ask what you have and what you're prepared to do with it, and each of them has an answer available today. Nothing in them requires you to feel especially ready beforehand, and nothing in them is a test that you could possibly fail. They're simply an inventory of your own resources, taken honestly.

Consider how this compares with the alternative. Waiting asks you to hand your progress over to a feeling, and commitment asks you to hold it yourself. Neither is easy, and neither amounts to a judgement on your character or your capability. Which would you prefer to be holding when lost motivation comes round again?

The Three Shortcuts in the Wilderness

According to the gospel writers, Jesus was taken into the wilderness at the outset of his ministry and faced three temptations, and Matthew's account of the temptations in the wilderness can be read in a few minutes. None of them was about doing anything wicked, for each was a shortcut: turn stones into bread, leap from a high tower and be rescued, or take the kingdoms of the world all at once. Whatever you make of the story, the temptations it describes will be familiar to most of us.

What stands out for me is that these temptations were about usefulness and shortcuts, and not about wrongdoing. Each one offered something good, and each one offered it without the accompanying labour. I'd say that's what makes a shortcut so attractive when we're tired: it doesn't look like an escape at all, it looks like efficiency.

I'll admit the shortcuts tempt me, and I can easily picture the good that one quick wave of a magic wand could do: hunger ended, risks taken without fear of failure, everything put right at once. Yet if we took the easier option, where would the satisfaction of the struggle come from, and where would we find the learning for the day life goes wrong?

Then there's a further question, which concerns where the resilience and the pride would come from, the knowledge of what you can achieve for yourself. I read the three temptations as shortcuts that cost exactly those things: the struggle, the learning, the resilience and the pride. I think lost motivation can tempt us in the same way, because it suggests that the right trick or the right burst of inspiration would end the wait in one go. A shortcut may bring you to the destination sooner, but it can remove the very things that the effort was going to provide.

What Do You Still Have With You?

Here are a few questions to carry with you into the day ahead. What do I have with me today that doesn't depend on how I feel? Which of my own resources, my knowledge or my understanding have I stopped counting? What would doing my best look like for the next hour, not for the year?

Then ask which shortcut you've been hoping for, whether it's the magic wand, the perfect plan or the sudden return of drive. What might it be asking you to skip, and is lost motivation really the thing standing in your way, or is it the wait for it to lift?

One gentle note before you go on is that, if the flatness has lasted a long time, or it feels heavy, please find a qualified professional to speak with before anything else, a person able to hear the whole of what you're carrying. What I've shared here is one mentor's view, and it's no substitute for that kind of help.

What Does It Mean to Listen Without Fixing?

People have confided in me since I was young, friends and strangers alike. Somewhere along the way I stopped simply listening and started handing out answers, and so when someone asks what it means to listen without fixing, I don't answer from a textbook. I answer from the gap between those two habits, and I'd like to tell you how I found it.

The Listener Who Became the Expert

I was a listener before I was anything else. Even when I was young, people would sit down and tell me what was on their minds, and I felt valuable for being the person they chose to confide in. It felt good to be trusted in that way.

As I listened, I began to add my own advice and my own little insights. Slowly I built something of a reputation, and being a mental health nurse contributed to cultivating that image. Without consciously noticing, the listener in me gave way to the expert.

I suspect an expert is a comfortable position to occupy, because an expert always has something to say. Have you ever noticed that happening in yourself, when a friend begins to speak and you already feel an answer forming? It's an understandable habit to slip into, and it's worth noticing without any harsh judgement.

When Their Problem Became My Problem

There came a time when I felt that I needed to be the person who resolved other people's difficulties. The problem had stopped belonging to them and had become my own. I got caught up in other people's dramas, became over-involved and, quite frankly, interfered in situations that were not mine.

I needed the attention. I needed to be recognised as the fixer. I told myself that it was servanthood, but it wasn't, and it became a weight.

What I'd say about it now is that fixing quietly lifts a problem out of the other person's hands, because once I'd hurried in with a suggestion, the story stopped being theirs. Have you ever shared something difficult and felt it being politely organised away before you'd finished telling it?

What Does It Mean to Listen Without Fixing?

To listen without fixing is to stay with what a person is telling you and to let the story remain theirs. I read it as a change of job, because you're no longer there to solve anything, only there to understand. It doesn't mean that you care any less about the person. Often it means you care enough to wait.

That doesn't make it passive. As I've come to practise it, listening at its best means offering reflections and asking questions. A reflection, as I'd describe it, gives back what you've heard, and a question opens a door without telling anyone which way to walk through it.

A reflection might be as simple as saying, "It sounds as though that really hurt," and a question might be as simple as asking, "What's it been like for you?" Both need you to be quiet long enough to hear what's being said, and that quiet is harder than it sounds.

Why the Urge to Fix Is So Strong

I can only speak for myself in this particular matter, and I'd encourage you to test it against your own experience. When I look back, I suspect the urge to fix was partly about what a solution did for me, because a solution let me feel important and valuable, and it let me off the discomfort of simply sitting beside someone else's suffering.

If that's true of you too, it isn't a reason for embarrassment or self-criticism. You might ask yourself what you would have to feel if you offered no answer at all. What rises in you when a person you love is struggling and you cannot make it better, and how do you usually respond to that feeling?

Might it be that to listen without fixing is to accept that discomfort and to stay anyway? I'd ask you to notice what comes up in you the next time you feel the pull toward an answer. What were you hoping would happen once you'd offered your answer, and for whom were you hoping it?

Reflections and Questions in Place of Answers

You're not alone in finding this difficult or uncomfortable. Caring Magazine, which The Salvation Army publishes, carries a piece arguing for less fixing and more listening, and for someone in pain it puts sitting with them ahead of offering solutions. It makes me wonder whether a person who is hurting needs company before anything else. I'm not suggesting that you should never offer advice, only that you might ask whether the person wants it yet.

If you'd like to read about what people feel when a helper responds with real empathy, What Clients Hear When a Helper Responds With Real Empathy takes up that question. For a wider map of the skill itself, What Are the 3 Different Types of Listening? is a reasonable place to begin.

Stepping Down onto the Same Road

I've come to think of good listening as an act of stepping down from the position of the expert. It calls for humility and a genuine willingness to listen, to offer reflections and to ask questions. Good listening is nothing like climbing onto a pedestal in order to preach at someone.

Stepping down matters because it places us where we can meet the other person. We're on the same journey as the person in front of us, only on a different part of the road. Might that change how we sit with them, and what we're prepared to say?

It also helps to remember our own road when we're trying to listen without fixing. We do well to recall how we fell and rose again, what our struggles were and how we came through them, and what we valued in the people who walked with us. Which of those people comes to your mind first, and what was it about them that you remember with gratitude? What did they do that made you feel genuinely heard, and could you offer the same attentiveness to somebody else this week?

What Would It Sound Like?

Picture the next conversation in which someone brings you a problem. What would it sound like if you offered one reflection and one question, and then waited? It's a modest experiment, and it's one you can try this week in an ordinary conversation, without telling anybody what you're attempting.

Afterwards, consider whose story it had become by the time the conversation ended. Was it still theirs, or had you taken responsibility for it, as I did? And what might one good question have opened that advice would have closed?

Then think about the people who have listened to you well. What did they do, and what did they leave undone? Did they hurry you toward an answer, or let you find your own? If what you're carrying feels heavy, it would be wise to speak with a doctor or another qualified professional too, since one person's story can't stand in for that care.

How Can Faith and Psychology Work Together?

I am both an Anglican priest and a Master Achologist, so it is a fair thing to ask me how faith and psychology can work together, and I can only answer as one person. This is my own view, seen from my own chair, and it isn't Achology's position. Nor is it a view that I expect anyone else to share, since each of us arrives at these questions along a different stretch of road.

Some readers may wonder whether the two can sit in one life at all. If you're one of them, I understand the wondering, and I'd never want to hurry you past it. I suspect that the question is easier to hold when we admit how much each of us has yet to understand, and that is a good place to start.

One Person's View, Held Lightly

Achology is not a religious organisation, and nothing I say here changes that. I mention my faith only because it is part of who I am, alongside my work as a group mentor and regular host of community events. I'm not here to persuade anyone of anything, and what you believe is yours to decide, now and whenever you next think it over.

You don't need to share my faith to read on. If your own view is different, or if you hold no faith at all, I'd simply like you to feel welcome, and to keep whatever you find helpful or interesting. What would it be like to read this as a description of one person's outlook, and nothing more than that, leaving you free to agree with some of it, none of it or all of it?

Where Faith and Psychology Meet in the Quiet Place

The place where faith and psychology seem closest to me is the quiet inner work. Learning about people asks you to look at the way you think and at the reasons behind what you choose. As I read them, the gospel accounts point to a work that happens within, in a quiet place, and I'm not claiming that this is true of every faith or every approach to psychology.

That inner work is seldom dramatic, as far as I can tell. It might happen on an ordinary weekday, in a conversation that goes a little differently from the one you expected, or in a moment when you notice what you are protecting. Do you have a quiet place like that in your own life, somewhere you can look at yourself without an audience?

Servanthood in the Gospel Accounts

The gospel writers describe Jesus washing feet, offering forgiveness and mixing with people who were destitute and pushed to the edges of society. The gospel of John tells of the washing of the feet in its thirteenth chapter, and I read it as an unusual picture of leadership, because the person with the most standing is the one who picks up the towel.

As I read them, that way of living doesn't look like success. There was no monument or establishment in his name when he died, and the few disciples who were left wondered whether they had chosen rightly. I'm not saying anything here about what anyone must believe, only offering what I think I see when I read them.

What would servanthood look like if it were carried into the place where you spend most of your days, whether that is your work or your home? Would it involve forgiveness, and if it did, would you find it easier to offer that to someone else or to yourself? It's a question I'd put to anyone, and not only to people of faith.

Growth That Comes Quietly

It is easy to imagine that a significant change in a life will arrive with some kind of announcement. As I read the gospel accounts, the work that happens within doesn't come with trumpets and fanfare, but more often with humility. Do you expect your own growth to be announced by something, or could it already be under way without anyone having noticed?

This is where faith and psychology feel to me like neighbours rather than rivals. Both are concerned with what goes on inside a person, and both can be taken up quietly, without any audience. I can't say whether that will match your experience, and I wouldn't want you to take my word for it.

Two Ways of Paying Attention

Life, as I see it, is a journey, and it's meant to be spent fully and with our eyes open. Nobody lives a journey by watching it from the side, and so the sensible course is to take the next step from wherever we happen to be standing. That view doesn't belong to a church or to a classroom, and it's available to anyone.

I'm wary of the question when it is put as though faith and psychology were two opposing teams, and as though a thoughtful person were obliged to pick one of them. For me they are two different ways of paying careful attention to the same life, and I'm grateful for both of them. You may see it quite differently, and that's a perfectly good place to begin. Which of the two have you tended to trust more, and what might the other have to say to it?

Questions for Your Own Chair

Sitting with a few questions requires no priesthood and no faith, and these ones are gentle on purpose. Begin with this one: where, in your own week, is the quiet place where you look at yourself without pretending? Notice whether you visit it often or whether it has been a long while, and go gently with whatever you find, since there's nothing to be gained by judging yourself for the answer.

Then ask who you serve, and how. It doesn't have to be grand. It might be a neighbour or a colleague, and the service might be as small as listening well when it would be easier to hurry on. You might also ask what you're still holding on to that forgiveness could loosen, whether that's towards someone else or towards yourself.

Finally, ask what growth looks like in your life when nobody is watching. If your answer is that you don't know yet, that's a fine answer. It's often the first thing we say, and it's worth sitting with for a while before you rush towards another. You might also ask which of your own beliefs, religious or not, you've never looked at closely, and what it would be like to look at one this week.

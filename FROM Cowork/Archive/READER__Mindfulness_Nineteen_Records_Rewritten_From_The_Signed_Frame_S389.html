A report due on Friday, a form that has to be back by the end of the month and a lift you promised for Saturday each carry their own deadline, and the deadlines pile up in the same way the post does.

Limited space is the second part, and it comes in three kinds: physical, mental and emotional. You might picture physical space as a desk with no clear corner or a hall table with no spare surface, mental space as room in your head to think one thing through, and emotional space as room to notice how you feel about something. When space runs short, it becomes harder to think clearly, to stay organised and to find any relief.

Limited space also magnifies the stress that time pressure brings, so the same deadline weighs more in a week with no room in it. That's two places to look: how much time a task has, and how much room there is around it. You might look back at last week and ask which deadlines came with room around them and which came with none.

What is stress management in practice, when the week already feels full?

Prioritising starts from a plain fact about choosing: doing one thing means not doing another, whether or not you say so out loud. Every yes uses up some of your time and space, so a useful question is which yes matters most this week. With the pile on the hall table, the form that has a deadline can come before the leaflet, and the leaflet can go straight into the recycling.

Creating space for reflection is the second move, and you can use it to ask what's really on your list, whose each item is, and what would happen if it waited until Thursday. You can also look at the list from further back, as though it belonged to someone else, and ask which item you'd tell them to drop.

Boundaries are another way of making room, and the ones that help are the ones that hold. You might tell a colleague that you can look at their request on Thursday and not today, and then keep to Thursday, or you might decide when your phone stays out of reach. Putting your own needs on the list, and not at the bottom of it, is part of the same habit.

Do stress management techniques work? What one review found

A meta-analysis by Katherine Richardson and Hannah Rothstein, published in 2008 in the Journal of Occupational Health Psychology, gathered the results of 36 experimental studies of stress management in workplaces. Together the studies covered 55 programmes and 2,847 people, and the programmes lasted 7.4 weeks on average. An effect size is a standard way of expressing how big a difference was, and the authors report an overall figure of 0.526, which they describe as "a significant medium to large effect".

They sorted the programmes into kinds. Programmes built on cognitive-behavioural methods, which work with how people think and act, produced larger effects than the other kinds, though adding further components reduced the effect. Programmes aimed at changing how the workplace itself runs were still scarce in the studies, and most of the results were measured in psychological terms.

The authors name three limits of their own. There was little information for judging programmes aimed at whole organisations, the differences they compared were tangled up with the type of people in each study and with each other, and they couldn't account for how stressed each workplace was before its programme began.

There are limits beyond those the authors list. The review is from 2008, it looks only at workplaces, and it's one review, so it can't speak for later studies or for stress that starts at home. An average across 2,847 people also says little about how things would go for one person, you included, and no technique can promise you a result. It didn't test the stress principle either, so Achology's approach stands as a way of looking at your own life and hasn't been measured by it.

What does looking at yourself add to stress management?

Looking inward puts a question beside each item on the list: what reason sits behind my keeping this? You can ask whether you believe you have to answer every message the moment it arrives, whether you say yes because a no feels awkward, or whether you want to be seen as someone who copes. Each of those is a reason, and once a reason is in view you can decide whether to keep acting on it.

If an answer stings, you can put it down for a day and come back when you're ready to decide what to do about it. You might write the answer down so it's there when you return, and wonder how you'd respond if a friend showed it to you. What you find is a record of what you've been choosing so far, and your next choice is still to be made.

Taking responsibility, in Achology's sense, looks forward. Some of what fills your week may arrive from outside, such as an employer's deadline or a relative's request, and the stress principle doesn't ask you to blame yourself for that. The invitation is about your next move: which item to bring forward, which to ask someone about, and which to hand over. That's good news in a practical way, since your next move is already yours to make.

You can begin with a single item. Look down your list, ask which are really yours, and pick one to act on today. A week later you can look again and see which of your choices held.

For anything beyond self-help and learning, the right person is a doctor or other qualified professional, and that includes a difficult time that keeps going or begins to get in the way of your work, your home life or your rest.

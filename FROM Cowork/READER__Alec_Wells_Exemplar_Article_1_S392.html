What does it mean to take responsibility when nobody is making you? I have carried that question for years, the way you might carry a smooth stone in a coat pocket, turning it over whenever life grows quiet, and it always carries me back to the same place: an ambulance garage at half past six on a mid-winter morning. The world outside is pitch black, the heavy, ice-cold rain is drumming down in sheets, and I am gathering an armful of cannulas and flushes for a truck that has been hammered all night.

The truck is weary. It needs fuel, restocking and a clean inside and out, and the controlled drug register stubbornly refuses to add up. I have fifteen mandated minutes to set all of it right before a twelve-hour shift begins, and at my station a single run to hospital can swallow two hours. Deployment can come right up to the final minute, so fourteen hours on duty is far from rare.

The crew I'm relieving look at me with a mixture of dread and hope. Nobody has asked me to start early. I do it because it's part of my professionalism, and because I'm a lead paramedic and it's expected, which makes it a voluntary act that we used to call making a rod for your own back.

Responsibility Is What You Carry When Nobody Is Watching

Emergency work lives by the clock. Every moment is measured, from the response to dispatch and the time of mobilisation to the drive, the arrival at the scene and the arrival at the patient. The way I'd put it now is that duty is what the rota asks of you, and responsibility is what you choose to carry when nobody would notice if you laid it down. Those fifteen minutes were all I was given, so everything beyond them was mine to give.

Two things sat side by side that morning: my own quiet sense of professionalism, and what was expected of a lead paramedic. How would you tell them apart, in your own work or your own home? It's a gentle question, and it's worth asking before you decide what you are really carrying.

The Morning It Cost Me

The call came before I had finished restocking. It was a category one call, a collision between two heavy goods vehicles twenty-five minutes away through the dark and the rain, with an eight-minute monitored response time that blue lights could only halve. The description alone told me how serious it was likely to be. We worked that patient the way I work every job, expecting a successful handover to the hospital, and in the end I was the one who looked at my watch, noted the time and told everyone to stop.

By then I had spent twenty-five years in the emergency services, and I returned to work and attended dozens more cardiac arrests. However many patients I took to hospital, I kept coming back to that one. That morning never left me, and every time I drove through that dip in the road I could see exactly where the vehicles had been. Over the next six years I replayed every incident I attended, laying harsher and harsher verdicts over my own work like layers of sediment, verdicts that were never even accurate.

At home I grew withdrawn and distant. My temper arrived without warning, and so did the despair. Then I was assaulted three nights running while simply doing my job, and after the last of them I went off sick. Two weeks later I sat in front of my doctor, and everything crumbled.

What Does It Mean to Take Responsibility Without Blame?

One line from Kain Ramsay's NLP teaching has stayed with me: "Get a cold can of grow the hell up." It sounds blunt, even brutal, but I read it as a line about the next move rather than about blame. In the hole allegory that goes with it, I found myself at the bottom of the hole.

I found the way out of it too. I got that cold can, popped the tab and listened to the hiss, then I drained it, sluicing the muck and grime from the back of my throat. Nobody else could have drunk it on my behalf.

Nothing I had seen, and nothing done to me at work, was mine to answer for. What I did next, and how I chose to live with all of it, was mine, including the decision to study Modern Applied Psychology and, in time, to help other people do the same. For me, that is where responsibility lives: in who holds the next move, and not in who deserves the blame.

If you're carrying something heavy of your own, please make your first call to your doctor or a suitably qualified person, someone who can look at your situation properly and in full. What I've written here is my own story from one working life, and it isn't a substitute for that help.

What My Vision Asks of Me

The word vision is a wide and generous one. It is the power of seeing with the eyes, and the power of anticipating what may come. It is also an event appearing vividly to the mind although it isn't there, and, in its fourth and gentlest sense, a vivid imaginative conception. That fourth one is the one I have chosen to live by.

Recently, at an Achology workshop, I was challenged to find my collaborative value and asked, "What tone do you offer to others?" My answer was, "I am prepared to do whatever it takes to fulfil my vision; I will not give in, I will not give up, and I will go to the very bottom of the hole if that is where I have to find you." To my ear that's responsibility pointed outward, at the people I mentor.

My mentoring today rests on one conviction. People aren't defined by their past, their conditioning or the survival patterns they once needed. With reflection and disciplined honesty, they can begin to live with more clarity and freedom, like a window being cleaned, slowly and patiently, until the light comes through.

Questions to Sit With

You don't need a road collision to find your own garage. Ask yourself which responsibilities you already carry that nobody asked you to take on. Then ask which of them are really yours, and which you carry because you're afraid of what might happen if you set them down.

Then ask the harder question: what in my own life have I been waiting for somebody else to pick up? Notice that this question leaves blame out of it and asks only who holds the next move, and whether you're ready to hold it. Give it a few quiet minutes before you answer.

For me, the desired state isn't perfection. It's the ability to live consciously in the present, free from the weight of the past and without being governed by fear of the future, and that begins with deciding that the next move is mine. It's a modest aim, but it's the one I keep coming back to.
